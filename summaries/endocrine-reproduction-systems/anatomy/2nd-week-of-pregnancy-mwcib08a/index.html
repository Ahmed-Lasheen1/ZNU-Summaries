Bilaminar germ disc

While implantation of the blastocyst continues, the embryoblast (inner cell mass) differentiates into two layers that form the bilaminar germ disc.

Hypoblast

  • Cuboidal cells
  • Lies ventrally, adjacent to the blastocyst cavity

Epiblast

  • Columnar cells
  • Lies dorsally

Amniotic cavity

A small cavity appears within the epiblast cells and enlarges to become the amniotic cavity. Its roof is formed by amnioblasts (amnion-forming cells), and its floor is the epiblast proper.

The amnioblasts are derived from the epiblast cells adjacent to the cytotrophoblast.

Yolk sac

Development

  1. Primary yolk sac

    The blastocele cavity changes into the primary yolk sac. Its roof is the hypoblast and its floor is Heuser's membrane, which is derived from hypoblast cells.

  2. Secondary yolk sac

    The hypoblast produces an additional layer of cells lining the inner surface of Heuser's membrane. This converts the primary yolk sac into the secondary yolk sac, completely surrounded by hypoblast.

  3. Definitive yolk sac

    After folding of the embryo, part of the secondary yolk sac is enclosed in the embryo to form the gut. The remaining part bulges through the umbilical ring outside the embryo and forms the definitive yolk sac.

Functions

  • It is the first hematopoietic organ in the embryo.
  • It absorbs nutrients (active and passive transport).
  • It synthesizes proteins such as alpha-fetoprotein.
  • From the 2nd to the 4th week it is a site of migration of the primordial germ cells.
  • Its endoderm gives the mucous membrane of the gut and the respiratory tract.
  • Its surrounding extraembryonic mesoderm gives the vitelline arteries and veins.

Extraembryonic mesoderm and coelom

By the end of the 2nd week the extraembryonic (primary) mesoderm appears and separates the cytotrophoblast from the yolk sac and the amnion. A single cavity then develops in it, the extraembryonic coelom (EEC), which splits the mesoderm into two layers.

Outer layer (somatopleuric or parietal)
Lines the whole trophoblast and covers the amnion
Inner layer (visceral or splanchnopleuric)
Covers the yolk sac

Functions of the extraembryonic mesoderm: it supports the epithelium of the amnion and the yolk sac, and it is involved in the development of fetal blood.

Chorion and chorionic villi

Source: page 73

The trophoblast and the underlying somatic extraembryonic mesoderm form the chorionic plate. The extraembryonic coelom is also called the chorionic cavity, and the blastocyst is now called the chorionic vesicle (chorion).

The coelom incompletely surrounds the vesicle, because part of the mesoderm persists without cavitation as the connecting stalk at the caudal part of the embryonic disc. Fetal blood vessels pass through it between the embryo and the placenta.

Finger-like chorionic villi develop from the syncytiotrophoblast and surround the chorionic vesicle. They are classified in two ways.

Chorionic villi
  • By relation to the decidua
    • Chorion frondosum between embryo and decidua basalis
    • Chorion laeve between embryo and decidua capsularis
  • By structure
    • Primary villi (day 11-13) syncytiotrophoblast cover, cytotrophoblast core
    • Secondary villi (day 16) extraembryonic mesoderm invades the core
    • Tertiary villi (day 21) extraembryonic vessels appear in the core

Twins

Source: page 74
Definition

Twins: the presence of more than one fetus in a pregnant woman. Twins are the most common (1:80), then triplets (80 x 80) and quadruplets (80 x 80 x 80).

Dizygotic (fraternal)

  • Two ova fertilized by two sperms
  • Not genetically similar
  • Sex may or may not be similar
  • Each has its own placenta, amniotic cavity and chorionic sac
  • Incidence 7/1000 births

Monozygotic (identical)

  • One ovum fertilized by one sperm, then the embryo splits
  • Genetically similar
  • Sex must be similar
  • Incidence about 3/1000 births
High-yield

Splitting at the two-cell stage gives two separate zygotes and blastocysts that implant separately, so the membranes resemble those of dizygotic twins. Later splitting (inner cell mass) gives a common placenta and chorionic cavity but separate amniotic cavities.

Check yourself

Which cells roof the amniotic cavity, and where do they come from?

Amnioblasts, derived from the epiblast cells adjacent to the cytotrophoblast.

What results if the whole vitello-intestinal duct persists?

A vitelline fistula, with the umbilicus discharging intestinal contents.

Why does the extraembryonic coelom not completely surround the chorionic vesicle?

Part of the mesoderm persists without cavitation as the connecting stalk, at the caudal part of the embryonic disc.

Key Points / Quick Review

Germ disc and amniotic cavity

  • Embryoblast gives hypoblast (cuboidal, ventral) and epiblast (columnar, dorsal).
  • Amniotic cavity forms in the epiblast; amnioblasts roof it, epiblast proper is the floor.

Yolk sac

  • Primary (roof hypoblast, floor Heuser's membrane), then secondary, then definitive after folding.
  • First hematopoietic organ; absorbs nutrients; makes alpha-fetoprotein; germ cell migration in weeks 2 to 4.
  • Duct persistence: Meckel's diverticulum (proximal), sinus (distal), fistula (whole), cyst (middle), fibrous cord.

Extraembryonic mesoderm and chorion

  • The coelom splits the mesoderm into an outer somatopleuric and an inner visceral layer.
  • Chorionic plate = trophoblast + somatic mesoderm; connecting stalk carries fetal vessels.
  • Villi: primary (day 11-13), secondary (day 16), tertiary (day 21); frondosum vs laeve.

Twins

  • Dizygotic: two ova, two sperms, separate placentas, 7/1000.
  • Monozygotic: one ovum, one sperm, same sex, about 3/1000.
