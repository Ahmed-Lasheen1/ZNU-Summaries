Internal iliac artery

Source: pages 41 to 42

The pelvis is supplied mainly by the internal iliac artery.

Definition

The internal iliac artery is the smaller of the two terminal branches of the common iliac artery. It passes down into the pelvis to the upper margin of the greater sciatic foramen, where it divides into an anterior and a posterior division.

The anterior division supplies the pelvic organs, the perineum and the gluteal region. The posterior division supplies the sacral canal and the back of the pelvis.

The anterior division gives eight branches. Each one has a specific supply or course that is examinable.

Posterior division and the internal iliac vein

Posterior division

  • Iliolumbar artery: divides into lumbar and iliac branches.
  • Lateral sacral arteries: usually two (upper and lower) on each side; their branches enter the sacral canal through the anterior sacral foramina.
  • Superior gluteal artery: leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen.

Internal iliac vein

The internal iliac vein runs upward lateral to the internal iliac artery and joins the external iliac vein to form the common iliac vein. Its tributaries correspond to the branches of the internal iliac artery, with two exceptions: the umbilical vein drains into the portal vein, and the lumbar vein drains into the common iliac vein.

Internal pudendal artery

The internal pudendal artery is one of the two terminal branches of the anterior division. It leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below the piriformis and crosses the dorsal surface of the ischial spine, lateral to the pudendal nerve. It then enters the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen and runs in the pudendal canal in the lateral wall of the ischiorectal fossa.

Pudendal nerve

The pudendal nerve is the chief nerve of the perineum. It arises from the ventral rami of S2, S3 and S4 in the pelvis and follows this course:

Ventral rami S2 to S4
Greater sciatic foramen, below piriformis
Dorsum of ischial spine
Lesser sciatic foramen
Pudendal canal (Alcock's)

Its branches are:

  • Inferior rectal nerve: crosses the ischiorectal fossa to supply the external anal sphincter and the perianal skin.
  • Large perineal nerve: one of the two terminal branches.
  • Small dorsal nerve of the penis (or clitoris): the other terminal branch.

Pudendal nerve block

The pudendal nerve is infiltrated with a local anesthetic where it crosses the ischial spine. The spine is palpated through the vaginal wall, and a long needle is guided by a finger to it. The needle can also be inserted through the perineal skin.

Clinical relevance

When the block is performed bilaterally, the anal reflex is lost (a useful test that the block has worked), the pelvic floor muscles relax, and sensation is lost over the vulva and the lower third of the vagina.

Key Points / Quick Review

Arterial supply

  • The internal iliac artery is the smaller terminal branch of the common iliac artery and divides into anterior and posterior divisions.
  • The anterior division gives eight branches; the posterior division gives three.

Internal pudendal artery

  • Principal artery of the perineum; enters through the lesser sciatic foramen.
  • Five branches: inferior rectal, perineal, artery to bulb and urethra, deep artery of penis or clitoris, dorsal artery of penis or clitoris.

Venous drainage

  • The internal iliac vein joins the external iliac vein to form the common iliac vein.
  • The umbilical vein drains to the portal vein; the lumbar vein drains to the common iliac vein.

Pudendal nerve

  • Arises from S2 to S4 and leaves through the greater sciatic foramen, re-entering through the lesser sciatic foramen.
  • Branches: inferior rectal nerve, large perineal nerve, small dorsal nerve of the penis or clitoris.
  • Block: infiltrate where it crosses the ischial spine; success is confirmed by loss of the anal reflex.
