Internal carotid artery (origin + branches)

The cerebral part of the internal carotid artery ends at the anterior perforated substance by dividing into the middle and anterior cerebral arteries.

  • Enters the cranial cavity through the carotid canal.
  • Takes a sinuous course through the cavernous sinus and pierces its roof.

Branches

  1. Ophthalmic artery: structures of the orbit including the eyeball.
  2. Posterior communicating artery: runs backwards and anastomoses with the posterior cerebral.
  3. Anterior choroidal artery: forms the choroid plexus of the lateral ventricle.
  4. Anterior cerebral artery: runs forwards above the optic nerve, joins its fellow by the anterior communicating artery, then curves backwards above the corpus callosum on the medial surface.
  5. Middle cerebral artery: runs laterally in the stem of the lateral sulcus, then upwards to the superolateral surface.

Key words carotid canal, cavernous sinus, ophthalmic, posterior communicating, anterior choroidal, anterior and middle cerebral

Circle of Willis (site + formation)

The circle of Willis is a circle of anastomosis between branches of the basilar and internal carotid arteries, lying in the interpeduncular fossa.

  • Anteriorly: the anterior communicating and the anterior cerebral arteries.
  • Posteriorly: the basilar artery dividing into two posterior cerebral arteries.
  • Laterally, each side: the posterior communicating artery.

Function: gives alternative routes for collateral circulation if one major artery of the circle is blocked.

Key words interpeduncular fossa, anterior communicating, posterior communicating, posterior cerebral, collateral circulation

Mandibular nerve: origin and branches

The mandibular nerve (V3) is the largest, third division of the trigeminal nerve, formed of a large sensory and a small motor root.

  • Leaves the skull through the foramen ovale into the infratemporal fossa.
  • Short trunk deep to the lateral pterygoid, dividing into anterior and posterior divisions.

Branches

  • Trunk: nervus spinosus (dura, through foramen spinosum); nerve to medial pterygoid (gives a branch to tensor palati and tensor tympani); motor root to the otic ganglion via the nerve to medial pterygoid.
  • Anterior division (motor except buccal): masseter, temporalis (2 or 3 branches), lateral pterygoid, and the sensory buccal nerve to cheek mucosa.
  • Posterior division (sensory except nerve to mylohyoid):
    • Auriculotemporal: scalp in front of the auricle; carries parasympathetic fibers to the parotid.
    • Lingual: general sensation of anterior two thirds of tongue; parasympathetic to submandibular and sublingual glands; joined by chorda tympani (taste).
    • Inferior alveolar: gives the nerve to mylohyoid (mylohyoid and anterior belly of digastric), enters the mandibular foramen and ends as the mental nerve.

Key words foramen ovale, spinosus, medial pterygoid, masseter, buccal, auriculotemporal, lingual, inferior alveolar, mylohyoid

Maxillary nerve: origin and branches

The maxillary nerve (V2) is the second division of the trigeminal nerve and passes in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus.

It passes through four openings:

Foramen rotundum
Pterygomaxillary fissure
Inferior orbital fissure
Infraorbital foramen

Branches

  1. Meningeal: dura mater.
  2. Ganglionic: suspend the pterygopalatine ganglion.
  3. Zygomatic: enters the orbit; divides into zygomaticofacial and zygomaticotemporal.
  4. Posterior superior alveolar.
  5. Infraorbital: gives middle and anterior superior alveolar nerves, then emerges and divides into palpebral, nasal and labial branches.

Key words foramen rotundum, pterygopalatine fossa, infraorbital, zygomatic, alveolar, ganglionic

Pterygopalatine ganglion: site, roots, and branches

The pterygopalatine ganglion is the largest parasympathetic ganglion of the head and neck, lying in the pterygopalatine fossa suspended from the maxillary nerve.

Roots

  • Sensory: two ganglionic branches from the maxillary nerve.
  • Sympathetic: from the plexus around the internal carotid artery, via the deep petrosal nerve.
  • Parasympathetic: preganglionic fibers from the superior salivary nucleus pass with the facial nerve, then the greater petrosal nerve, which unites with the deep petrosal to form the nerve of the pterygoid canal (Vidian nerve).

Branches

  • Postganglionic parasympathetic fibers to the lacrimal gland (with the zygomatic, then zygomaticotemporal, then lacrimal nerve).
  • Orbital branches.
  • Greater and lesser palatine nerves: palate and nasal cavity.

Key words pterygopalatine fossa, greater petrosal, deep petrosal, Vidian nerve, lacrimal gland, palatine

Cavernous sinus: site, structures within it, and tributaries

The cavernous sinus lies one on each side of the body of the sphenoid (about 2 cm long, 1 cm broad).

Structures

  • Lateral wall, from above downwards: oculomotor, trochlear, ophthalmic and maxillary nerves.
  • Floor: internal carotid artery, with the abducent nerve inferolateral to it.

Tributaries

Orbit
Superior and inferior ophthalmic veins, central vein of the retina
Brain
Superficial middle and inferior cerebral veins
Dura
Sphenoparietal sinus, middle meningeal vein

Key words body of sphenoid, III, IV, V1, V2, internal carotid, abducent, ophthalmic veins

Middle meningeal artery: beginning, end, branches, and surface anatomy

The middle meningeal artery begins from the first part of the maxillary artery in the infratemporal fossa and divides into anterior and posterior branches about 2 cm above the zygomatic arch.

  • Ascends through the foramen spinosum and enters the cranial cavity just above the middle of the zygomatic arch.
  • Grooves the squamous part of the temporal bone.

Anterior (frontal)

  • Ascends on the inner surface of the pterion.
  • Parallel to the central sulcus; bleeding may compress the motor area.
  • Surface: opposite the center of the pterion (3.5 cm behind the frontozygomatic suture, 4 cm above the zygomatic arch).

Posterior (parietal)

  • Passes up and back towards the lambda, grooving parietal and temporal bones.
  • Over the superior temporal gyrus; bleeding may compress the auditory area.
  • Surface: crossing of a horizontal line along the supraorbital margin and a vertical line along the posterior border of the mastoid process.

Key words maxillary artery, foramen spinosum, pterion, central sulcus, motor area, auditory area

Cranial nerve X: types of fibers (not names of nuclei); exit from the brainstem; exit from the skull; and branches

The vagus nerve is a mixed nerve that leaves the medulla at the posterolateral sulcus (above the accessory nerve) and the skull through the jugular foramen.

Fiber types

  • Special visceral efferent: muscles of palate, pharynx and larynx.
  • General visceral efferent (parasympathetic): heart, bronchial tree, most of the GIT.
  • General somatic afferent: external auditory canal and outer tympanic membrane (and proprioception).
  • Special visceral afferent: taste from the posterior-most tongue and epiglottis.
  • General visceral afferent: pharynx, larynx, trachea, esophagus, thoracic and abdominal viscera.

Branches in the neck

  1. Meningeal: dura of the posterior cranial fossa.
  2. Auricular: floor of external acoustic meatus and adjacent tympanic membrane.
  3. Motor branch to the pharyngeal plexus.
  4. Cardiac branches.
  5. Carotid branch to carotid sinus and body.
  6. Superior laryngeal: external (cricothyroid) and internal (mucosa above the vocal cords).
  7. Recurrent laryngeal: sensation below the vocal cords, all laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid, and cardiac branches. Right: arises in front of the first part of the subclavian artery and curves behind it. Left: arises in the superior mediastinum and hooks on the ligamentum arteriosum behind the aortic arch.

Thorax: cardiac, esophageal and bronchial plexuses. Abdomen: gastric, hepatic and celiac plexuses.

Key words jugular foramen, posterolateral sulcus, superior laryngeal, recurrent laryngeal, pharyngeal, cardiac

Nasociliary ganglion: roots and branches

The nasociliary ganglion is a small parasympathetic ganglion in the orbital fat above and lateral to the optic nerve, suspended by the nasociliary nerve.

Roots

  • Sensory: two ganglionic branches from the nasociliary nerve; pass without relay; sensory to the whole eyeball.
  • Sympathetic: postganglionic fibers from the superior cervical ganglion via the plexus around the ophthalmic artery; no relay; supply dilator pupillae and eyeball vessels.
  • Parasympathetic: preganglionic fibers from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus run with the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve and the nerve to inferior oblique.

Branches

Postganglionic parasympathetic fibers to the sphincter pupillae and the ciliary muscle.

Key words nasociliary nerve, Edinger-Westphal, oculomotor inferior division, sphincter pupillae, ciliary muscle

Ophthalmic artery: begin and branches

The ophthalmic artery branches from the internal carotid just after it leaves the cavernous sinus and enters the orbit through the optic canal below the optic nerve.

It passes forwards on the medial wall of the orbit with the nasociliary nerve.

Branches

  1. Central artery of the retina (occlusion causes blindness).
  2. Lacrimal.
  3. Ciliary.
  4. Muscular.
  5. Posterior ethmoidal.
  6. Anterior ethmoidal.
  7. Dorsal nasal.
  8. Supraorbital.
  9. Medial palpebral.
  10. Supratrochlear.

Key words optic canal, central artery of retina, lacrimal, ciliary, ethmoidal, supraorbital, supratrochlear

Middle ear only (boundaries and contents)

The middle ear is a six-walled bony chamber in the petrous part of the temporal bone.

Boundaries

Lateral
Tympanic membrane; epitympanic recess above it (head of malleus, body of stapes)
Roof
Tegmen tympani (thin bone of petrous temporal)
Floor
Thin bone separating it from the internal jugular vein
Anterior
Thin bone over the internal carotid artery; canal for tensor tympani above, auditory tube below
Posterior
Aditus to mastoid antrum, vertical part of facial canal, pyramid (origin of stapedius)
Medial
Promontory (basal turn of cochlea), oval window (stapes footplate), round window (secondary tympanic membrane)

Contents

  • Three ossicles: malleus, incus, stapes.
  • Two muscles: tensor tympani and stapedius.
  • Two nerves: chorda tympani; tympanic branch of glossopharyngeal with the tympanic plexus.
  • Vessels of the tympanic cavity.

Key words tegmen tympani, promontory, oval window, round window, ossicles, chorda tympani, tympanic plexus

Cranial nerve VII: types of fibers (not names of nuclei); exit from the brainstem; exit from the skull; intracranial and extracranial branches; facial nerve in the face; and facial palsy (Bell's palsy)

The facial nerve is a mixed nerve that emerges from the groove between the pons and the olive of the medulla and leaves the skull through the stylomastoid foramen.

Fiber types

  • Special visceral efferent: facial muscles, posterior belly of digastric, stylohyoid.
  • General visceral efferent (parasympathetic): lacrimal, nasal and palatine glands, submandibular and sublingual glands.
  • Special visceral afferent: taste from the anterior two thirds of the tongue.
  • General somatic afferent: skin of the auricle (and proprioception).

Course and branches

  • Intracranial: enters the internal acoustic meatus and facial canal; forms the geniculate ganglion at its sharp bend. Branches in the canal: greater superficial petrosal (parasympathetic to lacrimal gland and nose, palate and nasopharynx glands; palatal taste), nerve to stapedius, chorda tympani (taste of anterior two thirds of tongue; parasympathetic to submandibular and sublingual glands).
  • Extracranial, before the parotid: posterior auricular nerve (scalp behind auricle); nerve to posterior belly of digastric and stylohyoid.
  • Terminal, within the parotid: temporal, zygomatic, buccal, mandibular, cervical.

In the face

Supplies all facial muscles. It exits the stylomastoid foramen, passes lateral to the styloid process, enters the parotid through its posteromedial surface, and its five branches leave through the upper end, lower end and anterior border.

Bell's palsy

  • Orbicularis oculi: cannot close the eye or blink.
  • Frontal belly: loss of forehead corrugations.
  • Buccinator: food collects in the vestibule; angle of mouth deviates to the healthy side; cannot blow the cheek.
  • Orbicularis oris: cannot whistle.

Key words stylomastoid foramen, geniculate ganglion, greater petrosal, chorda tympani, parotid, five terminal branches, orbicularis oculi

Neck triangles (boundaries and contents)

The sternomastoid muscle divides the anterolateral neck into the anterior triangle and the posterior triangle.

Posterior triangle

  • Boundaries: posterior border of sternomastoid, anterior border of trapezius, base on the middle third of clavicle, apex at the mastoid process.
  • Inferior belly of omohyoid divides it into occipital and supraclavicular triangles.
  • Nerves: cutaneous branches of cervical plexus (great auricular, lesser occipital, transverse cervical, supraclavicular), spinal accessory, brachial plexus trunks.
  • Veins: subclavian vein, external jugular vein and tributaries.
  • Arteries: third part of subclavian, suprascapular and transverse cervical, occipital artery at the apex.

Anterior triangle

Boundaries: median plane of neck, anterior border of sternomastoid, base of mandible. Digastric bellies and superior belly of omohyoid divide it into four triangles.

Key words sternomastoid, omohyoid, occipital, supraclavicular, submental, muscular, digastric, carotid
