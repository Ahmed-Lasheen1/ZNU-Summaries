Hemorrhage

Source: page 133

Hemorrhage may be due to trauma, operation, disease of the blood vessels, or general diseases such as hemophilia, purpura, leukemia and scurvy.

By the vessel involved

TypeColourPatternNotes
CapillarySlightly darkContinuous ooze from the injured surfaceControlled by firm pressure or a pack
VenousDarkContinuous bleeding; more from the distal endControlled by elevating the part above heart level; a large neck vein may suck in air (air embolism)
ArterialBright redJets synchronous with the heartbeats; more from the proximal end—

By the site

  • External: blood reaches the surface from a wound or natural orifices (epistaxis, hematemesis, melena, hematuria).
  • Internal: subcutaneous, or collections in serous cavities (pleura, peritoneum, pericardium).

Hypovolemic shock

Source: page 134

Causes

  • Blood loss: hemorrhage from trauma, operation, GIT bleeding or blood diseases.
  • Plasma loss: as in burns.
  • Water and electrolyte loss: vomiting, diarrhea, high-output intestinal fistula.

Clinical picture by severity

GradeBlood volume lostOrgans affectedFeatures
MildUp to 20%Non-vital: skin, muscles, bonePallor, cold skin; mild tachycardia; may have postural hypotension
ModerateUp to 40%Plus kidneys, liver, intestineIncreased tachycardia and hypotension; oliguria or anuria
SevereMore than 40%Plus brain and heartRestlessness, agitation (sense of impending death), coma, may die; arrhythmia, may cardiac arrest; thirst; rapid shallow respiration
High-yield

Urine output below 0.5 cc/kg/hour indicates marked hypovolemia.

Treatment of bleeding and hypovolemic shock

Source: pages 135 to 136
  1. Airway first

    Ensure a patent airway and adequate oxygenation and ventilation.

  2. Arrest the bleeding

    Use a bandage, a pack or a tourniquet.

  3. Fluid therapy

    The mainstay of initial treatment. Insert two large-pore cannulas and draw blood for typing and cross-matching.

  4. Position

    Elevate the lower limbs while keeping the trunk supine.

  5. Keep warm

    Cover the patient with blankets to avoid a sense of coolness.

  6. Medications

    Given as listed below.

Fluid choices

  • Lactated Ringer’s: 2000 ml over 45 minutes.
  • Blood: the most effective, especially with blood loss.
  • Colloids: used in the absence of whole blood (human plasma, albumin solution, dextran, haemagel).

Medications

Morphine
Relieves pain and anxiety and reduces tissue oxygen requirement; give IV to avoid toxicity; contraindicated in abdominal and head injuries and with respiratory depression
Antibiotics
Third-generation cephalosporins, to avoid septic complications
Dopamine
Inotropic drug used when the condition fails to improve despite adequate volume replacement and oxygenation; improves myocardial contractility, renal blood flow and urine output
Vasodilators
Alpha-adrenergic blockers; improve tissue perfusion after full correction of hypovolemia
Sodium bicarbonate
Corrects acidosis

Neurogenic shock

Source: pages 136 to 137
Definition

Shock due to peripheral vasodilatation and pooling of blood in skeletal muscles, with inadequate venous return. Also called fainting, collapse or vasovagal attack.

Causes

  • Brain and spine injuries.
  • Painful stimulation, such as catheterization or severe trauma to the testis or abdomen.
  • Reaction to fear, fight or bad news.
  • May follow spinal anesthesia.

Clinical picture

  • Increasing facial pallor and cold extremities.
  • Fall of blood pressure together with bradycardia.
  • Nausea, vomiting and deep sighing respiration.
  • Fainting: falling to the ground assists venous return and helps recovery.

Treatment

Shock position
Lie flat with lower limbs elevated, to increase venous return and cardiac output
Atropine
In vasovagal shock, to improve bradycardia caused by increased vagal tone
Ephedrine
Vasoconstrictor in spinal shock; raises blood pressure by increasing peripheral resistance
IV fluids
If the shock persists for more than 20 minutes

Anaphylactic shock

Source: pages 137 to 138

An antigen-antibody (allergic) reaction releases a large amount of histamine, causing capillary paralysis, dilatation and pooling. The best example is a penicillin injection in a sensitized patient.

Clinical picture: skin eruption, bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, respiratory distress and collapse.

High-yield

Epinephrine (adrenaline) is the first and most critical step.

  1. Epinephrine

    Inject immediately, using an auto-injector (e.g. EpiPen) into the outer thigh.

  2. Position

    Lay the person flat and elevate the legs to improve blood flow.

  3. Additional medications

    Antihistamines (e.g. diphenhydramine) for itching and hives; corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and prevent a delayed reaction.

  4. Oxygen

    Provide supplemental oxygen if breathing is difficult and oxygen is available.

  5. Monitor

    Watch breathing, heart rate and level of consciousness; be ready to perform CPR.

Check yourself

Source: pages 133 to 138
How do arterial and venous bleeding differ in colour and pattern?

Arterial blood is bright red and comes in jets synchronous with the heartbeat; venous blood is dark and flows continuously.

Which organs are additionally affected when blood loss exceeds 40%?

The brain and heart.

Which drug treats the bradycardia of vasovagal shock?

Atropine.

Key Points / Quick Review

Hemorrhage

  • Capillary: dark ooze, controlled by pressure or pack.
  • Venous: dark, continuous, more from distal end; elevate above heart.
  • Arterial: bright red jets, more from proximal end.
  • Large neck vein injury: air embolism.

Hypovolemic shock

  • Causes: blood, plasma, water and electrolyte loss.
  • Mild up to 20%, moderate up to 40%, severe over 40%.
  • Urine output under 0.5 cc/kg/hour means marked hypovolemia.

Treatment

  • Airway, then stop bleeding, then fluids.
  • 2000 ml lactated Ringer’s over 45 minutes; blood is most effective.
  • Morphine: IV only; contraindicated in abdominal and head injuries.
  • Dopamine when no response to volume and oxygen.

Neurogenic shock

  • Vasodilatation and pooling; low BP with bradycardia.
  • Shock position, atropine, ephedrine, IV fluids after 20 minutes.

Anaphylactic shock

  • Antigen-antibody reaction with histamine release.
  • Epinephrine first, into the outer thigh.
