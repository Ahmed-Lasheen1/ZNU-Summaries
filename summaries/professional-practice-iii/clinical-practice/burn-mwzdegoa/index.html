Definition and causes

Source: page 127
Definition

A burn is coagulative necrosis of tissue due to exposure to heat.

Causes

Thermal burn

  • Caused by heat, which produces coagulative necrosis of the tissues
  • The time of exposure to heat determines the depth of the burn

Electrical burn

  • Caused by the heat of the electric current
  • Thrombosis of blood vessels may occur and lead to ischemia
  • Usually deeper than an ordinary burn

Chemical burn

  • Caused by strong acids and alkalis
  • They extract water from the cells and precipitate their proteins
  • Some chemicals, such as magnesium, also produce heat on contact with the tissues

Degrees of burn

Source: pages 127 to 128

The degree of a burn depends on how deep the destruction of the tissues goes.

  1. First-degreeSuperficial layers injured, not destroyed; erythema only
  2. Second-degreeSome outer layers destroyed; blebs and blisters
  3. Third-degreeWhole skin and appendages destroyed
  4. Fourth-degreeSubcutaneous tissue, muscles and even bone involved
High-yield

In a third-degree burn, epithelialization is not possible without skin grafting.

Extent of burn

Source: pages 128 to 129

The extent of a burn wound is the percentage of total body surface damaged.

Minor versus major

  • Minor burn: less than 10% in children, less than 15% in adults
  • Major burn: more than these values

The rule of nine

The rule of nine is a practical method of calculating the extent of a burn in an adult.

Worked example

An adult with both legs (36%), the groin (1%) and the front chest and abdomen (18%) burned has 55% of the body involved.

First aid and minor burns

Source: page 129

First aid

  • Cover the wound to prevent contamination and to minimize pain.
  • A clean sheet or cloth is suitable; if soaked in iced water it has a soothing effect and minimizes the action of heat on the tissues.
  • Give mouth-to-mouth artificial respiration if the patient has respiratory arrest due to inhalation of steam.

Treatment of minor burns

  • Fluids are not required, and the burn is cleaned in the usual way.
  • A well-applied occlusive dressing provides rest to the area.
  • An antibiotic cream is usually needed, and the wound is dressed after 5 days.
  • General antibiotics may be needed.
  • The burn usually heals in about 2 weeks.

Major burns: general management

Source: page 129

In shortIn the first 48 hours a needle is placed in a vein for blood tests, fluid infusion and intravenous morphine.

A needle is introduced into a vein for

  • Obtaining a blood sample for cross-matching, hematocrit value, blood electrolytes and blood nitrogen.
  • Starting the infusion with dextran or lactated Ringer's solution.
  • Giving morphine intravenously to allay the patient's anxiety and ameliorate pain.
High-yield

If morphine is given subcutaneously or intramuscularly, it will be ineffective.

Fluid therapy in major burns

Source: page 130

Fluids by mouth are withheld in the first 48 hours for fear of gastric distension or paralytic ileus.

Formula for the first 24 hours

ComponentExampleAmount
CrystalloidLactated Ringer's solution1.5 cc × burn surface area (%) × body weight (kg)
ColloidPlasma or dextran0.5 cc × burn surface area (%) × body weight (kg)
Daily water requirementGlucoseAbout 2000 cc for an adult, supplied as glucose/12 hours

The quantity is given as half in the first 8 hours and the other half in the next 16 hours.

Second 24 hours

Half of the calculated colloid and crystalloid of the first 24 hours, plus the daily requirement.

Give fluids with caution in

  • Young children and elderly patients
  • Patients with renal or cardiovascular diseases
  • Patients with respiratory tract irritation, for fear of pulmonary edema
  • Deep burns of the head and neck, for fear of brain edema
High-yield

Because of the tendency to metabolic acidosis, lactated Ringer's solution is preferred to normal saline in the early period.

Management of the burn wound

Source: pages 130 to 132

The aim of local treatment is to control wound infection.

  • Clean the wound thoroughly under absolute aseptic conditions; everyone attending wears sterile gowns and masks.
  • Remove all dead tissue from deep burns and prepare the wound for grafting as early as possible.
  • Wound coverage is the surest method of preventing infection.

Definitive local care

Occlusive dressing method

  • Covers the open wound to protect it from infection
  • Dressing is changed every 4 to 5 days

Exposure method

  • Leaves the burn exposed to air so it dries and a crust forms
  • The eschar acts as a protective covering, so it is in a scientific sense a closed method
  • The burn may be dusted with antibiotic powder until it dries

Exposure method: when it ends and when it is used

  • Superficial burns: exposure ends when the crusts disappear and fall.
  • Deep burns: exposure ends when the crust softens over the underlying granulation tissue; it is then removed and skin grafts are applied.
  • Indicated for: burns of the face, burns of one side of the trunk and burns of the perineum.

Topical antibacterial agents

  • Silver sulfadiazine: effective against gram-negative bacteria.
  • Povidone-iodine ointment: acts against gram-negative bacteria and fungi.
Clinical relevance

Iodine toxicity may occur with povidone-iodine in large burns treated for long periods.

Key Points / Quick Review

Causes and degrees

  • Burn = coagulative necrosis of tissue due to heat.
  • Causes: thermal, electrical (deeper, thrombosis and ischemia), chemical (water extracted, proteins precipitated).
  • First-degree: erythema; second: blebs and blisters; third: whole skin destroyed, needs grafting; fourth: reaches muscles and bone.

Extent

  • Minor: under 10% in children, under 15% in adults.
  • Rule of nine: head 9, chest 9, abdomen 9, back and buttocks 18, each arm 9, groin 1, each leg 18.

First aid and minor burns

  • Cover with a clean sheet or cloth, possibly soaked in iced water.
  • Mouth-to-mouth breathing for respiratory arrest from steam.
  • Minor burns: occlusive dressing, antibiotic cream, dressed after 5 days, heal in about 2 weeks.

Major burns

  • IV access in the first 48 hours; morphine must be IV.
  • No oral fluids for 48 hours (gastric distension, paralytic ileus).
  • Lactated Ringer's preferred to saline (metabolic acidosis).
  • Caution with fluids in children, the elderly, renal or cardiovascular disease, respiratory irritation and deep head and neck burns.

Wound management

  • Aim: control infection; wound coverage is the surest method.
  • Occlusive dressing changed every 4 to 5 days; exposure method for face, one side of the trunk and perineum.
  • Silver sulfadiazine: gram-negative; povidone-iodine: gram-negative and fungi, with possible iodine toxicity.
