The testis: structure

The testis is a paired ovoid gland lying within the scrotum. It has two functions: production of sperms (exocrine) and synthesis of the male sex hormone testosterone (endocrine). It is formed of stroma and parenchyma.

Stroma: the three coats (outer to inner)

  1. Tunica vaginalisOuter capsule from the double-layered peritoneal sac: parietal layer, visceral layer and a fluid-containing space
  2. Tunica albugineaMiddle coat of dense collagenous tissue, the true capsule
  3. Tunica vasculosaInner, highly vascular loose connective tissue
  • The tunica albuginea is thickened posteriorly to form the mediastinum testis.
  • The tunica vasculosa lines the tunica albuginea and the septa.
  • Delicate incomplete septa radiate from the mediastinum to the tunica albuginea and divide the testis into about 250-300 lobules; each lobule contains 1-4 convoluted seminiferous tubules.
  • Reticular fibers form the background network of the testis.

Seminiferous tubules and spermatogenic cells

The seminiferous tubules are the exocrine units of the testis, where spermatozoa are formed. Each is very long (30-80 cm) and highly convoluted, with a diameter of about 200 µm (150-300 µm).

  • Lined by two cell populations: germ (spermatogenic) cells and Sertoli (supporting) cells.
  • The cells rest on a prominent basement membrane, surrounded by a thin layer of contractile myoid cells, which produce waves of contraction to move immature (not yet motile) sperms out of the testis.
  • Spermatogenic cells are arranged in 4-8 layers from the basal lamina to the lumen.

Spermatogenesis and spermiogenesis

Spermatogenesis is the process by which spermatogonia develop into mature sperms. It starts at puberty and takes about 64-74 days. It includes three processes: spermatocytogenesis (mitosis), meiosis and spermiogenesis.

Spermiogenesis is the morphological transformation of spermatids into spermatozoa, occurring without any further cell division. It has four phases.

  1. Golgi phase

    The Golgi apparatus forms numerous proacrosomal secretory granules (hydrolytic enzymes). They fuse into a single acrosomal vesicle adherent to one pole of the nuclear membrane, while the pair of centrioles migrates to the opposite caudal pole.

  2. Cap phase

    The acrosomal vesicle spreads to cover the anterior half of the nucleus, forming the acrosomal cap.

  3. Acrosomal phase

    The nucleus elongates and condenses. The distal centriole grows to form the axoneme (nine doublets of microtubules with two central singlets). The mitochondrial sheath forms around the middle piece, and a fibrous sheath with nine columns of longitudinal dense fibers forms around the axoneme.

  4. Maturation phase

    A thin cytoplasmic cover forms and the developing sperm is covered with cell membrane. Sertoli cells phagocytose the residual cytoplasm, and the mature sperm is released into the tubule lumen.

Factors affecting spermatogenesis

Spermatogenic cells are very sensitive to: temperature (the suitable temperature is 35°C, and elevated testicular temperature impairs it), dietary deficiencies, general or local infections, steroid hormones and related medication, radiation, and toxic agents such as mutagens and drugs.

Sertoli cells and the blood-testis barrier

Sertoli cells are non-dividing epithelial cells of mesodermal origin with a supportive function.

  • LM: tall columnar cells resting on the basal lamina and extending to the lumen, spanning the whole thickness of the germinal epithelium. They are highly irregular with extensive lateral processes. The nucleus is large, basal, oval, pale and euchromatic with prominent nucleoli. The cytoplasm is pale acidophilic with a special crystalloid structure.
  • EM: numerous mitochondria, prominent Golgi and RER, lipid droplets, well-developed SER, microtubules and microfilaments, lysosomes. The lateral membrane has pockets enclosing proliferating spermatogonia, and tight junctions between lateral membranes form the blood-testis barrier.

Functions

  • Support for all spermatogenic cells.
  • Nutrition: secrete a fructose-rich fluid for the spermatogenic cells.
  • Phagocytosis of residual bodies from spermiogenesis.
  • Endocrine: androgen binding protein (ABP), inhibin, plasminogen activator, and anti-Müllerian hormone (during embryonic development).
  • Spermiation: active release of mature spermatozoa from Sertoli cells into the lumen, using their microtubules and microfilaments.
  • Formation of the blood-testis barrier.

Blood-testis barrier

It is formed by tight (occluding) junctions between the lateral processes of adjacent Sertoli cells near their bases, dividing the tubule into two compartments.

Basal (outer) compartment

  • Proliferating spermatogonia
  • Blood capillaries

Adluminal (inner) compartment

  • Away from the blood stream
  • Primary spermatocytes, secondary spermatocytes, spermatids and sperms
  • Creates the suitable environment for gamete production.
  • Prevents autoimmune reaction against the genetically different haploid cells.
  • Prevents exposure of spermatogenic cells to toxic substances in blood.
  • Selects and allows passage of hormones and nutrients needed for sperm growth and development.
Clinical relevance

A defect in barrier formation exposes spermatogenic cells and sperms, producing sperm-specific antibodies. These agglutinate the sperm and prevent their movement and interaction with the ovum.

Interstitial tissue and Leydig cells

Seminiferous tubules are separated by loose areolar connective tissue (interstitial tissue), forming a multilayered covering around each tubule. It contains clusters of endocrine Leydig cells (interstitial cells), blood vessels, lymphatics, nerves and myoid cells.

Leydig cells are mesenchymal in origin, endocrine in function (secrete androgens), and differentiate in early fetal life.

  • LM: large polyhedral cells in clusters; central rounded nucleus (may be binucleated) with one or two prominent nucleoli; pale acidophilic cytoplasm.
  • EM (steroid-secreting cells): lipid droplets, well-developed SER, numerous mitochondria with tubular cristae, well-developed Golgi, lysosomes, peroxisomes, lipofuscin granules, and small cytoplasmic inclusions called Reinke's crystalloids.

Clinical relevance

Orchitis is acute or chronic inflammation of the testis, frequently involving the ducts connecting it to the epididymis. Common forms are caused by infective agents, secondary to urinary tract infection or a sexually transmitted pathogen.

Epididymis

The epididymis is a crescent-shaped, highly convoluted tube about 6 m long, lying along the superior and posterior surfaces of the testis and connecting the efferent ductules to the vas deferens. It has a head, body and tail, and is formed of mucosa, musculosa and adventitia.

The mucosa is lined by pseudostratified columnar epithelium with stereocilia, on a clear basement membrane, with a thin delicate vascular lamina propria.

Principal cells

  • Tall columnar, acidophilic cytoplasm, basal oval nuclei, apical brush border
  • EM: stereocilia, supranuclear Golgi, many RER cisternae, coated vesicles, multivesicular bodies, lysosomes
  • Reabsorb excess luminal fluid, phagocytose remnants of spermatogenic cells, synthesize a glycoprotein that inhibits capacitation until sperms reach the female genital tract

Basal cells

  • Short pyramidal cells with rounded heterochromatic dense nuclei
  • Scanty clear acidophilic cytoplasm with few organelles
  • Stem cells of the principal cells
  • Musculosa: thin circular smooth muscle in the head and most of the body; inner and outer longitudinal layers plus a middle circular layer in the rest of the body and the tail. Contraction conducts sperms toward the vas deferens.
  • Adventitia: external connective tissue layer.

Functions of the epididymis

  • Absorption of excess fluid secreted in the seminiferous tubules (principal cells).
  • Secretion of substances aiding sperm maturation (glycerophosphocholine, sialic acid, glycoproteins).
  • Phagocytosis of residual bodies and degenerated sperms not phagocytosed by Sertoli cells.
  • Capacitation and decapacitation: sperms acquire motility and ability for ovum penetration during their passage, and acquire a decapacitation factor from the epididymal fluid that acts temporarily until they reach the female genital tract and is inactivated just before fertilization.
  • Contraction to move and push sperms during ejaculation.

Vas deferens, spermatic cord and ejaculatory duct

Ductus (vas) deferens

The longest part of the extratesticular ducts: a fibromuscular tube connecting the tail of the epididymis with the prostatic urethra. It ascends along the posterior border of the testis, enters the abdomen in the spermatic cord through the inguinal canal, then descends behind the urinary bladder where it dilates to form the ampulla. The ampulla joins the seminal vesicle duct to form the ejaculatory duct.

  • Mucosa: pseudostratified columnar epithelium with stereocilia; lamina propria with abundant elastic fibers.
  • Musculosa: thick in proportion to the lumen diameter; indistinctly three layers: inner and outer longitudinal, middle circular.
  • Adventitia: external connective tissue layer.

Spermatic cord: five structures surrounded by connective tissue coverings

  • Vas deferens
  • Testicular artery and vein
  • Pampiniform plexus of veins
  • Cremasteric muscle
  • Lymphatic vessels and nerves

Ejaculatory duct

Formed by the ampulla of the vas joining the duct of the seminal vesicle. It penetrates the prostate and opens in the prostatic urethra. Its wall is simple columnar epithelium with underlying lamina propria and it has no muscular layer.

Accessory glands

All accessory glands help in nutrition, protection and easy transport of sperms.

Seminal vesicle

  • A pair of long simple tubular glands (10-15 cm), highly coiled, located postero-inferior to the urinary bladder. Each opens through a narrow duct into the ampulla of the vas deferens.
  • Mucosa: pseudostratified columnar epithelium (tall secretory cells, basal stem cells); lamina propria rich in elastic fibers; mucosa thrown into primary, secondary and tertiary folds to increase the secretory surface.
  • Musculosa: thin, inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle. Adventitia outside.
  • Secretion (under testosterone): whitish yellow, viscous, clear; about 60% of the seminal fluid; contains fructose (energy for sperms), amino acids, ascorbic acid and prostaglandins.

Prostate gland

The largest accessory sex gland: a single compound tubuloalveolar exocrine gland lying immediately below the urinary bladder and surrounding the upper part of the urethra.

  • Stroma: fibromuscular: capsule, septa and reticular network.
  • Secretory part: 30-50 compound tubuloalveolar glands (acini) arranged in three concentric zones, lined by simple squamous, cuboidal, columnar or pseudostratified columnar epithelium.
  • Conducting part: branched ducts; the main ducts open into the prostatic urethra.

Clinical relevance

Bulbourethral (Cowper) glands and glands of Littre

  • Bulbourethral glands: paired compound tubuloalveolar glands in the urogenital diaphragm at the root of the penis, opening in the membranous urethra. Stroma as in the prostate; mucous acini lined by cuboidal or columnar epithelium. They secrete mucus that lubricates the penile urethra.
  • Glands of Littre: multiple paraurethral mucinous compound tubuloalveolar exocrine glands along the full length of the corpus spongiosum.

External genitalia

Scrotum

A thin external pouch below the abdomino-pelvic cavity containing the testes. Its skin is wrinkled and thin; a thin layer of dartos muscle is responsible for wrinkling of the skin; the connective tissue is loose with no fat.

Penis

The male copulatory organ: a root, a shaft and an expanded distal portion, the glans.

  • Erectile tissue: two dorsal corpora cavernosa and one ventral corpus spongiosum with the penile urethra in its center; the spongiosum ends distally as the glans penis. The erectile tissue consists of irregular vascular spaces lined with endothelium and separated by connective tissue with smooth muscle cells.
  • Each corpus is surrounded by a thick tunica albuginea. That around the two corpora cavernosa is thick but incomplete in the middle, allowing communication between their erectile tissues.
  • Skin: thin, with few sweat glands, hairless and devoid of fat; loosely attached to the underlying loose connective tissue except distally, where it forms the prepuce (foreskin) enclosing the glans. Circumcision is surgical removal of the foreskin.

Erection

  • Parasympathetic stimulation
  • Vasodilatation of penile arteries; vascular spaces fill
  • Length and rigidity of the penis increase

Ejaculation

  • Sympathetic stimulation
  • Vasoconstriction of penile arteries; vascular spaces drain of blood
  • Length and rigidity of the penis decrease

Check yourself

Which cells form the blood-testis barrier, and what do they separate?

Adjacent Sertoli cells, through tight junctions near their bases. They separate the basal compartment (spermatogonia, capillaries) from the adluminal compartment (spermatocytes, spermatids, sperms).

What are the four phases of spermiogenesis, in order?

Golgi phase, cap phase, acrosomal phase and maturation phase.

Which prostatic glands form 60% of the glandular tissue, and how are they lined?

The main (peripheral) glands, lined by pseudostratified columnar epithelium.

Which cells of the epididymis are the stem cells of the principal cells?

The basal cells.

Key Points / Quick Review

Testis and tubules

  • Coats: tunica vaginalis (outer), albuginea (middle, true capsule), vasculosa (inner)
  • 250-300 lobules, each with 1-4 seminiferous tubules
  • Myoid cells surround the tubule and move immature sperms out
  • Cell lineage: spermatogonia, primary spermatocyte (largest), secondary spermatocyte (1n, 2c), spermatid (1n, 1c), sperm
  • Spermatogenesis takes about 64-74 days

Spermiogenesis and sperm

  • Phases: Golgi, cap, acrosomal, maturation
  • Sperm: head (nucleus and acrosome), neck (centrioles), tail (middle, principal, end pieces)
  • Principal piece is the longest and has seven dense fibers

Sertoli and Leydig cells

  • Sertoli: support, fructose-rich fluid, phagocytosis, ABP, inhibin, spermiation, blood-testis barrier
  • Leydig: steroid-secreting cells with Reinke's crystalloids; secrete testosterone

Ducts

  • Tubuli recti, rete testis, efferent ductules, epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct
  • Epididymis: principal cells (absorb, phagocytose, secrete) and basal cells (stem cells)
  • Spermatic cord has five structures

Accessory glands and external genitalia

  • Seminal vesicle: about 60% of semen, fructose
  • Prostate: 10-30% of semen, three zones (25%, 10%, 60%), corpora amylacea
  • Cowper glands and glands of Littre: mucus for the urethra
  • Penis: two corpora cavernosa and one corpus spongiosum; erection is parasympathetic
