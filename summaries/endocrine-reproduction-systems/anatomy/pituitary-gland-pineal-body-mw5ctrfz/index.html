Hypothalamus and pineal gland

The hypothalamus is part of the diencephalon, lying below the thalamus (about 4 gm). It is the link between the nervous system and the endocrine system via the pituitary gland.

Pineal gland (epiphysis cerebri)

The pineal gland is part of the epithalamus, which belongs to the diencephalon. It is a neuroendocrine gland that secretes melatonin, which modulates the sleep-wake cycle by controlling the circadian rhythm.

  • Position: projects from the posterior wall of the 3rd ventricle, below the splenium of the corpus callosum.
  • Attachment: to the rest of the brain by the pineal stalk.
  • Shape and size: conical, about 0.1 gm.
  • Development: at the end of the 4th week of prenatal life, as an outward projection from the posterior wall of the 3rd ventricle.
Arterial supply
Posterior choroidal arteries (from the posterior cerebral artery)
Venous drainage
Internal cerebral veins

Pituitary gland: position, shape and relations

The pituitary gland (hypophysis cerebri) is a major endocrine gland that secretes hormones controlling the actions of other endocrine organs. It lies in the hypophyseal fossa of the sphenoid bone.

  • Superiorly it is covered by the diaphragma sellae, a reflection of dura mater pierced centrally for the infundibulum (pituitary stalk), which connects it with the hypothalamus.
  • It is a pea-sized oval structure, about 12 mm transverse and 8 mm anteroposterior, with an average adult weight of 500 mg.

Subdivisions and development

The pituitary is a "two-in-one" structure whose two parts have different embryonic origins and function very differently.

Anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis)

  • Derived from Rathke's pouch, an upward growth of the roof of the primitive mouth
  • Pars distalis, pars intermedia, pars tuberalis

Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis)

  • Develops as a downgrowth from the floor of the diencephalon
  • Connected with the hypothalamus by the infundibulum

Blood supply

Hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal system

This system connects the hypothalamus and the anterior pituitary through two capillary plexuses: a primary plexus in the hypothalamus and a secondary plexus in the anterior pituitary. It lets releasing factors released into the hypothalamic capillaries reach the anterior pituitary and stimulate it to release the appropriate hormones.

Venous circulation

Arterial supply and venous drainage

  • Arteries: a superior and an inferior hypophyseal artery on each side, both branches of the internal carotid artery.
  • Veins: short veins drain into neighboring dural venous sinuses (cavernous and intercavernous). Hormones leave the gland through the venous blood to reach their targets.

Pituitary adenoma: pressure effects

Clinical relevance
  • Downward (intrasellar) growth: enlarges the hypophyseal fossa, seen as ballooning of the sella turcica on a lateral skull radiograph.
  • Upward growth: presses the central part of the optic chiasma, causing bitemporal hemianopia (loss of vision in both temporal fields).

Key Points / Quick Review

Pineal gland

  • Part of the epithalamus; secretes melatonin (sleep-wake cycle, circadian rhythm).
  • Projects from the posterior wall of the 3rd ventricle; attached by the pineal stalk; conical, 0.1 gm.
  • Supplied by posterior choroidal arteries; drains to internal cerebral veins.

Pituitary gland

  • In the hypophyseal fossa of the sphenoid; covered by the diaphragma sellae; stalk connects to the hypothalamus.
  • Anterior lobe: Rathke's pouch; pars distalis, intermedia, tuberalis.
  • Posterior lobe: downgrowth of the diencephalon floor.

Blood supply

  • Portal system: two capillary plexuses carry releasing factors to the anterior pituitary.
  • Superior and inferior hypophyseal arteries from the internal carotid; veins drain to dural sinuses.

Adenoma

  • Downward growth: ballooned sella turcica.
  • Upward growth: bitemporal hemianopia.
