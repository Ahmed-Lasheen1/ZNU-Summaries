Central Nervous System
Anatomy
Spinal Cord

The spinal cord is the cylindrical lower part of the CNS that runs from the foramen magnum to the L1–L2 disc level, giving rise to 31 pairs of spinal nerves through its cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral, and coccygeal segments. This lesson covers its external features and segments, the internal grey and white matter organization, how spinal nerve roots exit the shorter-than-canal cord, the three spinal meninges and their spaces, the structures that anchor the cord in place, and its arterial and venous blood supply. Together these form the anatomical basis for understanding spinal cord injury levels, lumbar puncture, and cord ischemia syndromes.

External Features & Location

The spinal cord is the long (average length 45 cm) lower cylindrical part of the central nervous system, occupying the upper two-thirds of the vertebral canal.

  • Beginning: at the foramen magnum, as a continuation of the medulla oblongata.
  • Termination: in adults, opposite the intervertebral disc between L1 and L2; in the newborn, the cord ends at the level of L3, and the adult level is not reached until 20 years of age.
  • Its lower end tapers into a cone called the conus medullaris.
  • Spinal enlargements: cervical and lumbar (detailed in the next section).

Fissures and Sulci

  • Anterior median fissure: a deep midline groove along the entire anterior aspect of the cord.
  • Posterior median sulcus: a shallow sulcus on the posterior aspect.
  • 2 anterolateral sulci & 2 posterolateral sulci: mark the attachment sites of the spinal nerve roots.
  • A posterior median septum extends from the posterior median sulcus into the substance of the cord to a variable extent.

Spinal Cord Segments & Enlargements

A spinal segment is the portion of the spinal cord that gives origin to one pair of spinal nerves. The spinal cord consists of 31 spinal segments: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, and 1 coccygeal. The size of each segment depends on the amount of tissue it supplies.

Internal Structure

Grey Matter

A transverse section shows a central mass of grey matter that is H-shaped, divided into symmetrical right and left masses connected across the midline by a transverse grey commissure. The central canal passes through the centre of the grey commissure. Each lateral mass of grey matter is divided into horns:

HornFunctionDistribution
Posterior hornSensoryAll segments
Anterior hornMotorAll segments
Lateral hornAutonomicOnly in thoracic segments and upper two lumbar segments (T1–L2)

White Matter

In each half of the cord, the white matter is divided into three columns:

  • Posterior column — between the posterior median septum and the posterior horn.
  • Lateral column — between the anterior and posterior horns.
  • Anterior column — between the anterior median fissure and the anterior horn.

The anterior white columns of the two sides are joined together by the white commissure.

Spinal Nerves & Roots

31 pairs of spinal nerves emerge from the corresponding segments (8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal). Each spinal nerve emerges by two roots:

  • Anterior root (motor root): emerges along the anterolateral sulcus.
  • Posterior root (sensory root): attached to the posterolateral sulcus.

The anterior and posterior roots unite within their appropriate intervertebral foramina to form the nerve trunk.

Direction and exit of spinal nerve roots: since the spinal cord is shorter than the vertebral canal, the course of the roots within the canal to reach their intervertebral foramen becomes progressively longer and more oblique moving down the cord. Also, because there are 8 cervical nerves but only 7 cervical vertebrae, the exit level of the nerves relative to the corresponding vertebrae differs between the cervical and lower regions.

Cauda Equina

The cauda equina is a bundle of the lumbar (except L1), sacral, and coccygeal nerve roots surrounding the filum terminale, floating in the pool of CSF within the lumbar cistern (cauda = tail, equina = horse).

Lumbar cistern: the widened subarachnoid space below the conus medullaris; it contains the cauda equina and filum terminale in a pool of CSF.

Lumbar puncture: a needle is introduced into the lumbar cistern between L3–L4 or L4–L5 to withdraw CSF or to inject an anaesthetic agent or dye.

Processes of Pia Mater

  • Filum terminale: a thin, thread-like prolongation of pia mater beyond the conus medullaris. It pierces the dura, passes through the sacral canal and hiatus, and attaches to the dorsum of the first coccygeal piece.
  • Denticulate ligament: 21 tooth-like lateral projections from the spinal pia, lying between the dorsal and ventral nerve roots. They pass through the subarachnoid space and arachnoid mater and attach to the inner surface of the dura.

Factors Fixing the Spinal Cord

  • Denticulate ligament
  • Filum terminale
  • Dura, by its attachment to the foramen magnum and the posterior longitudinal ligament

Arteria radicularis magna: a large radicular artery arising from the lower posterior intercostal or upper lumbar arteries. It is the main source of supply to the lower part of the spinal cord — its occlusion leads to lumbosacral cord ischaemia.

Venous Drainage

The veins draining the cord form six longitudinal venous channels around it:

  • Two median longitudinal veins — one in the anterior median fissure, one in the posteromedian sulcus.
  • Two anterolateral veins — one on either side, posterior to the anterior nerve roots.
  • Two posterolateral veins — one on either side, posterior to the posterior nerve roots.

Communication: superiorly with the cranial dural venous sinuses, and within the vertebral canal with the internal vertebral venous plexus. Drainage occurs via radicular veins into segmental veins.

The internal vertebral venous plexus is valveless and communicates with both the cranial dural sinuses and the prostatic venous plexus below — this valveless connection is a route by which prostatic carcinoma can spread to the brain and meninges.

Key Points / Quick Review

Location & Segments

  • ~45 cm long; foramen magnum to L1–L2 disc (adult); L3 in newborn; adult level by age 20
  • 31 segments: 8 C, 12 T, 5 L, 5 S, 1 Co
  • Cervical enlargement C5–T1 → brachial plexus; Lumbar enlargement L2–S3 → lumbar/sacral plexus

Internal Structure

  • Grey matter: H-shaped; posterior horn (sensory), anterior horn (motor), lateral horn (autonomic, T1–L2 only)
  • White matter: posterior, lateral, anterior columns; anterior columns joined by white commissure

Roots & Nerves

  • Anterior root = motor (anterolateral sulcus); posterior root = sensory (posterolateral sulcus)
  • Roots unite in the intervertebral foramen to form the nerve trunk
  • Cauda equina = lumbar (except L1), sacral & coccygeal roots around the filum terminale

Meninges

  • Dura & arachnoid extend to S2; pia closely invests the cord
  • Subdural space (serous fluid); epidural space (venous plexus, fat); subarachnoid space (CSF)
  • Lumbar puncture: L3–L4 or L4–L5, into the lumbar cistern
  • Filum terminale and denticulate ligament (21 processes) are pial extensions

Fixation

  • Denticulate ligament, filum terminale, and dural attachment to the foramen magnum & posterior longitudinal ligament keep the cord in place

Blood Supply

  • 1 anterior spinal artery → anterior 2/3 of cord; 2 posterior spinal arteries → posterior 1/3
  • Segmental arteries give radicular branches that mainly nourish nerve roots
  • Arteria radicularis magna supplies the lower cord — occlusion causes lumbosacral ischaemia
  • 6 longitudinal venous channels drain via radicular → segmental veins; valveless plexus links to prostatic venous plexus
