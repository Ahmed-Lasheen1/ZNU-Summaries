Heart tube: fusion, dilatations and bending

Source: pages 127 to 128

Fusion of the heart tube

The two endothelial heart tubes fuse into a single primitive heart tube.

  • A: two separate endothelial heart tubes.
  • B: beginning of fusion of the heart tubes.
  • C and D: a single primitive heart tube with a bifurcated caudal end.

The five dilatations

The heart tube forms five dilatations. From cranial to caudal they are the truncus arteriosus, bulbus cordis, primitive ventricle, primitive atrium and sinus venosus.

The truncus arteriosus is the arterial end of the tube. The sinus venosus is the venous end, where blood flows in.

Bending of the heart tube

As the heart tube elongates it bulges ventrally into the pericardium. Progressive elongation bends it on itself, first into a U-shaped and then an S-shaped tube within the pericardial cavity.

Sinus venosus

The sinus venosus consists of a small transverse body and two horns, right and left. Each horn receives three veins:

Vitelline vein
From the yolk sac
Umbilical vein
From the placenta
Common cardinal vein
From the body of the embryo

Later, because of the left to right shift of blood:

  • The left horn becomes smaller and forms the coronary sinus; the left horn and its tributaries regress.
  • The right horn enlarges and is absorbed into the right atrium, forming its smooth sinus venarum part.
  • The opening of the sinus venosus into the atrium shifts to the right: the sinoatrial orifice changes from a transverse slit to a vertical slit, then an oblique slit.
  • The narrowed sinoatrial orifice is guarded by right and left venous valves, whose fusion forms the septum spurius.

Common atrium and AV canal

The original atrial chamber expands around the bulbus cordis to form the right and left auricles. The interatrial septum partitions the common atrium into right and left atria.

The atrioventricular (AV) septum divides the AV canal into right and left AV canals.

Formation of the interatrial septum

  1. Septum primum

    A crescent-shaped septum descends from the roof of the primitive atrium towards the septum intermedium (AV septum). The gap below its lower edge is the foramen primum.

  2. Foramen secundum

    The septum primum fuses with the AV septum and its upper part breaks down. The opening formed is the foramen secundum (ostium secundum).

  3. Septum secundum

    A second crescent-shaped septum arises from the roof immediately to the right of the septum primum. It grows downward and overlaps the foramen secundum.

  4. Foramen ovale

    The atria communicate through an oblique valvular passage between the upper margin of septum primum and the lower margin of septum secundum.

  • Septum primum forms the lower part of the interatrial septum.
  • Septum secundum forms the upper part of the interatrial septum.

Foramen ovale after birth

After birth the left atrium receives blood from the lungs and its pressure becomes greater than in the right atrium. This pushes the septum primum to the right against the septum secundum, so the foramen ovale closes (physiological closure) and blood cannot flow from the left to the right atrium.

Ventricles, great vessels and cardiac wall

Source: pages 132 to 134

Ventricles

The right and left ventricles develop from the common ventricle and the proximal part of the bulbus cordis. Absorption of the proximal bulbus cordis into the common ventricle forms the common bulboventricular chamber.

The absorbed part gives rise to the infundibulum of the right ventricle and the aortic vestibule of the left ventricle.

Interventricular septum

The common chamber is divided into right and left ventricles by the interventricular (IV) septum. It has three parts, from below upward:

  • Muscular part: develops from the floor of the ventricle.
  • Bulbar part: develops from the right and left bulbar ridges of the conical part of the common ventricular chamber.
  • Membranous part: fills the gap between the upper edge of the muscular part and the lower end of the bulbar septum.

Aorticopulmonary septum

The distal bulbus cordis and truncus arteriosus are divided by the spiral aorticopulmonary septum into the aorta and pulmonary trunk. Because the septum is spiral, the relation of the two vessels changes along their length:

  • Lower part: pulmonary trunk in front, ascending aorta posterior.
  • Middle part: side by side, aorta on the right and pulmonary trunk on the left.
  • Upper part: aorta anterior, pulmonary trunk posterior.

The figure labels the truncal ridges, bulbar ridges and neural crest cells.

Cardiac wall

The cardiac wall has three layers, from inside to outside: endocardium, myocardium and epicardium.

  • The endothelial heart tube forms the endocardium.
  • The splanchnopleuric mesoderm proliferates and thickens as the myoepicardial mantle, which forms the myocardium and epicardium.

Fetal circulation

Source: pages 135 to 137
  • Oxygenation of blood occurs in the placenta.
  • The umbilical vein carries this blood to the left branch of the portal vein.
  • The greater amount passes through the ductus venosus to the inferior vena cava (IVC); a small amount reaches the liver through the two branches of the portal vein.

Route of oxygenated blood

  1. Inferior vena cava to right atrium

    The IVC carries non-oxygenated blood from the lower limbs and abdomen, plus oxygenated blood from the ductus venosus, to the right atrium.

  2. Foramen ovale

    In the right atrium this blood is directed through the foramen ovale to the left atrium.

  3. Left side of the heart

    It passes through the mitral valve to the left ventricle, then up the ascending aorta to the arch of the aorta.

  4. Distribution

    Most goes to the heart, neck, brain and upper limbs through the coronary arteries and branches of the aortic arch. A small amount passes to the descending aorta.

Route of non-oxygenated blood

Non-oxygenated blood from the head, neck, brain and upper limbs returns to the right atrium by the superior vena cava.

Positional and septal anomalies

Source: pages 137 to 141

Dextrocardia

In dextrocardia all the chambers of the heart and the associated blood vessels are reversed as a mirror image. It occurs if the heart tube bends to the left instead of the right: the heart is displaced to the right, with transposition.

  • It may be associated with situs inversus (transposition of abdominal viscera).
  • It is the most common positional anomaly of the heart.

Ectopia cordis

Ectopia cordis is a rare condition in which the heart lies exposed on the surface of the thorax. It occurs due to nonunion of the two sternal plates of the developing sternum.

Atrial septal defects (ASD)

An atrial septal defect is a defect in the interatrial septum. Oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix, giving symptoms such as cyanosis and breathlessness. ASD is the most common congenital anomaly of the atria.

Ventricular septal defect (VSD)

VSD is the most common congenital anomaly of the heart.

  • It is most common in the membranous part of the IV septum.
  • It is caused by failure of fusion of the right and left bulbar ridges with the AV cushions, which form the membranous part.

Outflow tract and valve anomalies

Source: pages 142 to 144

Tetralogy of Fallot

Fallot's tetralogy results from a combination of four cardiac anomalies:

  1. Pulmonary stenosis.
  2. Overriding aorta (displacement of the aortic orifice to the right, over the IV septum).
  3. Ventricular septal defect.
  4. Hypertrophy of the right ventricle.

The resulting right-to-left shunting of blood leads to cyanosis.

High-yield

Fallot's tetralogy is the commonest congenital cyanotic heart disease.

Anomalies of the truncus arteriosus

  • Persistent truncus arteriosus: the spiral (aorticopulmonary) septum fails to develop, so the pulmonary arteries and aorta arise from a common vessel.
  • A defect in development of the spiral septum can cause communication between the ascending aorta and the pulmonary trunk.
  • Transposition of great vessels: the ascending aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk from the left ventricle, due to non-spiral development of the aorticopulmonary septum.

Tricuspid atresia

Tricuspid atresia is complete agenesis of the tricuspid valve. It is caused by an insufficient amount of tissue in the AV cushion for forming the valve.

Check yourself

Source: pages 127 to 144
What are the four components of Fallot's tetralogy?

Pulmonary stenosis, overriding aorta, ventricular septal defect and right ventricular hypertrophy.

How does the foramen ovale close after birth?

Left atrial pressure rises and pushes the septum primum to the right against the septum secundum.

Which adult structures do the umbilical vein, ductus venosus and ductus arteriosus become?

Ligamentum teres, ligamentum venosum and ligamentum arteriosum, respectively.

Key Points / Quick Review

Heart tube

  • Two endothelial heart tubes fuse into a single primitive heart tube.
  • Five dilatations, cranial to caudal: truncus arteriosus, bulbus cordis, primitive ventricle, primitive atrium, sinus venosus.
  • The tube bends into a U-shape, then an S-shape.

Atria

  • Left horn of sinus venosus forms the coronary sinus; right horn is absorbed into the right atrium.
  • Septum primum (lower part) and septum secundum (upper part) form the interatrial septum, leaving the foramen ovale.
  • Foramen ovale closes after birth when the septum primum is pushed against the septum secundum.

Ventricles and vessels

  • IV septum: muscular, bulbar and membranous parts.
  • The spiral aorticopulmonary septum divides the truncus arteriosus and distal bulbus cordis into aorta and pulmonary trunk.
  • Endocardium comes from the endothelial heart tube; myocardium and epicardium from the myoepicardial mantle.

Fetal circulation

  • Oxygenation occurs in the placenta; the ductus venosus carries most of the blood to the IVC.
  • Blood crosses the foramen ovale to the left atrium; the ductus arteriosus shunts pulmonary trunk blood to the descending aorta.
  • After birth: ligamentum teres, venosum and arteriosum, and the foramen ovale closes.

Anomalies

  • Dextrocardia: heart tube bends left; ectopia cordis: nonunion of the sternal plates.
  • ASD is the most common atrial anomaly; VSD is the most common heart anomaly, usually membranous.
  • Fallot's tetralogy is the commonest cyanotic heart disease.
  • Transposition: non-spiral septum; tricuspid atresia: insufficient AV cushion tissue.
