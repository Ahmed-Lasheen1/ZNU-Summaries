Ovary: functions, oogenesis and structure

The ovaries are the primary sex organs: paired almond-shaped glands about 3 x 1.5 x 1 cm, located intraperitoneally.

Functions

  • Produce immature female gametes (oocytes).
  • Secrete the female sex hormones: estrogens and progesterone.
  • Secrete inhibin, involved in the feedback control of pituitary FSH production.

Oogenesis

  1. First month of embryonic life

    Primordial germ cells migrate from the yolk sac to the gonads, divide by mitosis and differentiate into oogonia.

  2. Fifth month

    Oogonia enter prophase of the first meiotic division to become primary oocytes, which stop at the diplotene stage and are surrounded by flattened follicular cells.

  3. After birth to puberty

    Many primary oocytes are lost by atresia; about 600,000 remain at puberty.

  4. Shortly before ovulation

    The primary oocyte completes its first meiotic division and produces a secondary oocyte.

The surface layer is called "germinal epithelium", but it is not germinal.

The cortical fibroblasts respond to hormonal stimuli. Hilar cells in the hilum resemble the Leydig cells of the testis and secrete androgens.

Follicular atresia and corpus luteum

Follicular atresia

Definition

Atresia is the degeneration of follicles, which then become atretic follicles. More than 99% of ovarian follicles (primordial, primary, secondary and mature) undergo it.

  • A young female has about 600,000 follicles, and fewer than 500 develop to mature follicles.
  • It starts after birth and continues a few years after menopause.
  • It is accentuated just after birth, when the effect of maternal hormones subsides, and during puberty and pregnancy, when hormonal variations are marked.
  • Atretic follicles are phagocytosed by phagocytic cells.

The atretic process is characterized by:

  1. Termination of mitosis in the granulosa cells.
  2. Detachment of granulosa cells from the basal lamina, which becomes hyalinized (glassy membrane).
  3. Death of the oocyte.
  4. Invasion of macrophages that phagocytose the debris.
  5. Fibroblasts produce a scar of collagen fibers.
  6. Theca interna cells persist in small groups in the cortical stroma as interstitial cells, active steroid secretors stimulated by LH.

Corpus luteum

After ovulation, high LH reorganizes the ovulated follicle into a temporary endocrine gland, the corpus luteum (yellow body).

  • Release of liquor folliculi collapses and folds the follicle wall.
  • Blood enters the cavity and clots (corpus haemorrhagicum); phagocytes later remove the clot and connective tissue invades the center.
  • Blood and lymphatic capillaries grow in and form a rich vascular network.

Granulosa lutein cells

  • Derived from granulosa cells under LH
  • Large, pale, 30 to 50 µm
  • Steroid-secreting; secrete progesterone

Theca lutein cells

  • Derived from theca interna cells
  • Small and intensely stained
  • Secrete estrogen

Wall structure

  • Mucosa: many longitudinal folds, numerous in the ampulla and decreasing toward the uterus, where the intramural portion is smooth. The epithelium is simple columnar with ciliated cells (cilia beat toward the uterus) and secretory cells rich in protein-synthesis organelles. The lamina propria is loose connective tissue.
  • Musculosa: smooth muscle, inner circular and outer longitudinal; its contraction helps move the ovum toward the uterus.
  • Serosa: connective tissue lined by simple squamous mesothelium.

Functions

  • At ovulation the fimbriated end lies close to the ovary, and the oocyte enters the infundibulum through oviduct movement from muscle contraction and ciliated cell activity.
  • Secretions nourish and protect the oocyte, which remains viable for about 24 hours.
  • The tube is the proper environment for fertilization (usually in the ampulla), and its secretion helps capacitate sperms.
  • Cilia and muscle contraction transport the zygote toward the uterus.
  • Active ciliary movement obstructs passage of microorganisms from the uterus to the peritoneal cavity.

Uterus

The uterus is a pear-shaped organ made of the body (expanded upper portion above the internal os), the fundus (its dome-shaped top) and the cervix (lower cylindrical part projecting into the vagina).

Wall layers (outside to inside)

  1. PerimetriumSerosa (connective tissue and mesothelium) or adventitia
  2. MyometriumThickest layer; four poorly defined muscle layers
  3. EndometriumMucosa with simple tubular glands

Myometrium

The inner and outer layers have fibers arranged longitudinally. The middle layers are circular or spiral fibers containing the larger blood vessels and lymphatics, called the stratum vasculare.

Endometrium

  • The epithelium is ciliated and secretory simple columnar cells.
  • The lamina propria is connective tissue rich in fibroblasts, ground substance and type III collagen, containing simple tubular glands that sometimes branch deeper.
  • Uterine gland epithelium resembles the surface epithelium but is rarely ciliated.

Stratum functionalis

  • Superficial lamina propria, superficial parts of glands and surface epithelium
  • Sloughs off during menstruation
  • Thickness controlled by ovarian hormones
  • Supplied by spiral arteries

Stratum basalis

  • Deepest zone next to the myometrium; lamina propria and bases of glands
  • Gland and connective tissue cells proliferate and regenerate the functionalis
  • Does not undergo cyclic changes
  • Supplied by straight arteries

Straight and spiral arteries

Uterine artery branches penetrate to the stratum vasculare and form arcuate arteries running circumferentially. Straight arteries supply the stratum basalis; spiral arteries supply the stratum functionalis and are of special significance in its periodic sloughing.

Uterine cervix

The cervix is the lower cylindrical part of the uterus, below the internal os, projecting into the vagina.

  • Epithelium: mucus-secreting simple columnar cells in the internal part; stratified squamous epithelium covers the external aspect bulging into the vagina.
  • Lamina propria: contains extensively branched mucous cervical glands.
  • Wall: few smooth muscle fibers; mainly (85%) dense collagenous connective tissue mixed with numerous elastic fibers.
  • The mucosa shows no remarkable cyclic change and does not desquamate during menstruation.

Menstrual cycle

After puberty the endometrium undergoes cyclic structural changes driven by ovarian estrogen and progesterone. The cycle averages 28 days, usually starts at 12 to 15 years and continues to about 45 to 50 years (menopause). Fertility is continuous during the years of menstrual cycles, but sexual activity does not end with menopause.

How menstruation happens

  1. Hormone withdrawal

    Progesterone and estrogen fall rapidly when the corpus luteum stops functioning.

  2. Spiral artery contraction

    Blood flow decreases, causing ischemia.

  3. Necrosis and shedding

    Vessels and part of the stratum functionalis necrose, followed by bleeding and detachment of that portion.

  4. Restart

    The endometrium is reduced to a thin layer (about 0.5 mm), and stratum basalis cells divide to reconstitute the mucosa for a new cycle.

Vagina

A highly vascular tube with few naked nerve endings.

  • Epithelium: non-keratinized stratified squamous, with small amounts of keratohyalin granules in some cells.
  • Lamina propria: loose connective tissue rich in elastic fibers, with relatively large numbers of lymphocytes and neutrophils.
  • Musculosa: thick outer longitudinal and thin inner circular smooth muscle bundles; skeletal muscle fibers circumscribe the external orifice.
  • Adventitia: dense connective tissue rich in thick elastic fibers, with an extensive venous plexus and nerve bundles.

Vaginal pH

Under estrogen the epithelium accumulates glycogen and deposits it in the lumen when cells desquamate, so the vagina changes cyclically. Bacteria metabolize the glycogen into lactic acid, giving the low pH that protects against some pathogens.

High-yield

The vaginal wall has no glands; most luminal mucus comes from the glands of the uterine cervix.

Mammary gland: resting

At puberty the gland enlarges through accumulation of adipose and connective tissue and increased growth and branching of lactiferous ducts, caused by rising ovarian estrogens.

Stroma

  • No capsule; covered by thin skin.
  • Interlobar septa: thick fibrous septa with adipose tissue, many lymphocytes and plasma cells; divide the gland into 15 to 20 lobes.
  • Interlobular septa: smaller and more cellular; divide the lobes into lobules.

Parenchyma: no secretory alveoli, only a duct system

Cyclic changes

  • Estrogenic phase: proliferation of duct cells at about ovulation.
  • Luteal phase: increased progesterone enlarges the gland through hydration of connective tissue.

Mammary gland: pregnancy, lactation and involution

Pregnancy

Intense growth results mainly from estrogen, progesterone, prolactin and human placental lactogen.

  • The areola darkens through local accumulation of melanin.
  • Alveoli proliferate at the ends of terminal ducts; they are spherical collections of epithelial cells that will secrete milk.
  • Alveolar cells contain RER, Golgi apparatus, mitochondria and secretory vesicles with dense aggregates of milk proteins; vesicles and fat droplets greatly increase in lactation.
  • Stellate myoepithelial cells lie between the alveolar cells and the basal lamina.
  • Connective and adipose tissue decrease relative to the parenchyma.

Lactating gland

  • Milk is made by alveolar epithelial cells and accumulates in alveolar lumens and lactiferous ducts; many alveoli are distended with milk.
  • Secretory cells are small and low cuboidal, with spherical droplets of various sizes.
  • Cells are acidophilic but may show basal basophilia; lipid droplets appear as clear vacuoles.
  • The cytoplasm has abundant RER, mitochondria, several Golgi complexes, many lipid droplets and vesicles.

After weaning and after menopause

  • With weaning, most alveoli degenerate by apoptosis, with sloughing of whole cells and atrophic absorption of components; macrophages remove dead cells and debris.
  • Senile involution after menopause: reduced size, atrophy of secretory portions and, to some extent, the ducts, plus atrophy of connective tissue.

Check yourself

Which ovarian cells resemble the Leydig cells of the testis?

The hilar cells in the ovarian hilum, which secrete androgens.

Which endometrial layer regenerates the stratum functionalis after menstruation?

The stratum basalis, supplied by straight arteries; it does not undergo cyclic changes.

What prevents degeneration of the corpus luteum in pregnancy?

hCG synthesized by trophoblasts of the implanting embryo, which keeps it growing for 6 months.

What softens the cervix before delivery?

Relaxin, through intense collagen lysis, so the cervix softens and dilates.

Key Points / Quick Review

Ovary

  • Produces oocytes, estrogens, progesterone and inhibin.
  • Primary oocytes stop at diplotene; about 600,000 at puberty, about 450 liberated.
  • Layers: germinal epithelium, tunica albuginea, cortex, medulla (hilar cells resemble Leydig cells).

Follicles and corpus luteum

  • Primordial, unilaminar, multilaminar, secondary, mature (Graafian); FSH drives growth at puberty.
  • Theca interna secretes the estrogen precursor; theca externa is fibrous and vascular.
  • More than 99% of follicles undergo atresia.
  • Granulosa lutein cells secrete progesterone, theca lutein cells estrogen; no pregnancy leads to corpus albicans, pregnancy keeps it for 6 months via hCG.

Uterine tube

  • Infundibulum, ampulla (fertilization), isthmus, intramural portion.
  • Ciliated cells beat toward the uterus; secretory cells nourish the oocyte.
  • Musculosa: inner circular and outer longitudinal.

Uterus and cervix

  • Endometrium, myometrium, perimetrium.
  • Functionalis (spiral arteries) is shed; basalis (straight arteries) regenerates it.
  • Cervical secretion is watery at ovulation and viscous in the luteal phase and pregnancy; relaxin softens the cervix before delivery.

Menstrual cycle

  • Proliferative (about 10 days, estrogen), secretory (about 14 days, progesterone), menstrual (3 to 7 days).
  • Menstruation: hormone drop, spiral artery contraction, ischemia, shedding of the functionalis.

Vagina, vulva and breast

  • Vagina: non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, no glands, glycogen to lactic acid gives low pH.
  • Bartholin glands are homologous to bulbourethral glands; clitoris is homologous to the penis.
  • Resting breast has ducts only; pregnancy adds alveoli; weaning causes apoptosis.
