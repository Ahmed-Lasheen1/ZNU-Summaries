Genital ducts in both sexes

Source: page 113

Embryologically, two pairs of genital ducts develop in both sexes. Which pair persists depends on the gonad.

  • Mesonephric (Wolffian) ducts: in the male they form the definitive genital ducts (vasa efferentia, duct of epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct). In the female they mostly disappear.
  • Paramesonephric (Müllerian) ducts: in the female they form the definitive genital ducts (Fallopian tube, uterus, vagina). In the male they mostly disappear.

Differentiation into the male or female direction is influenced by the gonads:

  • The testis secretes testosterone, which stimulates development of the mesonephric ducts.
  • The testis also secretes Müllerian inhibitory factor, which inhibits development of the paramesonephric ducts.
  • The ovary secretes estrogen, which stimulates development of the paramesonephric ducts.

The male genital ducts develop from the mesonephros and mesonephric ducts, as discussed with the urinary system.

Female ducts: the indifferent stage

Source: pages 113 to 114

The female genital ducts develop from the two paramesonephric (Müllerian) ducts in two stages: indifferent and different.

In both sexes the paramesonephric ducts arise from the coelomic epithelium lateral to the mesonephric duct on each side.

  • They pass caudally in front of the mesonephric duct and finally lie medial to it.

Each duct can be divided into three parts:

  • Cranial vertical part.
  • Middle horizontal part.
  • Caudal vertical part.
  • The two ducts descend side by side and join, and the wall between them disappears.
  • They fuse into a single canal, the utero-vaginal canal, which grows down to touch the dorsal wall of the pelvic part of the definitive urogenital sinus.

  • The tubes descend to lie horizontally in the pelvic cavity, and their lateral ends give rise to finger-like fimbriae.
  • In the fetus, the cervix is larger than the body of the uterus.

Development of the vagina

  1. Contact

    The solid caudal tip of the utero-vaginal canal touches the dorsal wall of the pelvic part of the definitive urogenital sinus.

  2. Sino-vaginal bulbs

    The tip induces two outgrowths called the sino-vaginal bulbs.

  3. Vaginal plate

    Endodermal cells of the bulbs proliferate rapidly and fuse into a solid vaginal plate.

  4. Canalization

    The plate canalizes from cranial to caudal, and the vagina is completely canalized by the 5th month.

  5. Hymen

    A thin plate of tissue, the hymen, keeps the vagina separate from the phallic part of the urogenital sinus. Its central part degenerates to allow passage of menstrual blood.

Anomalies of the uterus

Source: pages 117 to 118

Double uterus

  • Double uterus with double vagina: complete failure of fusion of both paramesonephric ducts and the sino-vaginal bulbs.
  • Double uterus with single vagina (uterus didelphys): failure of fusion of the paramesonephric ducts, while the vagina develops normally.

Bicornuate uterus

The paramesonephric ducts fuse in their lower parts, while the upper parts fail to fuse.

Unicornuate uterus

Only one paramesonephric duct develops and the other fails, so one half of the uterus with one uterine tube persists.

Septate uterus

It is the most common uterine anomaly. The two ducts fuse, but the vertical septum separating them does not disappear, giving a uterus with two halves (cavities).

Arcuate uterus

The fundus is depressed.

Infantile uterus

A small uterus resembling that present before puberty. The vagina and ovaries are normal, and the clinical presentation is amenorrhea.

Anomalies of the vagina

Source: pages 116 to 119
  • Agenesis: developmental failure of the sino-vaginal bulbs.
  • Atresia: failure of canalization of the vaginal plate.
  • Double vagina: mostly associated with a double uterus.
  • Vagina with septum: incomplete degeneration of the lower part of the utero-vaginal septum.

Imperforate hymen

The hymen separates the vagina from the phallic part of the urogenital sinus, and its central part normally degenerates to form an opening.

  • When the hymen is imperforate, the patient presents with amenorrhea.
  • Menstrual blood is retained, causing a condition called hematocolpos.

Development of the gonads

Source: pages 120 to 121

The gonads develop from three sources:

  • Intermediate mesoderm.
  • Coelomic epithelium covering the intermediate mesoderm.
  • Primordial germ cells.

Testis and ovary start alike (the indifferent stage). Only afterwards does development proceed in the male or female direction (the definitive stage).

  • The coelomic epithelium between the mesonephros and the dorsal mesentery differentiates into the genital ridges.
  • Each ridge extends from the diaphragm cranially to the genital swelling on the skin caudally.

Development of the testis:

  • It occurs under the effect of the Y chromosome, which carries the testis detecting factor (TDF).
  • The upper part of the genital ridge becomes fibrosed and forms the suspensory ligament of the testis.
  • The lower part, from the lower pole of the testis to the genital swelling, gains muscle fibers from the surrounding mesoderm and forms the fibromuscular gubernaculum.

Descent of the testis

Source: pages 121 to 123
  • The testis develops on the posterior abdominal wall in the upper lumbar region and descends into the scrotum at or just after birth.
  • Spermatogenesis needs a temperature lower than that of the abdomen. The thin scrotal skin keeps the scrotum about one degree cooler.

Stages of descent

  1. 3rd to 7th month

    Internal descent from the upper posterior abdominal wall to the pelvis, beside the deep inguinal ring.

  2. 7th to 8th month

    The testis passes through the inguinal canal and lies opposite the superficial inguinal ring.

  3. 9th month

    The testis reaches its final position in the scrotum.

Factors affecting descent

  • Elongation of the body relatively lowers the testis.
  • Formation of the processus vaginalis helps the testis reach its final position.
  • The gubernaculum may assist through contraction of its muscular fibers.
  • Increased intra-abdominal pressure helps push the testis out of the abdomen.
  • Male sex hormones greatly influence descent.

The processus vaginalis is a diverticulum of the peritoneal cavity that grows into the inguinal canal and scrotum. The descending testis invaginates it from behind. After descent, the part between the testis and the deep inguinal ring is obliterated, and the part covering the testis becomes the tunica vaginalis.

Anomalies of the testis

Source: pages 123 to 124

Cryptorchidism (undescended testis) is a condition in which one or both testes fail to descend into the scrotum.

  • In about 97% of newborns the testes are in the scrotum before birth. In the remaining cases descent occurs postnatally during the first 3 months.
  • In less than 1% of infants one or both testes fail to descend.
  • It may occur due to decreased androgen (testosterone) production.
  • Ectopic testis: the testis descends to an abnormal site, for example the front of the thigh or the wall of the scrotum.
  • Congenital inguinal hernia: a loop of small intestine descends along the processus vaginalis.
  • Congenital hydrocele: fluid accumulates in the peritoneal sac within the scrotum, because incomplete obliteration of the processus vaginalis leaves a tiny communication between the peritoneal cavity and the tunica vaginalis, allowing fluid to escape into the sac.

Descent of the ovary

Source: page 125

Oogenesis does not need a temperature lower than that of the abdomen, so the ovary descends internally only.

  • The ovary develops on the posterior abdominal wall in the upper lumbar region.
  • It descends into the pelvis and lies on the lateral pelvic wall in a fossa called the ovarian fossa.

Factors assisting descent:

  • Elongation and growth of the body.
  • Role of the gubernaculum ovarii, as in the testis.

Anomalies of the ovary

  • Agenesis of one or both ovaries.
  • Ectopic ovary in the labia majora or the front of the thigh.

Check yourself

Source: pages 113 to 125
Into what do the two paramesonephric ducts fuse, and what does its tip touch?

They fuse into the utero-vaginal canal, whose tip touches the dorsal wall of the pelvic part of the definitive urogenital sinus.

What are the upper 4/5 and lower 1/5 of the vagina derived from?

The upper 4/5 comes from the caudal part of the utero-vaginal canal (mesodermal). The lower 1/5 comes from the vaginal plate of the sino-vaginal bulbs (endodermal).

Why does the testis descend out of the abdomen but the ovary does not?

Spermatogenesis needs a temperature lower than the abdomen's, while oogenesis does not.

Key Points / Quick Review

Genital ducts

  • Mesonephric ducts form the male ducts; paramesonephric ducts form the female ducts.
  • Testosterone stimulates the mesonephric ducts; Müllerian inhibitory factor inhibits the paramesonephric ducts; estrogen stimulates the paramesonephric ducts.

Female tract

  • Cranial vertical parts form the tubes; middle horizontal parts form the fundus and upper body.
  • Cranial utero-vaginal canal forms the lower body and cervix.
  • Vagina: upper 4/5 mesodermal, lower 1/5 endodermal; canalized by the 5th month.

Anomalies of uterus and vagina

  • Septate uterus is the most common uterine anomaly.
  • Vaginal agenesis: sino-vaginal bulbs fail; atresia: vaginal plate fails to canalize.
  • Imperforate hymen causes amenorrhea and hematocolpos.

Gonads and descent

  • Gonads arise from intermediate mesoderm, coelomic epithelium and primordial germ cells.
  • Testis reaches the scrotum in the 9th month; the processus vaginalis forms the tunica vaginalis.
  • Testis anomalies: cryptorchidism, ectopic testis, congenital hernia, congenital hydrocele.
  • The ovary descends internally only, to the ovarian fossa.
