Factors affecting spermatogenesis

Source: pages 214 to 216

In shortSpermatogenesis depends on hormones, a testicular temperature lower than the abdomen, a healthy CNS, and protection from irradiation, hypoxia and toxins.

1. Hormonal factors

FSH
Growth and maturation of the testis; normal Sertoli cell function; helps production of androgen binding protein (ABP); helps the last stages of spermatid maturation
LH (ICSH)
Needed for testosterone secretion by the Leydig cells
Testosterone
Development and maintenance of the germinal epithelium; complete meiosis; spermiogenesis
Estrogen
Needed for spermiogenesis; formed by Sertoli cells under FSH stimulation
Thyroxin
Stimulates spermatogenesis through its effect on cell metabolism; deficient in cretinism
Growth hormone
Promotes early division of the spermatogonia; deficient in pituitary dwarfism
High-yield

Excessive estrogen (a raised estrogen/androgen ratio) decreases FSH secretion and depresses spermatogenesis.

2. Temperature

Spermatogenesis requires a lower temperature than the abdomen, about:

32°C
required for spermatogenesis

Factors that maintain a low testicular temperature:

  • The scrotum lies outside the abdominal cavity.
  • There is no subcutaneous fat in the scrotum.
  • The cremasteric (dartos) muscle relaxes in hot weather, drawing the testis away from the abdomen, and contracts in cold weather, drawing it near to the abdomen.
  • A counter-current heat exchanger in the pampiniform plexus: warm arterial blood runs parallel to, but opposite to, cold venous blood, so heat passes from arterial to venous blood. Testosterone is also exchanged by counter-current.

Increased testicular temperature decreases sperm count, for example with hot baths (45 °C for 30 min/day, as in a sauna), insulated athletic supporters, and cryptorchidism.

Clinical relevance

Cryptorchidism is failure of the testis to descend into the scrotum (undescended testis). Treatment: surgical dilatation of the inguinal canal, or testosterone administration.

3. Central nervous system

The hypothalamus contains dopaminergic and noradrenergic neurons. Their stimulation releases gonadotropin releasing factor and stimulates the anterior pituitary to secrete gonadotropins. Psychic stimuli acting on the hypothalamus can therefore change the degree of fertility. Stress decreases dopamine and gonadotropins and increases prolactin.

4. Irradiation, hypoxia and toxins

  • Irradiation destroys the germinal epithelium. Certain doses spare the Sertoli and Leydig cells, so the endocrine function of the testis is spared.
  • Hypoxia and ischemia depress spermatogenesis.
  • Bacterial or chemical toxins depress spermatogenesis.

Testicular hormones

Source: pages 217 to 221

The testis secretes androgens (mainly testosterone), a little estrogen and inhibin.

Definition

An androgen is any steroid hormone with masculinizing effects, including testosterone. It also includes male sex hormones made outside the testes, such as in the adrenal glands.

The total masculinizing activity of the non-testicular androgens is less than 5% in the adult male. Even in women they cause no significant masculine characteristics, except growth of pubic and axillary hair.

Testosterone

  • Source and synthesis: the principal testicular hormone, secreted by the Leydig cells. The major substrate is cholesterol, and secretion is controlled by LH. A small amount is also secreted in females, probably from the ovary and adrenal cortex.
  • Secretion rate: 4 to 9 mg/day in normal adult males.
  • Transport: 98% is bound to plasma proteins: 65% to β-globulin (gonadal steroid-binding globulin) and 33% to albumin. A small amount is free.
  • Metabolism: most is converted to 17-ketosteroids and excreted in urine; about 2/3 of urinary 17-ketosteroids are adrenal in origin and 1/3 testicular. It is also conjugated with glucuronic acid or sulfate. In tissues it is converted to dihydrotestosterone by 5α-reductase (especially in the adult prostate and the male fetal external genitalia), and a small amount is converted to estrogen by aromatase.
  • Mechanism: like other steroids, it binds cytoplasmic receptors; the receptor-testosterone complex binds DNA in the nucleus and facilitates transcription of mRNAs, which stimulate synthesis of proteins that alter cell function.

Actions of testosterone

Fetal development

  • Development of the male sex organs, for example the penis and scrotum
  • Descent of the testis into the scrotum during the last 2 months of gestation

At puberty

  • Primary sex organs: essential for spermatogenesis
  • Secondary sex organs: growth, maturation and maintenance of seminal vesicles, prostate, bulbourethral glands and external genitalia
  • Body hair increases (beard, chest, limbs, axillae, around the anus); pubic hair is triangular with apex up (male escutcheon); baldness is accelerated with bilateral temporal recession of the frontal hairline
  • Voice: laryngeal mucosa hypertrophies, larynx enlarges, vocal cords thicken and lengthen, so the voice deepens
  • Skin: thicker, more sebaceous secretion, acne
  • Body: broad shoulders and increased muscle bulk
  • Psychological: aggressiveness, interest in sex, increased libido

General metabolic effects

  • Anabolic: increases protein synthesis and decreases protein breakdown
  • Increases bone matrix and Ca++ retention, giving the pubertal growth spurt; fusion of the epiphyses limits the spurt
  • Increases basal metabolic rate, an indirect result of protein anabolism
  • Increases red cell formation by stimulating erythropoietin secretion
  • Increases Na+ reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubules, so extracellular fluid rises slightly

Hormonal control of testicular functions

Source: pages 221 to 223

Control of testosterone secretion through life

  1. Fetus

    Testosterone is secreted by the embryonic testes under the effect of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) from the placenta.

  2. First year of life

    There is a temporary increase in testosterone secretion.

  3. Childhood

    The testes remain quiescent until puberty.

  4. Puberty

    Maximal testosterone secretion, under the effect of LH.

  5. After 60 years

    Gradual decrease, called the male climacteric.

The hypothalamic-hypophyseal testicular axis

Hypothalamus (Gn-RH)
Anterior pituitary (FSH, LH)
Testis

Gn-RH is a decapeptide secreted by the hypothalamus. It reaches the anterior pituitary through the hypophyseal portal circulation and releases FSH and LH, acting through cyclic AMP. Its secretion is continuous in the male, not cyclic as in the female; this is known as sexual differentiation of the hypothalamus.

Control of Gn-RH

  • Long loop: feedback between testosterone, the hypothalamus and the anterior pituitary.
  • Short loop: negative feedback between FSH and LH and the hypothalamus.
  • Nervous factors: emotional and physical stress act on the hypothalamus and decrease Gn-RH, so pituitary gonadotropins and fertility fall.

Pituitary gonadotropins

LH

  • Tropic to the Leydig cells
  • Stimulates testosterone secretion

FSH

  • Tropic to the Sertoli cells
  • Stimulates spermatogenesis
  • Maintains a high concentration of testosterone in the seminiferous tubular fluid
  • Stimulates secretion of inhibin and estrogen

LH and FSH are glycoproteins. They act on their target testicular cells mainly by activating cyclic AMP, which activates specific enzyme systems.

Negative feedback

High testosterone
LH secretion

A high testosterone level inhibits LH secretion at both hypothalamic and pituitary levels, and vice versa.

High inhibin
FSH secretion

A high inhibin level inhibits FSH secretion from the anterior pituitary, and vice versa.

Abnormalities of testicular function

Source: pages 224 to 225

Hypogonadism

Hypogonadism results from failure of testicular function.

Hypogonadism
  • Primary testicular disease
    • Complete eunuchoidism (castration)
    • Partial cryptorchidism: spermatogenesis fails but testosterone continues
  • Secondary pituitary or hypothalamic disease, usually complete

The effect depends on the age at which hypogonadism develops.

Complete prepubertal hypofunction (eunuchoidism)

  • Due to bilateral castration in childhood
  • Infantile sex organs and loss of libido
  • Taller stature due to delayed union of the epiphysis
  • No male secondary sex characteristics: body configuration resembles an adult female, voice remains sharp, skin soft, pale and finely wrinkled
  • Body hair in the female pattern; frontal scalp hairline does not recede
  • Low BMR and psychological changes

Post-pubertal hypofunction

  • Due to testicular, pituitary or hypothalamic disease; normally occurs in old age (male climacteric)
  • Voice remains deep
  • Secondary sex organs regress slowly
  • Reduced growth of body and face hair
  • Wasting of muscles and osteoporosis
  • Depressed sexual functions and sterility
  • The subject becomes irritable, passive and depressed

Hyperfunction

This is a rare condition resulting from androgen-secreting tumors. They cause a detectable effect if they occur early in childhood: the prepubertal boy develops precocious pseudo puberty.

Testicular function tests

  1. Urinary gonadotropins: increase in primary hypogonadism and decrease in secondary hypogonadism.
  2. Urinary 17-ketosteroids: decrease in testicular disease; they reflect mainly adrenocortical secretory activity.
  3. Semen analysis.
  4. Testicular biopsy: may be taken in azoospermia, by needle, to show whether male sterility is due to a defect in spermatogenesis or to obstruction of the duct system. It may also be done to exclude testicular carcinoma.

Key terms

Check yourself

Why does a hot bath or an insulated athletic supporter reduce sperm count?

They raise testicular temperature, and spermatogenesis needs a lower temperature than the abdomen.

What is the difference between the long loop and the short loop of Gn-RH control?

The long loop is between testosterone, the hypothalamus and the anterior pituitary; the short loop is negative feedback between FSH and LH and the hypothalamus.

How do urinary gonadotropins differ in primary and secondary hypogonadism?

They increase in primary hypogonadism and decrease in secondary hypogonadism.

Key Points / Quick Review

Spermatogenesis

  • FSH, LH, testosterone, estrogen, thyroxin and GH all support it.
  • Needs about 32 °C; kept low by the scrotum, cremasteric muscle and counter-current exchange.
  • Stress lowers dopamine and gonadotropins and raises prolactin; irradiation spares Sertoli and Leydig cells at certain doses.

Testicular hormones

  • Testosterone: Leydig cells, LH-controlled, 4 to 9 mg/day, 98% protein-bound.
  • DHT is more potent because its receptor complex is more stable.
  • Estrogens come mostly from aromatization; inhibin from Sertoli cells inhibits FSH.

Control

  • Gn-RH is continuous in the male; LH acts on Leydig cells, FSH on Sertoli cells.
  • Testosterone inhibits LH; inhibin inhibits FSH.

Abnormalities

  • Primary hypogonadism is testicular; secondary is pituitary or hypothalamic.
  • Prepubertal: taller, sharp voice, female-pattern features; post-pubertal: voice stays deep.
  • Tests: urinary gonadotropins, 17-ketosteroids, semen analysis, testicular biopsy.
