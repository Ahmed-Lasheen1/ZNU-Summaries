Pharyngeal apparatus: arches, pouches and clefts

Source: pages 145 to 146

In shortSix arches on each side are separated outside by clefts and inside by pouches.

  • Six pharyngeal arches develop by thickening of mesoderm on each side of the primitive pharynx.
  • Externally the arches are separated by grooves covered by ectoderm, the pharyngeal clefts.
  • Internally they are separated by grooves covered by endoderm, the pharyngeal pouches.
  • The arches are horseshoe-shaped cylindrical bars in the lateral wall of the primitive pharynx.
  • The 5th arch is rudimentary from the start and rapidly disappears; the 6th arch is the most caudal.

The embryo's arches lie around the stomodeum, the future mouth cavity.

In the primitive pharynx the wall has endoderm inside, mesoderm in the middle and ectoderm outside. The arches, pouches and clefts are numbered from above downward.

What each arch contains

The mesoderm of each arch gives three things, and each arch receives a nerve that supplies the muscles of the arch.

  • Cartilages, which change to bones.
  • Striated muscles.
  • A pharyngeal (aortic) arch artery.

Pharyngeal arches and their derivatives

Source: pages 146 to 149

First arch cartilage

The cartilage of the first arch divides into two processes.

  • A cranial maxillary process: right and left processes grow forward above the oral membrane and meet to form the primitive upper jaw.
  • A caudal mandibular process (Meckel's cartilage): right and left processes grow forward below the oral membrane and meet to form the primitive lower jaw.

Pharyngeal pouches

Source: page 149

The pouches are evaginations of the endodermal lining of the primitive pharynx between two arches. There are four pairs.

First pouch
Tubotympanic recess: mucous membrane of middle ear and auditory tube
Second pouch
Palatine tonsils
Third pouch
Most of thymus gland; inferior parathyroid gland
Fourth pouch
Small part of thymus gland; superior parathyroid gland

Pharyngeal clefts and branchial cyst

Source: pages 150 to 151

In shortOnly the first cleft persists; the others are buried by the second arch and the cervical sinus normally closes.

Pharyngeal clefts

  • The clefts are invaginations of surface ectoderm between pharyngeal arches on the outer pharyngeal wall; there are four pairs.
  • Only the first cleft gives the external auditory meatus and the outer layer of the tympanic membrane.
  • The mesenchyme of the second arch grows downward, overlaps the second, third and fourth clefts and fuses with the 6th arch, forming the cervical sinus.
  • The cervical sinus is normally obliterated gradually, so the side of the neck becomes smooth.

Branchial cyst

  • Definition: a cystic swelling lying anywhere along the anterior border of sternomastoid.
  • Site: anterior border of sternocleidomastoid, at the junction of its upper one-third and lower two-thirds, below and behind the angle of the mandible.
  • Cause: failure of obliteration of the cervical sinus.

Fate of a ruptured cyst

  • Rupture on the outer surface of the neck forms an external branchial fistula on the lateral neck along the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid.
  • Rupture on the inner surface of the neck forms an internal branchial fistula.

Development of the thyroid gland

Source: page 152
  • The thyroid begins in the 3rd week as an endodermal thickening in the midline of the pharyngeal floor, immediately behind the tuberculum impar.
  • The thickening is depressed to form a diverticulum, the thyroglossal duct.
  • The duct grows downward and reaches its definitive position by the end of the 7th week.
  • Its tip bifurcates and proliferates into bilateral terminal swellings that expand to form the gland.
  • A portion of the duct near its tip sometimes forms the pyramidal lobe; the rest of the duct disappears.
  • Its site of origin is marked by the foramen caecum, at the junction of the anterior two-thirds and posterior one-third of the tongue.
  • Parafollicular (C) cells develop from the ultimobranchial body (5th pharyngeal pouch).

Check yourself

Source: pages 145 to 152
Which structures separate the pharyngeal arches externally and internally?

Externally the clefts (ectoderm); internally the pouches (endoderm).

Which pouch gives the superior parathyroid gland?

The fourth pouch.

What failure causes a branchial cyst?

Failure of obliteration of the cervical sinus.

Where is the site of origin of the thyroglossal duct marked in the adult?

By the foramen caecum.

Key Points / Quick Review

Arches

  • Six arches; 5th rudimentary; each has cartilage, muscle, artery and a nerve.
  • 1st: mandibular nerve; 2nd: facial; 3rd: glossopharyngeal; 4th: superior laryngeal; 6th: recurrent laryngeal.
  • 4th arch artery: left arch of aorta, right right subclavian; 6th: pulmonary arteries and left ductus arteriosus.

Pouches

  • 1st: middle ear and auditory tube; 2nd: palatine tonsil.
  • 3rd: most of thymus and inferior parathyroid; 4th: small part of thymus and superior parathyroid.

Clefts

  • Only the 1st cleft persists: external auditory meatus and outer tympanic membrane.
  • Failed obliteration of the cervical sinus gives a branchial cyst and external or internal fistula.

Thyroid

  • Starts in week 3 behind the tuberculum impar; thyroglossal duct reaches position by week 7.
  • Foramen caecum marks the origin; pyramidal lobe from the duct near its tip; C cells from the ultimobranchial body.
