Normal blood glucose and its sources

Blood glucose is kept within a narrow range. Fasting blood glucose is 70 to 110 mg/dl, and after a carbohydrate meal it rises to 120 to 135 mg/dl.

Blood glucose comes from three sources:

  • Carbohydrate diet.
  • Hepatic glycogenolysis.
  • Gluconeogenesis, which takes place in the liver and the kidney.

Tissue regulation

Several tissues help regulate blood glucose. The liver is the main regulator, and the other tissues support it in both the fed and fasting states.

TissueRole in blood glucose regulation
Gastrointestinal tractDelays food evacuation to prevent hyperglycemia after a meal. Glucose is absorbed at about 1 g/kg body weight per hour. Incretins (GLP-1 and GIP) help stimulate insulin secretion.
LiverFed state: excess glucose is converted to glycogen and triglyceride. Fasting state: maintains blood glucose through glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
Skeletal muscleStores glucose as glycogen for its own energy. Lactic acid from muscle is reconverted to glucose in the liver. During fasting, muscle is the main source of alanine for liver glucose production.
Adipose tissueFasting state: lipolysis releases glycerol, which the liver converts to glucose. Fed state: glucose uptake increases and is converted to glycerol phosphate and triglycerides.
KidneysTake part in gluconeogenesis. Reabsorb glucose from the proximal tubules through SGLT-1 while blood glucose is below the renal threshold of 180 mg/dl.
High-yield

The liver is the main regulator of blood glucose. Its cells are freely permeable to glucose through the GLUT-2 transporter.

Insulin

Insulin is a hypoglycemic hormone that is secreted after a meal in response to high blood glucose. It lowers blood glucose by:

  • Speeding glucose uptake by muscle and adipose cells through GLUT-4.
  • Increasing glucose oxidation through glycolysis.
  • Stimulating glycogenesis.
  • Inhibiting glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.

Insulin has 51 amino acids in two chains, an A chain of 21 and a B chain of 30, joined by disulfide bonds. It is made in the beta cells in this order:

Preproinsulin
Proinsulin
Insulin + C-peptide

Insulin secretion is stimulated by glucose, amino acids, GLP-1 and GIP, and it is inhibited by epinephrine.

Glucagon

Glucagon is a peptide of 29 amino acids secreted by the alpha cells of the islets when blood glucose falls. High blood glucose inhibits its secretion. It acts on hepatocyte membrane receptors and raises cAMP. In the liver, glucagon inhibits glycogenesis and glycolysis, activates glycogenolysis, and stimulates gluconeogenesis. It has no receptors on skeletal muscle.

Don't confuse

Insulin and glucagon act in opposite directions. Insulin lowers blood glucose, and glucagon raises it.

Epinephrine, corticosteroids, growth hormone and thyroxine

  • Epinephrine is secreted in the fasting state and during stress. It inhibits glycogenesis, activates glycogenolysis in liver and muscle, and blocks insulin secretion.
  • Corticosteroids stimulate gluconeogenesis from amino acids and increase synthesis of the enzyme phospho-enol pyruvate carboxykinase.
  • Growth hormone decreases glucose uptake by tissues, which causes hyperglycemia and can exhaust the beta cells.
  • Thyroxine in excess causes hyperglycemia even though it enhances glycolysis. It increases glucose absorption from the intestine, speeds insulin degradation in the liver, and activates glycogenolysis.

Key terms

Key Points / Quick Review

Normal levels and sources

  • Fasting: 70 to 110 mg/dl. After a carbohydrate meal: 120 to 135 mg/dl.
  • Sources: carbohydrate diet, hepatic glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis.

Tissues

  • Liver is the main regulator; it is freely permeable to glucose through GLUT-2.
  • Kidney reabsorbs glucose through SGLT-1 below the 180 mg/dl renal threshold.
  • Fasting: adipose lipolysis and muscle alanine both feed liver glucose production.

Insulin and glucagon

  • Insulin lowers blood glucose through GLUT-4, glycogenesis and inhibition of gluconeogenesis.
  • Glucagon from alpha cells raises blood glucose through glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.

Other hormones

  • Epinephrine, corticosteroids, growth hormone and thyroxine all raise blood glucose.
