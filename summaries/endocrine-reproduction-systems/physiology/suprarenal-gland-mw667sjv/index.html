Adrenal cortex

The cortex has three zones, from the capsule inwards, each secreting a different class of hormone.

Zona glomerulosa
Mineralocorticoids (mainly aldosterone)
Zona fasciculata
Glucocorticoids (mainly cortisol)
Zona reticularis
Sex hormones (androgens and estrogens)

The gland is rich in vitamin C and cholesterol, the precursor of the corticoid hormones.

Aldosterone (mineralocorticoid)

Aldosterone regulates mineral salts, particularly the concentration of Na⁺ and K⁺.

Mechanism of action

It binds a cytoplasmic receptor in tubular cells; the complex migrates to the nucleus and induces mRNA, giving an aldosterone-induced protein. Three hypotheses explain what this protein does:

  • Permease: increases passive permeability of the luminal membrane to Na⁺.
  • Metabolic: increases substrate oxidation to give ATP for the basal Na⁺ pump.
  • Na⁺ pump: acts directly to increase pump activity.

Actions

  • Renal tubules (distal convoluted tubules and collecting ducts): Na⁺ reabsorption in exchange for K⁺ and H⁺. Lack of aldosterone causes excess Na⁺ loss in urine. Water is reabsorbed with Na⁺, raising ECF volume.
  • Circulation: increases blood volume, cardiac output and blood pressure.
  • Sweat and salivary glands: increase Na⁺ and Cl⁻ absorption, so less Na⁺ in sweat and saliva.
  • GIT: increases Na⁺ and Cl⁻ absorption, and so water absorption.

Regulation of secretion

  • Plasma ions: a 10% fall in Na⁺ or a 10% rise in K⁺ directly stimulates aldosterone.
  • Renin and angiotensin II (and angiotensin III) stimulate the zona glomerulosa.
  • Hypovolemia, hypotension and stress stimulate it.
  • ACTH is not a major factor; large doses raise aldosterone only transiently.
  • Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF) inhibits aldosterone and renin (negative feedback) and reduces glomerulosa responsiveness to stimuli.

Aldosterone escape

With excess aldosterone (as in primary hyperaldosteronism), the kidneys escape its Na⁺-retaining effect despite its continued action, seen as K⁺ diuresis. ANF, secreted from the right atrial muscle because of hypervolemia, inhibits Na⁺ reabsorption in the distal nephron.

Cortisol (glucocorticoid)

Cortisol comes from the zona fasciculata, accounts for about 90% of glucocorticoid activity and is essential for life. It regulates carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism long term and modifies metabolism in mental and physical stress. In excess it is immunosuppressive, anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic.

Metabolic actions

  • Carbohydrate: stimulates hepatic gluconeogenesis and decreases tissue glucose uptake, so blood glucose rises ("adrenal diabetes").
  • Protein: increases catabolism and inhibits amino acid entry into all cells except the liver, raising blood urea.
  • Fat: mobilizes fatty acids, raises blood free fatty acids and their use for energy; ketosis occurs from increased conversion to acetyl-CoA.
  • Electrolytes: promotes Na⁺ and water retention and K⁺ excretion. This is weak normally; excess cortisol gives a mineralocorticoid-like action.
  • Water: causes diuresis by suppressing ADH.

General actions

  • CVS: raises blood pressure (more angiotensinogen) and increases vascular smooth muscle sensitivity to adrenaline and noradrenaline.
  • Blood: decreases lymphocytes, eosinophils and basophils; increases neutrophils, RBC and platelets.
  • Bone: less Ca²⁺ absorption from GIT, less Ca²⁺ deposition, more urinary Ca²⁺ loss; excess leads to osteoporosis.
  • GIT: increases gastric acidity and may cause peptic ulcer.
High-yield
  • Immunosuppressive: large doses decrease antibody formation by destroying lymphoid tissue.
  • Anti-inflammatory: blocks all stages of inflammation and stabilizes leucocyte lysosomal membranes.
  • Anti-allergic: inhibits histamine synthesis in mast cells and basophils. It also delays wound healing.

Regulation

Stressful stimuli act on the hypothalamus to release CRF, which stimulates ACTH from the anterior pituitary, which stimulates cortisol release. Cortisol then inhibits both the hypothalamus and the anterior pituitary (negative feedback).

Adrenal medulla and catecholamines

The medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion supplied by secretory cholinergic preganglionic sympathetic fibers (greater splanchnic nerves). It secretes adrenaline, noradrenaline (both catecholamines) and adrenomedullin directly into the blood.

Receptors and mechanism

  • Alpha receptors: α1, α2. Beta receptors: β1, β2, β3.
  • β1 responds equally to adrenaline and noradrenaline; β2 is more sensitive to adrenaline.
  • Binding either raises Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ permeability (depolarization, excitation), raises K⁺ and Cl⁻ permeability (hyperpolarization, inhibition), or activates enzymes that raise cyclic AMP.

Noradrenaline

  • General vasoconstriction via α1, raising blood pressure
  • Stimulates the heart via β1

Adrenaline

  • Coronary dilatation (indirect metabolic effect, β2)
  • Evokes more anxiety than noradrenaline
  • Stimulates the heart via β1

Regulation of medullary secretion

Secretion is controlled by the adrenaline-secreting center in the medulla oblongata, which is affected by:

  • Fall in arterial BP (hemorrhage): carotid sinus pressure receptors stimulate the center; adrenaline raises cardiac output and BP.
  • Hypoglycemia: hypothalamic glucoreceptors stimulate the center, correcting the low glucose.
  • Hypothermia: hypothalamic thermoreceptors raise metabolic rate and prevent heat loss by vasoconstriction.
  • Muscular exercise: via cerebral cortex and hypothalamus, aiding energy production and power of contraction.
  • Emotional conditions: hypothalamus raises adrenaline and noradrenaline.

These are emergencies with generalized sympathetic stimulation; circulating catecholamines exaggerate sympathetic actions body-wide besides their metabolic effects.

Adrenomedullin

Adrenomedullin (ADM) is a 52-amino-acid peptide made by the adrenal medulla, endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle, myocardium, CNS, placenta, uterus and fetal membranes. It acts as a circulating hormone and local paracrine mediator.

  • Production is increased by hypoxia, proinflammatory cytokines, angiotensin II and aldosterone.
  • Receptors: ADM1 and ADM2 (specific) and the non-specific CGRP1 receptor.
  • Actions: lowers peripheral vascular resistance and BP; increases urine output and urinary Na⁺ excretion; inhibits thirst and salt appetite (tonic inhibition); inhibits insulin and aldosterone secretion; stimulates oxytocin and somatostatin secretion; anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial.

Check yourself

Which cortical zone secretes sex hormones?

The zona reticularis (androgens and estrogens).

Which hormone from the right atrial muscle inhibits aldosterone and renin?

Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF).

Which receptor mediates noradrenaline's general vasoconstriction?

The α1 receptor.

Key Points / Quick Review

Cortex

  • Glomerulosa: aldosterone; fasciculata: cortisol; reticularis: sex hormones.
  • Cholesterol is the precursor.

Aldosterone

  • Acts on DCT and collecting ducts: Na⁺ in, K⁺ and H⁺ out.
  • Stimulated by low Na⁺, high K⁺, angiotensin II, hypovolemia, stress; inhibited by ANF.
  • Aldosterone escape in primary hyperaldosteronism.

Cortisol

  • Raises glucose, protein catabolism, free fatty acids.
  • Suppresses ADH; excess causes osteoporosis and peptic ulcer.
  • Controlled by CRF, ACTH and negative feedback.

Medulla

  • Adrenaline, noradrenaline, adrenomedullin.
  • Noradrenaline: α1 vasoconstriction; β2 more sensitive to adrenaline.
  • Stimuli: low BP, hypoglycemia, cold, exercise, emotion.
