Nervous tissue and the neuron

Nervous tissue is classified structurally into nerve cells that conduct impulses and glial cells that support them.

  • Neurons (nerve cells) conduct electrical impulses.
  • Neuroglia (glial cells) support and protect neurons.
Definition

The neuron is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. It has a cell body (soma or perikaryon) and processes: axons conduct impulses away from the cell, and dendrites receive stimuli toward the cell.

Cell body: light microscopy

  • Size: variable, from 5 to 150 µm in diameter.
  • Nucleus: single, large, spherical and central; pale-staining (vesicular) with a well-defined nucleolus.
  • Nissl bodies: abundant basophilic granules, well stained by basic stains such as toluidine blue. They are free ribosomes and ribosomes attached to RER, and they synthesize the proteins of the nerve cell.
  • Nissl bodies are present all over the neuron except in the axon hillock and axon, around the nucleus, and at the periphery of the cell.
  • Degeneration of Nissl bodies is called chromatolysis.
  • Golgi complex: around the nucleus (the perikaryon); a silver stain shows it well developed.

Cell body: electron microscopy

  • The nucleus has abundant euchromatin.
  • The Golgi complex is well developed and perinuclear; mitochondria are numerous and scattered throughout the cytoplasm.
  • Well-developed RER and free ribosomes appear as Nissl bodies at light microscopy.
  • Most neurons have abundant SER extending into axons and dendrites; its function is unclear but it may help transport synaptic vesicles.

The cytoskeleton supports and maintains the shape of the cell, transports neurotransmitter vesicles from the Golgi complex to the nerve endings, and transmits nerve impulses.

Inclusions

  • Melanin granules: in some CNS neurons such as the substantia nigra of the midbrain; function unknown.
  • Lipofuscin granules: increase with age; accumulated product of lysosomal activity.
  • Glycogen granules and lipid droplets.

Centrioles are absent because mature nerve cells do not divide.

Axon versus dendrites

Axon

  • Single
  • Long
  • Thin, with constant diameter
  • Regular contour
  • Branches at its terminal end (terminal arborization); few collaterals arise at right angles
  • Cytoplasm has mitochondria and neurofibrils; neither Nissl granules nor Golgi complex
  • Centrifugal conduction: away from the cell body

Dendrites

  • Multiple
  • Short
  • Thick with a wide base and tapering end
  • Irregular contour
  • Extensive branches arising at acute angles; some bear dendritic spines (gemmules) that act as synaptic sites
  • Cytoplasm has all structures of the cell body except the Golgi complex
  • Centripetal conduction: toward the cell body

Classification of neurons

Neurons
  • Morphological
    • By number of processes
      • Unipolar single process; rare, mesencephalic nucleus of V
      • Pseudounipolar dorsal root ganglia
      • Bipolar cochlear ganglia, retina, olfactory epithelium
      • Multipolar one axon, many dendrites; most common
    • By shape
      • Pyramidal cerebral cortex
      • Pyriform e.g. Purkinje cells of cerebellum
      • Polygonal or stellate e.g. anterior horn cells
    • By size and axon length
      • Golgi type I large, long axon; cortical pyramidal cells
      • Golgi type II small, short axon; spinal interneurons
  • Physiological
    • Sensory stimuli to the CNS
    • Motor CNS to muscles and glands
    • Interneurons connect sensory and motor neurons

A pseudounipolar neuron is a rounded cell whose single process is convoluted at its beginning (forming a glomerulus) and then divides in an inverted T-shape into a dendrite and an axon.

Nerve fiber

Definition

Nerve fibers are the axons of nerve cells. Histologically a nerve fiber has three parts: the axon, the myelin sheath and the neurolemmal sheath.

The axon (axis cylinder)

The axon has a constant diameter along its length with few collaterals at right angles. Its cytoplasm (axoplasm) is surrounded by the axolemma. It is divided into segments:

Axon hillock
Initial segment
Axon proper
Terminal arborization
  • Axon hillock: first short pyramid-shaped segment; no Nissl bodies, but many microtubules and neurofilaments.
  • Initial segment: between the end of the hillock and the beginning of myelination; contains microtubules and neurofilaments.
  • Axon proper: the remaining part; no Nissl bodies or Golgi complex, but mitochondria, SER, microtubules and neurofilaments.
  • The axon ends in terminal branches (terminal arborization); each ends in a terminal end bulb (button) containing many mitochondria and neurosecretory vesicles.

Myelin sheath

The myelin sheath is a whitish lipoprotein covering of the axon. It is not continuous: it is interrupted by gaps called nodes of Ranvier. The distance between two successive nodes is the internodal segment (0.2 to 2 mm). Each internodal segment shows several oblique clefts of Schmidt-Lanterman, where cells between the myelin lamellae supply nutrition to the deeper myelin layers and the axon.

Functions of the myelin sheath: insulation, acceleration of nerve conduction, and protection and nutrition of the axon.

Neurolemmal sheath

The neurolemmal sheath is a chain of flattened Schwann cells forming a tube around the axon. The cells have flattened nuclei and a small amount of cytoplasm with a small Golgi complex and few mitochondria. Schwann cells are covered externally by a basal lamina and a thin delicate connective tissue layer, the endoneurium. The sheath is interrupted by the nodes of Ranvier.
