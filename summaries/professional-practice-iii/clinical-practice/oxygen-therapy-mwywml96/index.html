Oxygen transport

Source: pages 93 to 94

Oxygen therapy maintains adequate tissue oxygenation and relieves symptoms of hypoxia.

Respiratory anatomy

  • Upper tract: nose, pharynx and larynx filter, warm and moisten incoming air.
  • Lower tract: trachea, bronchi and bronchioles conduct air to the alveoli.
  • Alveoli: tiny air sacs where gas exchange occurs between air and blood.

Oxygen in blood

  • Dissolved oxygen: a small amount in plasma.
  • Oxygen bound to hemoglobin: the majority, carried in red blood cells.

Oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve

Shift to the right

  • Decreased affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen
  • Facilitates oxygen release to tissues
  • Increased temperature, increased CO2, decreased pH (acidosis)

Shift to the left

  • Increased affinity for oxygen
  • Harder for hemoglobin to release oxygen to tissues
  • Decreased temperature, decreased CO2, increased pH (alkalosis)

Indications

Source: page 95

Acute

  • Hypoxemia: low blood oxygen, measured by pulse oximetry or arterial blood gas analysis.
  • Respiratory distress: pneumonia, COPD exacerbations, acute asthma attacks.
  • Cardiac conditions: acute myocardial infarction or heart failure, where oxygen delivery is compromised.

Chronic

  • COPD: long-term oxygen therapy for chronic hypoxemia.
  • Interstitial lung disease: progressive disease needing continuous oxygen support.
  • Sleep apnea: home oxygen for significant hypoxemia during sleep.

Special populations

  • Premature infants: supplemental oxygen to prevent and manage neonatal hypoxia.
  • Cystic fibrosis: oxygen to manage chronic lung infections and inflammation.

High-flow systems

Source: pages 98 to 100

Venturi mask

  • Venturi valve gives precise control of FiO2: a fixed FiO2 of 24 to 60%, with variable flow.
  • Used when accurate oxygen concentrations are required, such as in COPD patients.

High-flow nasal cannula

  • Delivers humidified oxygen at up to 60 L/min, with a precise FiO2 of 21 to 100%.
  • Used for severe hypoxemia or respiratory failure.

CPAP and BiPAP

Devices providing continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) to maintain open airways.

Don't confuse
  • CPAP: obstructive sleep apnea.
  • BiPAP: respiratory distress or COPD.

Administration techniques

Source: pages 101 to 102

Nasal cannula

  • Place prongs in the nostrils, tubing around the ears and under the chin.
  • Set the prescribed flow (typically 1 to 6 L/min); check fit and comfort.

Face masks

  • Place the mask securely over the nose and mouth; set the prescribed flow.
  • Ensure a tight seal and comfort; adjust straps to prevent leaks.

Venturi mask

  • Attach the correct color-coded adapter for the desired FiO2.
  • Set flow according to the manufacturer's guidelines; ensure a snug fit.

High-flow nasal cannula

  • Place the cannula in the nostrils; set flow as prescribed (up to 60 L/min) and adjust FiO2.
  • Check for adequate humidification and comfort; monitor the response.

CPAP and BiPAP

  • Fit the mask properly over the nose or mouth.
  • Adjust pressure settings as prescribed; regularly check for leaks, comfort and effectiveness.

Monitoring and adjusting therapy

Source: page 102

Monitoring techniques

  • Pulse oximetry: non-invasive monitoring of oxygen saturation (SpO2).
  • Arterial blood gas (ABG): detailed information on blood gases and pH.
  • Clinical observation: respiratory distress, cyanosis, changes in mental status.

Adjusting and evaluating

SpO2
Adjust flow rates or device settings to reach target SpO2
ABG
Modify therapy according to changes in blood gas values
Clinical response
Adjust based on improvement or deterioration

Effectiveness is judged by symptom improvement (dyspnea, cyanosis), SpO2 within target range and patient comfort.

Patient education, home therapy and follow-up

Source: pages 103 to 104

Educating patients

  • Demonstrate how to use the device properly.
  • Safety: avoid open flames and ensure adequate ventilation.
  • Teach recognition of signs of oxygen-related complications.

Compliance and home oxygen

  • Regular check-ins for adherence and effectiveness; address concerns.
  • Ensure patients can operate and maintain home equipment.
  • Develop an emergency plan for emergencies and equipment failures.
  • Periodic assessments to adjust therapy and equipment.

Documentation and follow-up

  • Record type of therapy, flow rate and device used.
  • Record patient response (symptoms, oxygen saturation) and any adjustments.
  • Follow-up visits assess effectiveness, track progress and reinforce education.

Key Points / Quick Review

Transport

  • Most oxygen is bound to hemoglobin; a small amount is dissolved in plasma.
  • Right shift: lower affinity, more release to tissues (heat, CO2, acidosis).
  • Left shift: higher affinity, less release (cold, low CO2, alkalosis).

Indications

  • Acute: hypoxemia, respiratory distress, cardiac conditions.
  • Chronic: COPD, interstitial lung disease, sleep apnea.
  • Special: premature infants, cystic fibrosis.

Devices

  • Nasal cannula 1 to 6 L/min (24 to 44%); simple mask 5 to 10 (40 to 60%).
  • Partial rebreather 6 to 10 (60 to 80%); non-rebreather 10 to 15 (80 to 100%).
  • Venturi: fixed 24 to 60%, e.g. COPD; high-flow nasal cannula: up to 60 L/min, 21 to 100%.
  • CPAP: obstructive sleep apnea; BiPAP: respiratory distress or COPD.

Monitoring and complications

  • Pulse oximetry, ABG and clinical observation guide adjustment.
  • Toxicity: lower FiO2. Barotrauma: adjust pressure. Atelectasis: promote expansion. Dryness: humidifier.

Patient care

  • No open flames, adequate ventilation, emergency plan for equipment failure.
  • Document therapy, response and adjustments; follow up regularly.
