Overview: exit and passage

The glossopharyngeal, vagus and accessory nerves leave the brainstem from the same sulcus and share one skull opening. The hypoglossal nerve is the exception.

Tympanic branch and the otic ganglion

  1. Entry to the middle ear

    It passes through a small canal in the roof of the jugular foramen and forms the tympanic plexus (sensory).

  2. Lesser petrosal nerve

    The plexus gives the lesser petrosal nerve, which passes through a small hiatus and groove on the anterior surface of the petrous part of the temporal bone.

  3. Relay

    It leaves through the foramen ovale and relays in the otic ganglion, giving parasympathetic fibers to the parotid gland.

High-yield

The carotid nerve (nerve of Herring) is the afferent limb for pressoreceptor and chemoreceptor reflexes from the carotid sinus and body, regulating heart rate and respiration respectively.

Clinical relevance

A complete lesion causes loss of taste and general sensation over the posterior one-third of the tongue, difficulty in swallowing, loss of salivation from the parotid gland, and unilateral loss of the gag reflex. Pharyngitis is usually associated with middle ear pain.

Recurrent laryngeal nerve

The origin of the recurrent laryngeal nerve differs on the two sides. On both sides it ascends in the groove between the trachea and esophagus to reach the larynx.

Right

  • Arises in the neck in front of the first part of the subclavian artery
  • Curves behind the artery
  • Ascends between the right sides of trachea and esophagus

Left

  • Arises in the superior mediastinum in front of the aortic arch
  • Hooks on the ligamentum arteriosum and ascends behind the aortic arch
  • Ascends between the left sides of trachea and esophagus

The recurrent laryngeal nerve supplies:

  • General sensation to the mucous membrane of the larynx below the vocal cords.
  • All muscles of the larynx except the cricothyroid.
  • Cardiac branches: two long slender nerves on each side that descend to the thorax and share in the superficial and deep cardiac plexuses.
Don't confuse

The cricothyroid is supplied by the external laryngeal nerve (superior laryngeal), while all other laryngeal muscles are supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve. Sensation above the vocal cords is carried by the internal laryngeal nerve and below them by the recurrent laryngeal nerve.

Gastric nerves
  • The left vagus joins the opposite (right) vagus to form the esophageal plexus, from which the anterior and posterior gastric nerves arise.
  • The anterior gastric nerve is formed mainly from the left vagus; the posterior gastric nerve mainly from the right vagus.
  • The gastric nerves supply all abdominal organs and the GIT, ending just before the left colonic (splenic) flexure.
  • Thorax: cardiac, esophageal and bronchial parasympathetic plexuses.
  • Abdomen: gastric and hepatic parasympathetic plexuses, and the celiac plexus, which innervates the pancreas, kidneys, spleen, suprarenal bodies and intestine.

Hypoglossal nerve (XII)

Source: PDF page 101

In shortThe motor nerve of the tongue, supplying every tongue muscle except palatoglossus.

  • Fiber type: general somatic efferent, motor to all muscles of the tongue except palatoglossus.
  • Nucleus: the hypoglossal nucleus in the medulla oblongata, plus the mesencephalic nucleus.
  • It emerges from the anterolateral sulcus of the medulla oblongata.
12rootlets
emerge from the anterolateral sulcus of the medulla

Course

  1. Leaves the skull

    It exits the cranial cavity through the hypoglossal canal.

  2. Joined by C1

    Just below the skull it is joined by fibers from the ventral ramus of the first cervical nerve.

  3. Deep to digastric and stylohyoid

    It passes deep to the posterior belly of digastric and the stylohyoid muscles.

  4. Crosses the carotids

    It curves forwards and medially, crosses the carotid arteries and passes deep to enter the submandibular region.

  5. Crosses the lingual artery

    In the submandibular region it crosses the first part of the lingual artery.

  6. Terminal branches

    It passes deep to the mylohyoid muscle and divides into terminal branches to supply the muscles of the tongue.

Clinical relevance

Injury causes paralysis and atrophy of the tongue muscles on the affected side. On protrusion, the tongue deviates towards the paralysed side.

Check yourself

Which structure carries the afferent limb for pressoreceptor and chemoreceptor reflexes from the carotid sinus and body?

The carotid nerve (nerve of Herring), a branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve.

Which laryngeal muscle is not supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve, and which nerve supplies it?

The cricothyroid, supplied by the external laryngeal nerve (a branch of the superior laryngeal nerve).

Where do the cranial and spinal roots of the accessory nerve go after they separate below the jugular foramen?

The cranial root joins the vagus, and the spinal root supplies sternomastoid and trapezius.

In which direction does the tongue deviate on protrusion after a hypoglossal nerve injury?

Towards the paralysed side.

Key Points / Quick Review

Exit and passage

  • IX, X and XI emerge from the posterolateral sulcus of the medulla and leave through the jugular foramen.
  • IX emerges above the vagus; X emerges above the accessory.
  • XII emerges as 12 rootlets from the anterolateral sulcus and leaves through the hypoglossal canal.

Glossopharyngeal (IX)

  • Nucleus ambiguus supplies stylopharyngeus; inferior salivary nucleus supplies the parotid via the otic ganglion.
  • Solitary nucleus: taste and general sensation of the posterior third of the tongue, plus the carotid sinus and body.
  • Tympanic branch gives the tympanic plexus and the lesser petrosal nerve to the otic ganglion.
  • Complete lesion: loss of posterior-third sensation and taste, dysphagia, no parotid salivation, loss of the gag reflex on one side.

Vagus (X)

  • Five nuclei; the dorsal nucleus gives parasympathetic supply to the heart, bronchial tree and most of the GIT.
  • Descends in the carotid sheath and crosses the first part of the subclavian artery into the thorax.
  • External laryngeal: cricothyroid. Internal laryngeal: mucosa above the vocal cords.
  • Recurrent laryngeal: mucosa below the cords and all laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid.
  • Right recurrent loops behind the subclavian artery; left hooks around the ligamentum arteriosum behind the aortic arch.
  • Gastric nerves: anterior mainly from the left vagus, posterior mainly from the right; supply ends before the splenic flexure.

Accessory (XI)

  • Three nuclei: nucleus ambiguus, spinal nucleus (upper five cervical segments) and mesencephalic nucleus.
  • Cranial root joins the vagus; spinal root supplies sternomastoid and trapezius.

Hypoglossal (XII)

  • Motor to all tongue muscles except palatoglossus.
  • Joined by C1 fibers, crosses the carotid arteries and the first part of the lingual artery, then passes deep to mylohyoid.
  • Injury: atrophy of the tongue on the affected side, with deviation towards the paralysed side.
