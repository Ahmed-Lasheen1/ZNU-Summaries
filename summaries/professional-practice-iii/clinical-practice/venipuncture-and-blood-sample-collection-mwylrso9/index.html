Overview and importance

Source: page 76
Definition

Venipuncture is a fundamental procedure used to obtain blood samples for diagnostic tests, monitoring and treatment.

  • Blood tests diagnose diseases, monitor health conditions and guide treatment plans.
  • Proper technique gives high-quality samples, less patient discomfort and fewer complications.

Anatomy and physiology

Source: pages 76 to 77

Peripheral veins

Median cubital
Antecubital fossa (elbow crease); most commonly used, due to size and accessibility
Cephalic
Lateral side of forearm and arm; harder to puncture, used if median cubital is not accessible
Basilic
Medial side of forearm and arm; deeper and more difficult, used in certain situations

Central veins

Internal jugular
Neck; central venous access in critical situations
Subclavian
Beneath the collarbone; central venous catheters and long-term access
Femoral
Groin; emergencies or when other sites are unavailable

Physiology of blood flow

Veins carry deoxygenated blood back to the heart.

  • Vein wall: three layers (intima, media, adventitia) that affect flexibility and responsiveness.
  • Valves: prevent backflow and can affect the ease of blood collection.
  • Venous pressure: lower than arterial pressure, which can affect the ease of drawing blood.

Indications and contraindications

Source: page 77

Indications

  • Diagnostic testing: infections, anemia, metabolic disorders
  • Monitoring: medication levels, electrolytes, blood glucose
  • Therapeutic: blood transfusions, therapeutic phlebotomy

Contraindications / precautions

  • Infection: avoid sites with signs of infection
  • Thrombophlebitis: avoid inflammation or clot formation
  • Recent surgery: avoid nearby areas
  • Vein condition: avoid sclerosed, scarred or hard-to-palpate veins

Preparation and equipment

Source: page 78

Patient preparation

  1. Informed consent: explain the procedure and obtain consent.
  2. Positioning: patient comfortable, arm well supported.
  3. Hand hygiene: thorough hand washing or alcohol-based sanitizer.

Equipment

  • Needle and syringe: appropriate size (e.g. 21G or 23G for most venipunctures).
  • Blood collection tubes: type depends on the tests required.
  • Tourniquet: engorges veins to make them more visible.
  • Antiseptic solution: typically iodine-based or alcohol swabs.
  • Dressing and tape: secure the puncture site afterwards.

Sterility and infection control

  • Use sterile gloves, drapes and a clean field to avoid contamination.
  • Clean the site in a circular motion from the center outward.

Venipuncture techniques

Source: page 80

Peripheral venipuncture

  1. Apply tourniquet

    About 4-6 inches above the intended site, to distend the vein.

  2. Palpate vein

    Identify a suitable vein by palpation or visualization.

  3. Clean site

    Disinfect with antiseptic solution.

  4. Insert needle

    At a 15-30 degree angle, with a smooth, steady motion.

  5. Collect blood

    Attach the tube or syringe and draw the required amount.

  6. Remove needle

    Apply pressure to the puncture site.

  7. Secure site

    Apply a sterile dressing.

Central venipuncture

  • Use sterile drapes and gloves; consider ultrasound guidance.
  • Identify the site by anatomical landmarks or imaging; give local anesthesia if needed.
  • Insert with sterile technique, confirm placement by blood return, draw blood, then secure and dress.

Special considerations

  • Pediatric: smaller needles and tubes; consider scalp veins or veins in the feet for infants.
  • Elderly: fragile veins; use smaller gauge needles and gentle pressure.

Blood sample collection and handling

Source: page 81

Types of samples

  • Venous blood: from peripheral or central veins, for most tests.
  • Capillary blood: by fingerstick or heelstick, often for glucose monitoring and some screening tests.

Tubes and additives

TubeUsed forContains
EDTAHematologyAnticoagulant EDTA, prevents clotting
Serum separator (SST)ChemistryGel that separates serum from blood cells after centrifugation

Handling

  • Mixing: gently invert tubes with additives.
  • Labeling: patient information, date and time of collection.
  • Transporting: appropriate conditions (e.g. refrigeration for some tests).

Complications and management

ComplicationDescriptionManagement
HematomaBlood outside the vein; swelling and bruisingApply pressure; monitor for further swelling or pain

Documentation and follow-up

Source: page 83

Documentation

  • Date and time of the procedure
  • Vein used and needle gauge
  • Patient reactions or complications

Follow-up

  • Monitor the puncture site for complications
  • Advise patients on site care and when to seek medical attention

Check yourself

Source: pages 76 to 83
Which vein is most commonly used for venipuncture, and why?

The median cubital vein, because of its size and accessibility.

Which tube is used for coagulation tests?

The sodium citrate tube, which binds calcium to prevent clotting.

How should phlebitis be managed?

Remove the needle and apply warm compresses to the affected area.

Key Points / Quick Review

Veins

  • Peripheral: median cubital (most common), cephalic, basilic
  • Central: internal jugular, subclavian, femoral

Technique

  • Tourniquet 4-6 inches above the site
  • Needle at 15-30 degrees; circular cleaning from center outward
  • Pressure and sterile dressing after removal

Tubes

  • EDTA: hematology
  • SST: chemistry
  • Sodium citrate: coagulation

Complications

  • Hematoma, phlebitis, infection, nerve injury
