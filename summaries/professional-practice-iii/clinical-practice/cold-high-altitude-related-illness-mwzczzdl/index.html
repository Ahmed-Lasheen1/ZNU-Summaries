Why this topic matters

Source: page 115

Extreme environments cause serious illness that is preventable if recognised early.

  • Cold-related illnesses: hypothermia and frostbite.
  • High-altitude illnesses: acute mountain sickness (AMS), high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) and high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE).

Why it is important clinically

  • Prevention: knowing risk factors and preventive measures lowers incidence.
  • Diagnosis and management: early recognition and proper management improve outcomes.
  • Patient education: teaching patients to prevent and recognise these conditions improves safety.

Cold-related illnesses: mechanisms and features

Source: pages 116 to 117

These illnesses follow prolonged exposure to low temperatures, which disrupts physiology and metabolism.

Body's response to cold

  • Thermoregulation keeps core temperature steady through vasoconstriction (narrowing of blood vessels), shivering, and behavioural adaptation (seeking warmth, layering clothes).

The two illnesses

Frostbite

  • Localised freezing of tissue, mainly the extremities: fingers, toes, ears, nose.
  • Ice crystals form within cells, causing cellular damage and reduced blood flow.

Hypothermia

  • The body loses heat faster than it can produce it.
  • Core temperature falls below 35°C (95°F), affecting metabolism and organ function.

Clinical manifestations of frostbite

  • Early: numbness, tingling and redness of the affected area.
  • Advanced: skin pale or bluish, hard and waxy; blisters may develop; severe cases progress to tissue necrosis and gangrene.

Cold-related illnesses: diagnosis and management

Source: page 117

Diagnosis

  • Frostbite: clinical examination (skin colour and texture); advanced cases may need imaging to assess tissue damage.
  • Hypothermia: clinical history and examination. Core temperature measurement is crucial; labs may show electrolyte or metabolic abnormalities.

Management

Frostbite

  • Rewarm gradually with body heat or warm (not hot) water.
  • Do not rub or use direct heat: it causes further tissue damage.
  • Analgesics, assess for infection; surgery for severe necrosis.

Hypothermia

  • Move to a warm place, remove wet clothing, rewarm gently with blankets or warm water baths.
  • Avoid rapid rewarming: it can cause cardiovascular instability.
  • Warmed IV fluids, close monitoring of vital signs, treat arrhythmias and electrolyte imbalances.

High-altitude illnesses: mechanisms and features

Source: page 118

They result from reduced atmospheric pressure and lower oxygen levels at elevations above 2,500 m (8,200 ft).

  • Hypoxia: insufficient oxygen in blood and tissues.
  • Acclimatization: physiological adaptation, with more red blood cell production, better oxygen delivery and adjusted ventilation rate.

High-altitude illnesses: diagnosis and management

Source: pages 119 to 120

Diagnosis

  • AMS: clinical, from symptoms and altitude exposure; other conditions must be excluded.
  • HAPE: clinical presentation and examination; chest X-ray may show pulmonary edema.
  • HACE: symptoms and neurological examination; CT or MRI may rule out other causes of neurological symptoms.

Management

IllnessImmediate careMedical treatment
AMSRest, hydration, descend if symptoms persist or worsenAcetazolamide (Diamox) to facilitate acclimatization; anti-nausea and pain relief
HAPEDescend immediately, supplemental oxygen, restDiuretics (e.g. furosemide) and nifedipine to reduce pulmonary pressure and edema
HACERapid descent, supplemental oxygenDexamethasone (corticosteroid) to reduce cerebral edema; close monitoring and supportive care
High-yield

Descent is part of the immediate care for all three illnesses; HAPE and HACE also need supplemental oxygen.

Prevention

Source: pages 120 to 121

Cold-related illnesses

  • Proper clothing: layered, moisture-wicking clothing; insulated gloves, hats and footwear.
  • Avoid prolonged exposure: limit time in extreme cold and take warming breaks.
  • Stay dry: wet clothing increases heat loss; use waterproof outer layers and change wet clothes promptly.
  • Acclimatization: gradual exposure improves tolerance; adapt clothing and activity to the weather.

High-altitude illnesses

  • Gradual ascent: no more than 300-500 m (1,000-1,500 ft) per day above 3,000 m (10,000 ft).
  • Hydration and nutrition: adequate fluids and a balanced diet.
  • Medication: prophylactic acetazolamide for individuals at high risk.
  • Acclimatization: spend time at intermediate altitudes before going higher.

Case studies

Source: page 121

Frostbite

  • Case: a skier has numb, tingling fingers after a long day in sub-zero temperatures; fingertips are pale and hard with diminished sensation.
  • Diagnosis: frostbite, from the skin changes and symptoms.
  • Management: rewarm with body heat or warm water, no rubbing, pain management, monitor for infection; surgery to remove necrotic tissue in severe cases.

HACE

  • Case: a trekker at 4,500 m has severe headache, shortness of breath and confusion; examination shows cyanosis and ataxia.
  • Diagnosis: HACE, from symptoms, altitude history and neurological findings.
  • Management: rapid descent, supplemental oxygen, dexamethasone, close monitoring and supportive care.

Check yourself

Source: pages 115 to 122
Below what core temperature is hypothermia defined?

Below 35°C (95°F).

Which drug is given in HACE to reduce cerebral edema?

Dexamethasone, a corticosteroid.

Why should frostbitten areas not be rubbed or heated directly?

It can cause further tissue damage.

Key Points / Quick Review

Cold-related illnesses

  • Frostbite: local freezing, ice crystals in cells; hypothermia: core temperature below 35°C.
  • Hypothermia: shivering and confusion (mild), intense shivering (moderate), shivering stops (severe).
  • Rewarm gradually; avoid rubbing and rapid rewarming.

High-altitude illnesses

  • Occur above 2,500 m because of hypoxia.
  • AMS: headache, nausea, dizziness within 6-12 hours.
  • HAPE: dyspnea, cough, pink frothy sputum after 1-4 days.
  • HACE: severe headache, confusion, ataxia; life-threatening.

Management and prevention

  • AMS: rest, hydration, acetazolamide; HAPE: descent, oxygen, furosemide, nifedipine; HACE: descent, oxygen, dexamethasone.
  • Cold: layered clothing, stay dry, limit exposure.
  • Altitude: ascend 300-500 m per day above 3,000 m, hydrate, acclimatize.
