What hormones are

Definition

Classically, a hormone is a substance produced by endocrine glands directly into the blood to act on distant target tissue. In a broader sense, a hormone is any substance that carries a signal to the tissue.

Hormones share several properties. They are synthesized by endocrine glands, act at very small concentrations, have a short half-life, are regulated by feedback, and respond to physiological changes.

Functions

  • Regulatory functions
  • Growth
  • Reproduction
  • Homeostasis, keeping the internal environment constant

Classification of hormones

Hormones are classified in three ways: by chemical nature, by solubility, and by mechanism of action. The chemical classes and their examples are:

Proteins and glycoproteins
Insulin, growth hormone, prolactin, LH, FSH, parathyroid hormone
Peptides
Glucagon, oxytocin, ADH, hypothalamic hormones
Amino acid derivatives
T3, T4, epinephrine, norepinephrine
Steroids
Glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, male and female sex hormones, 1,25 dihydroxycholecalciferol

By solubility, hormones are either hydrophilic, such as insulin and glucagon, or hydrophobic, such as steroid hormones and thyroxine. This solubility is what decides the mechanism of action.

Hormone receptors

Hormones act through specific hormone receptors. Every receptor has two domains:

  • A binding domain, which binds the hormone.
  • A signal (transducing) domain, which produces signals that control the intracellular function.

Group I hormones: intracellular receptors

In shortGroup I hormones are lipophilic and switch on gene transcription through receptors inside the cell.

Group I hormones are hydrophobic. They use receptors located in the cytoplasm, following these steps:

  1. Entry

    The hormone enters the cell by diffusion.

  2. Complex formation

    It binds an intracellular cytoplasmic receptor, forming a hormone-receptor complex.

  3. Nuclear binding

    The complex moves to the nucleus and binds a specific area of DNA called the hormone response element (HRE).

  4. Gene expression

    Binding stimulates transcription of specific genes, so specific proteins, mainly enzymes, are synthesized.

Examples of group I hormones are glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, male sex hormones, female sex hormones, retinoic acids and 1,25 dihydroxycholecalciferol.

Group II hormones: membrane receptors and second messengers

Group II hormones are hydrophilic and act through receptors on the cell membrane. The hormone is the first messenger. It forms a second messenger inside the cell, and the second messenger carries out the biochemical function.

  • Hydrophobic

  • Acts through the hormone-receptor complex

The G protein

Receptors in this group work with a G protein, made of alpha, beta and gamma subunits. The alpha subunit binds either GDP or GTP. When the hormone binds its receptor, GDP on the alpha subunit is replaced by GTP. The alpha-GTP then separates from the beta and gamma subunits and becomes active. The type of G protein decides the effect:

  • Gs stimulates adenyl cyclase.
  • Gi inhibits adenyl cyclase.
  • Gplc activates phospholipase C.

G protein cycle

The alpha subunit contains GTPase, which hydrolyzes GTP to GDP and Pi. The inactive alpha-GDP then binds again to the beta and gamma subunits.

The cAMP pathway

  1. Gs activation

    The alpha-GTP of Gs activates adenyl cyclase in the cell membrane.

  2. cAMP formation

    Adenyl cyclase converts ATP to 3',5'-cAMP, the second messenger.

  3. PKA activation

    Four cAMP molecules bind the two regulatory subunits of protein kinase A (PKA). This releases the two catalytic subunits, which are now active.

  4. Phosphorylation

    Active PKA adds phosphate to serine or threonine residues of enzymes. Phosphorylation activates glycogen phosphorylase and inhibits glycogen synthase.

  5. Gene control

    The catalytic subunits phosphorylate CREB, which moves to the nucleus, binds the cAMP response element (CRE) and affects gene transcription.

Don't confuse

Gs stimulates adenyl cyclase, while Gi inhibits it. Gi does this in two ways: its released alpha-GTP directly inhibits the enzyme, and its beta and gamma subunits block the activation of adenyl cyclase by Gs.

Phosphodiesterase hydrolyzes 3',5'-cAMP to 5'-AMP and so ends the hormone's action. Insulin activates phosphodiesterase, while caffeine inhibits it.

Clinical relevance

Cholera toxin ribosylates the alpha subunit of Gs. This blocks its GTPase activity and keeps the G protein permanently active, so cAMP stays high. The result is sustained chloride channel activity, loss of sodium chloride and watery diarrhea.

The calcium and phosphatidylinositol pathway

  • The Gplc-activated phospholipase C hydrolyzes phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate into diacylglycerol and inositol triphosphate (IP3).
  • Diacylglycerol activates protein kinase C, which phosphorylates many enzymes and proteins.
  • IP3 releases calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds calmodulin, which has four calcium binding sites, and activates calmodulin-dependent kinases.
  • The signal is turned off when Ca2+-ATPase pumps remove calcium from the cytosol and IP3 is degraded.

The cGMP pathway

Guanylyl cyclase makes cGMP from GTP. cGMP activates protein kinase G, which phosphorylates smooth muscle myosin, leading to relaxation and vasodilatation. Atrial natriuretic factor binds transmembrane receptors with guanylyl cyclase in their cytoplasmic domain. Nitric oxide, produced by nitric oxide synthase, diffuses into cells and activates soluble guanylate cyclase.

The kinase and phosphatase cascade

Insulin and growth factors such as epidermal growth factor and insulin-like growth factor act through transmembrane receptors whose cytoplasmic domain contains tyrosine kinase. Hormone binding to the extracellular domain activates this tyrosine kinase, which starts a cascade of protein kinases.

Check yourself

Which hormone group uses receptors in the cytoplasm?

Group I hormones, which are hydrophobic. Glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids are examples.

What does a Gi protein do to adenyl cyclase?

It inhibits adenyl cyclase, which lowers cAMP formation.

Which second messenger does atrial natriuretic factor use?

cGMP, made from GTP by guanylyl cyclase.

Key Points / Quick Review

Classification

  • Chemical classes: proteins and glycoproteins, peptides, amino acid derivatives, steroids.
  • Hydrophilic: insulin and glucagon. Hydrophobic: steroids and thyroxine.

Group I

  • Hydrophobic hormones with cytoplasmic receptors.
  • The complex binds the HRE and stimulates transcription.

Group II

  • Hydrophilic hormones with cell membrane receptors and G proteins.
  • Gs stimulates adenyl cyclase; Gi inhibits it; Gplc activates phospholipase C.

Second messengers

  • cAMP: PKA. Calcium and IP3/DAG: PKC and calmodulin. cGMP: PKG. Kinase cascade: insulin and growth factors.
