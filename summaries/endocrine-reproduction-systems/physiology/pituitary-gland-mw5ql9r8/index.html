Pineal gland

The pineal gland projects from the posterior wall of the 3rd ventricle and is attached to the rest of the brain by the pineal stalk. It is a neuroendocrine gland that secretes melatonin, which modulates the sleep-wake cycle by controlling the circadian rhythm.

Functions of melatonin

  • Secreted only during the dark period of the day. It modulates sleep and waking by establishing the circadian rhythm, the 24-hour cycle of bioactivity that matches the solar cycle.
  • Regulates reproductive processes such as puberty and gonadal function.
  • Has an inhibitory influence on hypothalamic secretion of GnRH in humans.
  • Regulates energy metabolism and glucose homeostasis.
  • Has antioxidant, anti-aging and antitumor activities.

Pituitary gland: lobes and hypothalamic control

The pituitary is a major endocrine gland, lying in the hypophyseal fossa, that secretes hormones controlling the actions of other endocrine glands.

Posterior pituitary

  • Neurohypophysis
  • Vasopressin (ADH)
  • Oxytocin

Intermediate lobe

  • Rudimentary in humans
  • No hormones secreted in adults
  • MSH spreads melanin pigment in melanocytes

Anterior pituitary

  • Adenohypophysis, the "Master Gland"
  • Makes and secretes trophic hormones
  • TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, GH, PRL
Definition

Trophic hormones act on other endocrine glands to stimulate the release of their hormones.

High-yield

ACTH has the same pigmenting effect as MSH. It is the cause of the pigmentation in Addison's disease.

Hypothalamic factors

Neurons from various parts of the hypothalamus send fibres into the median eminence, which secretes polypeptide hormones. These are absorbed into the hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal vessels and carried to the adenohypophysis, where they control release of its hormones.

TSH and ACTH

The anterior pituitary hormones are TSH, ACTH, the gonadotrophic hormones (FSH and LH), GH and prolactin.

Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH, thyrotropin)

  • Stimulates development of the thyroid gland, helps its growth and increases its vascularity.
  • Stimulates the process of thyroxine formation.

Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH, corticotropin)

Functions:

  • Stimulates development of the adrenal cortex.
  • Stimulates formation and secretion of all adrenal cortex hormones except aldosterone.
  • Has a fat mobilizing effect and a melanocyte-stimulating effect.

Control:

  • Feedback: a rise in adrenocortical hormones inhibits ACTH secretion directly on the anterior pituitary and through inhibition of the hypothalamus.
  • Emotional stress stimulates the hypothalamus to secrete corticotropin releasing factor, which stimulates ACTH secretion.
  • ADH stimulates corticotrophin release.

Growth hormone (GH, somatotropin)

GH is a polypeptide hormone that is metabolized rapidly in the liver. It stimulates growth of all body tissues, increasing the size and number of cells.

Functions

  • Protein synthesis: increased by raising amino acid transport through the cell membrane and by stimulating DNA transcription and RNA translation.
  • Lipolytic and ketogenic: increases mobilization of fatty acids and their use for energy. Excess GH causes ketosis.
  • Carbohydrates: decreases glucose uptake by cells (anti-insulin or diabetogenic effect) and increases hepatic glucose output. It also increases insulin release, which may burn out the beta cells.
  • Causes reabsorption of Na+, K+, Ca++, PO4-- and Cl- from the kidney, which helps bone matrix formation.
  • Increases calcium absorption from the GIT.
  • Stimulates chondrogenesis and bone growth in young individuals.

Control of secretion

Feedback

  • Hypoglycemia and increased amino acids stimulate release
  • GH inhibits its own secretion

Hypothalamus

  • SRF stimulates release
  • Cellular protein depletion and stress enhance SRF
  • Somatostatin is inhibitory

GH release is also increased by exercise, stress, glucagon and ghrelin.

Mechanism of action

GH acts on the liver to produce somatomedin C (IGF-1, or sulfation factor, because it stimulates incorporation of sulfate into cartilage). Somatomedin C induces growth-promoting activities in many tissues, such as cartilage, with a prolonged duration of action (20 hours).

Prolactin

Prolactin is a protein hormone released from the lactotroph cells (alpha cells) of the anterior pituitary.

Control of secretion

The hypothalamic effect is mainly inhibitory.

Release inhibiting factors

  • Dopamine (tonic inhibition)
  • GABA
  • Gonadotrophin associated peptide (GnAP)

Releasing factors

  • Thyrotrophin releasing hormone (TRH)
  • Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)
  • Peptide histidine isoleucine (PHI)
  • Thyroxine inhibits prolactin secretion by negative feedback and decreases the number of TRH receptors on lactotrophs.
  • Estrogen stimulates prolactin release: it increases the number of TRH receptors, stimulates lactotrophs to secrete and increases their proliferation.
  • Secretion also increases during sleep, severe exercise, stress and sexual stimulation.

Functions

  • Principal hormone stimulating milk formation (the primary protein, casein).
  • Inhibits ovulation by blocking the effect of gonadotropic hormone on the ovaries, the cause of amenorrhea during lactation.
  • Has general metabolic functions similar to GH, such as the diabetogenic effect.
  • During pregnancy, high prolactin stimulates breast growth but no lactation occurs.
High-yield

Lactation is inhibited during pregnancy by placental progesterone, which interferes with prolactin action at the receptor sites on the alveolar cells. Its rapid disappearance after delivery lets prolactin stimulate milk formation.

Prolactin is luteotropic (maintains the corpus luteum). It stimulates milk secretion from mammary glands already prepared by estrogen and progesterone.

Posterior pituitary and ADH

ADH (vasopressin)

  • Formed in the supraoptic nuclei
  • Precursor: prepropressophysin, then pressophysin (neurophysin II)

Oxytocin

  • Formed in the paraventricular nuclei
  • Precursor: preprooxyphysin, then oxyphysin (neurophysin I)

Both are polypeptides formed of 9 amino acids. They are transported as granules by axoplasmic flow to the nerve endings in the posterior pituitary, stored as Herring bodies, and released by nerve impulses from the hypothalamus with the help of Ca++ ions.

Functions of ADH

  • Antidiuresis: increases permeability of the DCT and collecting ducts to water, increasing water reabsorption.
  • Vasoconstriction: no effect on blood vessels normally. In large doses it causes vasoconstriction all over the body except cerebral and renal vessels. A 10% fall in blood volume releases enough ADH to cause vasoconstriction and control blood pressure.
  • Contracts smooth muscle of the intestinal wall, bile duct and uterus.
  • Inhibits renin release.
  • Stimulates ACTH release from the anterior pituitary.

Mechanism of antidiuretic action

  1. Binding

    ADH binds to receptors on the blood-side membrane of the tubular cells.

  2. Adenyl cyclase and cAMP

    Adenyl cyclase is stimulated and cAMP forms.

  3. Protein kinase

    cAMP stimulates protein kinase, increasing microtubule formation in the cell membrane.

  4. Water permeability

    Permeability of the luminal side of the cell membrane to water increases.

Osmotic loop
  • Osmotic: osmoreceptors are in the AV3V region of the hypothalamus. Impulses pass via the hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract. Dilution of extracellular fluid inhibits ADH, and Na+ and mannitol are potent stimulators.
  • Plasma volume: atrial volume receptors normally send tonic inhibitory impulses to the supraoptic nuclei. Increased plasma volume strengthens this inhibition and lowers ADH. Decreased volume (as in hemorrhage) removes it, so ADH rises, water is retained and ECF volume returns to normal.
  • Angiotensin II: increases the size or number of Na+ channels in osmoreceptors, which increases Na+ influx, depolarization and firing in the hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract, so ADH increases.
  • Pain, trauma, anxiety and nicotine increase ADH.
  • Alcohol decreases ADH secretion, causing marked diuresis.

Oxytocin

Functions

  • Uterus: stimulates the pregnant uterus during labour, causing powerful contraction.
  • Fertilization: sexual stimulation reflexly stimulates the paraventricular nuclei, increasing oxytocin. Uterine contractions (orgasm) produce uterine suction of semen upward toward the fallopian tubes.
  • Milk ejection: contracts the myoepithelial cells around the alveoli of the mammary glands (let down).
  • Ejaculation: helps sperm transport by increasing contractility of the vas deferens and seminal vesicle.

Regulation of secretion

Labour
Stretch of uterus, cervix and vagina stimulates stretch receptors, paraventricular nuclei and oxytocin, then stronger contraction
Intercourse
Stimulation of vagina and cervix sends impulses to paraventricular nuclei (Ferguson reflex)
Suckling
Sensory nerves (3rd, 4th and 5th thoracic) to paraventricular nuclei (suckling reflex)
Opioid peptides
Endorphins decrease oxytocin release
Clinical relevance

Stress, fear and anger inhibit milk output in lactating women, because opioid peptides (endorphins) decrease oxytocin release.

Check yourself

Which adrenal cortex hormone is not stimulated by ACTH?

Aldosterone.

How are ADH and oxytocin stored in the posterior pituitary?

They reach the nerve endings as granules by axoplasmic flow and are stored as Herring bodies.

Why does lactation not occur during pregnancy despite high prolactin?

Placental progesterone interferes with prolactin action at the receptor sites on the alveolar cells.

Key Points / Quick Review

Pineal and pituitary overview

  • Melatonin: secreted in the dark, sets circadian rhythm, inhibits hypothalamic GnRH in humans.
  • Posterior lobe: ADH and oxytocin. Intermediate lobe: rudimentary, MSH.
  • Anterior lobe: master gland with trophic hormones, controlled by hypothalamic factors via the portal vessels.

TSH and ACTH

  • TSH: thyroid growth, vascularity and thyroxine formation.
  • ACTH: adrenal cortex hormones except aldosterone, fat mobilization, pigmentation.
  • ACTH is inhibited by adrenocortical hormones and raised by stress (CRF) and ADH.

Growth hormone

  • Increases protein synthesis, lipolysis and ketogenesis, and decreases glucose uptake.
  • Released by hypoglycemia, increased amino acids and SRF. Inhibited by somatostatin and by itself.
  • Acts through somatomedin C (IGF-1) from the liver, lasting 20 hours.

Prolactin

  • Mainly inhibited by dopamine. Stimulated by TRH, VIP, PHI and estrogen. Inhibited by thyroxine.
  • Normal 10-25 ng/ml, 200-400 at term, 400-800 with suckling.
  • Stimulates milk formation and causes amenorrhea during lactation. Progesterone blocks lactation in pregnancy.

ADH

  • Made in the supraoptic nuclei. V1A vasoconstriction, V1B ACTH, V2 antidiuresis via cAMP.
  • Increased by high osmotic pressure, low plasma volume, angiotensin II, pain and nicotine.
  • Decreased by dilute ECF, increased plasma volume and alcohol.

Oxytocin

  • Made in the paraventricular nuclei.
  • Labour contraction, semen transport, milk ejection and ejaculation.
  • Released by uterine stretch, the Ferguson reflex and the suckling reflex. Decreased by endorphins.
