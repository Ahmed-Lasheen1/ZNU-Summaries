Thyroid hormones and how they act

Source: page 64

The thyroid is the largest endocrine gland of the body, lying superficially in the lower front and sides of the neck.

It is H-shaped, with right and left lateral lobes joined by an isthmus. It secretes triiodothyronine (T3), tetraiodothyronine (T4, thyroxine) and calcitonin.

Mechanism of action of T3 and T4

  1. Entry

    T3 and T4 pass across the cell membrane into the cell.

  2. Conversion

    Most T4 is converted into T3 in the cytoplasm.

  3. Nuclear binding

    T3 enters the nucleus and binds its receptors, forming a thyroid hormone nuclear receptor complex.

  4. Protein synthesis

    The complex stimulates synthesis of specific protein molecules with enzyme activity, which produce the hormone's actions.

Two receptor types

Alpha receptor

  • Responsible for general metabolic function

Beta receptor

  • Responsible for brain development and maturation
  • Responsible for TSH release

Functions of thyroid hormones

Source: pages 65 to 66

In shortThyroid hormones raise metabolic rate and influence almost every system, and both too much and too little impair growth and tissue maintenance.

General and special metabolism

  • Calorigenic hormone: increases O2 consumption, heat production and BMR.
  • Carbohydrates: raise blood glucose by stimulating glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis and by increasing glucose uptake by tissues.
  • Lipids: decrease blood cholesterol level.
  • Proteins: a normal level stimulates protein synthesis; an excess increases protein breakdown; low levels decrease protein anabolism. In both hypo- and hyperthyroidism, growth, development and maintenance of tissues are usually impaired.
  • They convert beta carotene to vitamin A.

Cardiovascular system

  • Heart rate rises because the SAN becomes more sensitive to circulating catecholamines.
  • Cardiac output rises by potentiating the chronotropic and inotropic effects of catecholamines, and by a direct action on the heart.
  • Peripheral vasodilation lowers peripheral resistance, giving a rise in systolic pressure and a fall or no change in diastolic pressure.

Regulation of thyroid hormone secretion

Source: pages 67 to 68

TRH from the hypothalamus stimulates release of TSH from the anterior pituitary, and TSH stimulates synthesis and release of thyroid hormones. Two negative feedback routes control this.

Hypothalamus: TRH
Anterior pituitary: TSH
Thyroid: T3 and T4
Negative feedback

Direct feedback

  • Rising T3 and T4 suppress TSH release from the anterior pituitary
  • This suppresses further T3 and T4 synthesis and release

Indirect feedback

  • Rising T3 and T4 suppress TRH release from the hypothalamus
  • This suppresses TSH, then T3 and T4

Other regulators

  • Long-acting thyroid stimulator (LATS): formed by lymphocytes, related to antibodies and causes thyrotoxicosis. Its effect is long-acting, while TSH acts rapidly and briefly.
  • Autonomic nervous system: sympathetic stimulation increases thyroid secretion and effect.
  • Iodine supply: the effect of excess iodine depends on the person.

Normal person

  • Iodine is stored in the gland with no rise in thyroxine

Hypothyroid person

  • Gland enlarged by increased TSH returns to normal and hyperplasia decreases, because iodine inactivates TSH

Hyperthyroid person

  • T4 formation falls through TSH inactivation and inhibited T4 biosynthesis
Definition

The Wolff-Chaikoff effect is inhibition of organification of trapped iodide by excess iodide or iodine administration.

Thyrocalcitonin

Source: page 68

Thyrocalcitonin is a calcium-lowering hormone secreted by the parafollicular C-cells of the thyroid.

  • Suppresses the number and activity of osteoclasts.
  • Increases urinary excretion of Na+, Ca++ and phosphate.
  • Regulation is a simple feedback: a rise in plasma calcium stimulates its release.

Parathyroid gland and parathormone

Source: pages 69 to 70

The parathyroids are two pairs (superior and inferior) of small oval yellowish-brown glands along the posterior borders of the thyroid lobes, within the thyroid capsule. They secrete parathormone (PTH), which is essential for life.

Role in calcium homeostasis

PTH increases plasma Ca++ and maintains it at 9 to 11 mg%. It decreases plasma phosphate and maintains it at 4.5 to 5.5 mg%, keeping the solubility product (calcium x phosphate) constant.

Effects on the cardiovascular system

  • Direct hypertrophic effect on the heart, increasing protein synthesis and protein mass in isolated cardiomyocytes.
  • Positive chronotropic effect (increases heart rate).
  • Vasodilation of arterioles, which decreases mean arterial blood pressure.
  • Coronary and afferent arteriolar vasodilation, which increases renal blood flow.

Parathormone receptors

Type 1 (PTH1R)

  • Found in bone and kidney
  • Ca++ and phosphate homeostasis
  • Acts through increased cAMP

Type 2 (PTH2R)

  • Found in the cardiovascular system, brain, pancreas and placenta
  • Increases cAMP and activates PLC via Gq
  • Raises intracellular Ca++ and activates protein kinase C

Control of parathormone secretion

Source: pages 70 to 71

PTH secretion is not under control of the CNS, the hypothalamus or the anterior pituitary.

  • Plasma Ca++: a direct metabolic negative feedback; a rise in Ca++ decreases PTH secretion and vice versa.
  • Adrenaline: increases PTH secretion through beta-receptor stimulation.

Key terms

Check yourself

Which thyroid hormone receptor type controls brain development and TSH release?

The beta receptor; the alpha receptor handles general metabolic function.

Name the two feedback routes by which T3 and T4 lower thyroid activity.

Direct: suppression of TSH from the anterior pituitary. Indirect: suppression of TRH from the hypothalamus.

Why does PTH lower plasma phosphate while raising calcium?

It increases phosphate excretion at the proximal tubules, keeping the solubility product (calcium x phosphate) constant.

Key Points / Quick Review

Thyroid hormones

  • T4 is mostly converted to T3, which binds nuclear receptors and stimulates specific protein synthesis.
  • Alpha receptors: general metabolism. Beta receptors: brain development and TSH release.
  • Calorigenic; raise blood glucose, lower cholesterol, increase heart rate and cardiac output.
  • Peripheral vasodilation gives a raised pulse pressure (water-hammer pulse).

Regulation

  • TRH stimulates TSH, which stimulates T3 and T4; direct and indirect negative feedback.
  • LATS is long-acting, antibody-related and causes thyrotoxicosis.
  • Excess iodine reduces T4 formation in hyperthyroidism (Wolff-Chaikoff effect).

Thyrocalcitonin

  • Secreted by C-cells; lowers plasma calcium by suppressing osteoclasts.
  • Released when plasma calcium rises.

Parathormone

  • Raises Ca++ (9 to 11 mg%) and lowers phosphate (4.5 to 5.5 mg%).
  • Acts on bone, kidney and (indirectly, with vitamin D) intestine.
  • PTH1R in bone and kidney; PTH2R in cardiovascular system and other tissues.
  • Controlled by plasma Ca++ (negative feedback) and adrenaline, not by CNS or pituitary.
