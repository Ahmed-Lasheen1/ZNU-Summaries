What the ICU is

Source: page 106
Definition

The Intensive Care Unit (ICU) is a specialized hospital unit that provides comprehensive, continuous care to patients with severe or life-threatening conditions.

It is equipped with advanced monitoring and life-support technology and staffed by a multidisciplinary team.

Why the ICU matters

  • Life-sustaining treatment: advanced interventions for acute organ failure or critical illness.
  • Monitoring and support: continuous surveillance of vital signs and immediate response to change.
  • Multidisciplinary care: several specialties collaborate on complex medical situations.

Indications for ICU admission

Source: pages 109 to 110

Common conditions

  • Severe respiratory failure: e.g. ARDS or COPD exacerbations needing mechanical ventilation.
  • Severe cardiovascular conditions: e.g. cardiogenic shock or acute myocardial infarction.
  • Multiple organ failure: several organ systems compromised.
  • Severe trauma: significant injuries from accidents or falls.

Assessment criteria

  • Severity of illness: based on clinical parameters and organ system involvement.
  • Need for continuous monitoring: intensive and frequent monitoring is required.
  • Requirement for specialized interventions: advanced life-support measures or therapies are needed.

Decision-making process

  • Clinical judgment: the patient's overall condition, prognosis and response to initial treatment.
  • Multidisciplinary input: specialists collaborate to decide the best course.
  • Patient and family preferences: patient values and goals of care are considered.

Diagnostic imaging

  • Chest X-ray: commonly used to assess lung and cardiac conditions.
  • CT scan: detailed imaging for trauma, stroke or other conditions.
  • Ultrasound: bedside assessment of cardiac function, fluid status and other conditions.

ICU interventions and therapies

Source: page 111

Ventilatory support

  • Mechanical ventilation: machines support or control breathing.
    • Modes: assist-control, synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation, pressure support.
    • Settings: tidal volume, respiratory rate, FiO2, PEEP.
  • Non-invasive ventilation: CPAP or BiPAP for respiratory distress without full respiratory failure.

Cardiovascular support

  • Vasopressors (e.g. norepinephrine, dopamine): support blood pressure and cardiac output.
  • Inotropes (e.g. dobutamine): enhance cardiac contractility.
  • Mechanical circulatory support: intra-aortic balloon pumps or ventricular assist devices for severe cardiac failure.

Renal replacement therapy

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)

  • For acute kidney injury
  • For hemodynamically unstable patients

Intermittent hemodialysis

  • For patients needing periodic removal of waste products from the blood

Infection control

  • Antibiotics: given based on culture results and clinical suspicion of infection.
  • Sterile techniques: essential to prevent hospital-acquired infections.
  • Hand hygiene: critical for reducing cross-contamination.

ICU management protocols

Source: page 112

Fluid and electrolyte management

  • Fluid resuscitation: fluids to manage shock or dehydration.
  • Electrolyte balance: monitor and correct sodium, potassium, calcium and other imbalances.

Nutrition and metabolism

  • Enteral nutrition: the preferred method of feeding, to maintain gut function.
  • Parenteral nutrition: used when enteral feeding is not possible or insufficient.

Sedation and analgesia

  • Sedatives (propofol, midazolam): keep patients comfortable and cooperative.
  • Analgesics: pain management with opioids or non-opioid medications.

Pain and delirium management

  • Pain assessment: regular evaluation using scales and patient feedback.
  • Delirium prevention: maintain sleep patterns, reduce sensory overload, address underlying causes.

Communication and team dynamics

Source: pages 112 to 113

Interdisciplinary team

  • Regular rounds: structured team discussions reviewing patient progress and treatment plans.
  • Collaborative decision-making: input from various specialists to tailor care plans.

Family communication and support

  • Regular updates: clear and compassionate updates on patient status and treatment.
  • Support services: emotional support and counseling for families.

Ethical considerations and end-of-life care

  • Ethical dilemmas: treatment withdrawal, patient autonomy and best interests.
  • Palliative care: focuses on comfort and quality of life in patients with terminal conditions.

Patient care and safety

Source: page 113

Preventing ICU-associated complications

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)

  • Elevate the head of the bed
  • Oral care

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

  • Prophylaxis with anticoagulants
  • Mechanical devices

Infection prevention and control

  • Aseptic techniques: strict adherence to infection control protocols.
  • Regular cleaning: disinfection of equipment and surfaces.

Safe patient handling and mobilization

  • Proper techniques: lifts and support devices to prevent injury.
  • Early mobilization: encourages movement to reduce muscle weakness and pressure ulcers.

Discharge planning

  • Assessing readiness: deciding when the patient is stable enough to leave the ICU.
  • Coordination with other services: a smooth transition to general care or rehabilitation.

Transition of care

  • Handoff procedures: detailed transfer of patient information to the receiving unit or facility.
  • Follow-up appointments: scheduling and coordinating post-ICU care and monitoring.

Post-ICU follow-up

  • Long-term monitoring: addressing ongoing health issues and recovery.
  • Rehabilitation services: support for physical and psychological recovery.

Key Points / Quick Review

Structure

  • ICU types: general, cardiac, neonatal, pediatric, neuro.
  • Equipment: ventilators, monitors, infusion pumps, dialysis machines.
  • Team: intensivists, nurses, respiratory therapists, pharmacists, physical therapists.

Admission and monitoring

  • Criteria: severity, continuous monitoring, specialized interventions.
  • CVP: fluid status and cardiac function.
  • Pulmonary artery catheter: cardiac output and pulmonary pressures.

Therapies

  • Ventilation: mechanical, or non-invasive (CPAP/BiPAP).
  • Vasopressors support BP; inotropes enhance contractility.
  • CRRT for unstable acute kidney injury; intermittent hemodialysis for periodic removal.
  • Enteral nutrition preferred; parenteral when enteral is not possible.

Safety and discharge

  • VAP: head-of-bed elevation and oral care; DVT: anticoagulants and mechanical devices.
  • Early mobilization reduces muscle weakness and pressure ulcers.
  • Discharge: assess readiness, handoff, follow-up, rehabilitation.
