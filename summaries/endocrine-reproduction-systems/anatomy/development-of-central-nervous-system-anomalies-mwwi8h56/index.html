Formation of the neural tube

Source: page 157

From neural plate to neural tube

At about day 16 of embryonic life, the ectoderm overlying the newly formed notochord thickens in the midline to form the neural plate.

  • The margins of the plate are elevated as the neural folds.
  • As a result, the center of the plate sinks, creating the neural groove.
  • The neural folds move gradually toward the midline and fuse to form a cylindrical neural tube.
  • The tube loses its connection with the surface ectoderm.

Neuropores

Fusion of the neural folds at the cranial and caudal ends of the tube is somewhat delayed. This leaves two small openings, the anterior neuropore and the posterior neuropore.

Development of the spinal cord

Source: page 158

The spinal cord develops from the caudal part of the neural tube.

Positional changes

  • Week 8: the cord extends along the entire length of the vertebral canal, and the spinal nerves exit through the intervertebral foramina at the level of their origin.
  • Week 24: the lower end of the cord reaches the S1 vertebra, due to unequal growth rates of the cord and the vertebral column. The spinal nerves therefore run down obliquely to reach their intervertebral foramina.
  • At birth: the lower end of the cord lies at the level of the L3 vertebra.
  • In adults: the lower end of the cord lies at the lower border of the L1 vertebra.

Development of the brain

Source: page 159

Primary brain vesicles

The brain develops from the enlarged cranial part of the neural tube. At about the end of the fourth week, this part shows three distinct dilatations called the primary brain vesicles, listed craniocaudally.

  • Prosencephalon (forebrain): divides into the cranial telencephalon, which forms the cerebral hemisphere, and the diencephalon, which becomes embedded in the telencephalon.
  • Mesencephalon (midbrain): forms the midbrain.
  • Rhombencephalon (hindbrain): divides into the metencephalon, which forms the pons and cerebellum, and the myelencephalon, which forms the medulla.

Ventricular system

The cavities of the brain vesicles form the ventricular system of the adult brain.

  • The hindbrain cavity becomes the fourth ventricle.
  • The narrowed mesencephalic cavity becomes the cerebral aqueduct (aqueduct of Sylvius).
  • The diencephalic cavity becomes the third ventricle.
  • The twin telencephalic cavities become the lateral ventricles.

Congenital anomalies of the nervous system

Source: page 160

Spinal cord anomalies

Spina bifida is failure of fusion of the overlying vertebral arches, so the vertebral column is defective posteriorly. It includes three types:

  • Spina bifida occulta: bifid spines; the cord and meninges are covered by skin with a tuft of hair.
  • Meningocele: bifid spines, with herniation of the meninges forming a small sac under the skin.
  • Myelomeningocele: the spinal cord protrudes into the meningeal sac, with serious neurological manifestations.

Rachischisis is a severe form of spina bifida in which neural tissue is exposed to the surface. It usually occurs in the lumbar region, due to failure of fusion of the posterior neuropore. Craniorachischisis is a variant in which the spinal cord and brain are both exposed.

Cranium bifidum

Cranium bifidum is defective ossification of skull bone, which leads to herniation of the following:

  • Meningocele: herniation of the meninges.
  • Meningo-encephalocele: herniation of the meninges with part of the brain.
  • Meningo-hydro-encephalocele: herniation of the meninges and brain, with part of the ventricle inside the herniated brain tissue.

Anencephaly (cranioschisis)

Anencephaly is due to failure of the anterior neuropore to close. The brain is represented by degenerated tissue, and the skull vault is absent.

High-yield

Failure of the anterior neuropore gives anencephaly, while failure of fusion of the posterior neuropore gives rachischisis, usually in the lumbar region.

Check yourself

Source: pages 157 to 160
On which days do the anterior and posterior neuropores close?

The anterior neuropore closes on day 25 and the posterior neuropore on day 27.

Where does the lower end of the spinal cord lie at 24 weeks, at birth and in adults?

At S1 at 24 weeks, at L3 at birth, and at the lower border of L1 in adults.

How does myelomeningocele differ from meningocele?

In myelomeningocele the spinal cord itself protrudes into the meningeal sac; in meningocele only the meninges herniate.

Key Points / Quick Review

Neural tube

  • Neural plate (day 16) leads to neural folds, neural groove, then neural tube.
  • The tube loses its connection with the surface ectoderm.
  • Anterior neuropore closes on day 25; posterior on day 27.

Spinal cord

  • Develops from the caudal part of the neural tube.
  • Lower end: whole canal at week 8, S1 at week 24, L3 at birth, lower border of L1 in adults.

Brain

  • Three primary vesicles: prosencephalon, mesencephalon, rhombencephalon.
  • Telencephalon forms the cerebral hemisphere; metencephalon forms pons and cerebellum; myelencephalon forms the medulla.
  • Cavities: fourth ventricle, cerebral aqueduct, third ventricle, lateral ventricles.

Anomalies

  • Spina bifida: occulta, meningocele, myelomeningocele; rachischisis is the severe form.
  • Cranium bifidum: meningocele, meningo-encephalocele, meningo-hydro-encephalocele.
  • Anencephaly: failure of the anterior neuropore to close.
