Metabolic changes of fasting & fed status
At a glance

This lesson explains how the liver, adipose tissue, resting skeletal muscle and brain change their fuel use and metabolism between the fed state and the fasting state.

Hormonal basis of the two states

Feeding and fasting shift the balance between two pancreatic hormones. After a meal, insulin is high and the body is in the absorptive state. During starvation, plasma glucose, amino acids and triacylglycerol all fall, insulin secretion declines and glucagon release increases.

The decreased insulin/glucagon ratio favors a catabolic period, in which triacylglycerol, glycogen and protein are broken down. The metabolic changes of fasting are generally opposite to those of the fed state.

Fed state: the liver

The liver is the nutrient distribution centre. Blood from the gut and pancreas reaches it through the hepatic portal vein, carrying absorbed glucose, amino acids and fatty acids, together with high insulin. During the absorptive period the liver takes up carbohydrates, lipids and most amino acids and either metabolizes them, stores them or routes them to other tissues.

  • Carbohydrate: the liver consumes about 60% of the glucose from the portal circulation. Glucokinase phosphorylates glucose, glycolysis increases, glycogenesis increases and the pentose phosphate pathway is more active. Gluconeogenesis decreases.
  • Fat: fatty acid synthesis and triacylglycerol synthesis are favored, using acetyl CoA and NADPH from glucose metabolism, glycerol 3-phosphate from glycolysis, and fatty acids from de novo synthesis and chylomicron remnants.
  • Amino acids: the liver synthesizes protein from dietary amino acids only up to a limit. Excess amino acids are released to other tissues or degraded.

Fed state: adipose tissue, muscle and brain

Adipose tissue is the energy storage depot. Its GLUT-4 transporters are insulin-sensitive, so glucose enters in large amounts. Glycolysis supplies glycerol 3-phosphate, and the pentose phosphate pathway supplies the NADPH needed for fatty acid synthesis. Fatty acids from chylomicrons and VLDL, together with glycerol 3-phosphate, are used to make triacylglycerol, so storage is favored.

Resting skeletal muscle accounts for about 30% of body oxygen consumption at rest. Its GLUT-4 transporters are insulin-sensitive, glucose is the primary fuel and excess glucose is stored as glycogen. Amino acid uptake and protein synthesis increase to replace protein broken down since the previous meal. Fatty acids are the secondary fuel.

The brain uses glucose exclusively as its fuel in the fed state. It has no glycogen stores, so it depends completely on the availability of blood glucose. Cerebral functions are impaired when blood glucose falls below the level shown below.

Fasting state: the liver

In fasting the liver first supplies glucose from its glycogen stores, which are used up after about 10 to 18 hours. Gluconeogenesis, the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources, begins 4 to 6 hours after the last meal and becomes fully active once glycogen is depleted. Its substrates are amino acids and lactate from muscle and glycerol from adipose tissue.

Fatty acids from adipose tissue become the main energy source of the liver. The liver converts them into ketone bodies, which are released for use as fuel by other tissues.

High-yield

The liver can synthesize and release ketone bodies, but it cannot use them as a fuel.

Fasting state: adipose tissue, muscle and brain

Adipose tissue degrades its triacylglycerol. Fatty acids are released into the blood for use as energy by tissues, and some are sent to the liver for conversion into ketone bodies. Glycerol is taken up by the liver and converted to glucose.

Don't confuse

Glycerol from fat can be converted to glucose, but fatty acids cannot be converted to glucose. Fat is therefore a source of glucose only through its glycerol.

Resting skeletal muscle switches from glucose to fatty acids as its major fuel. By contrast, exercising muscle initially uses its glycogen stores. During the first few days of fasting, muscle protein is rapidly broken down, and the amino acids supply the liver for gluconeogenesis, with alanine as the main amino acid.

The brain continues to use only glucose during the first few days of fasting. In prolonged fasting, longer than 2 to 3 weeks, plasma ketone bodies rise to high levels and are used by the brain as well as glucose. This reduces the need for protein breakdown to supply gluconeogenesis.

Key Points / Quick Review

Hormones

  • Fed state: high insulin, absorptive period.
  • Fasting: insulin falls, glucagon rises, favoring catabolism.

Fed state

  • Liver takes up about 60% of portal glucose and decreases gluconeogenesis.
  • Adipose tissue stores triacylglycerol using NADPH from the pentose phosphate pathway.
  • Resting muscle stores glucose as glycogen; fatty acids are its secondary fuel.
  • Brain uses only glucose and has no glycogen stores.

Fasting state

  • Liver glycogen lasts 10 to 18 hours; gluconeogenesis starts at 4 to 6 hours and is fully active after about 18 hours.
  • Only glycerol from fat can become glucose; fatty acids become energy or ketone bodies.
  • Muscle switches to fatty acids at rest, but uses glycogen first when exercising.
  • Ketone bodies supply the brain in prolonged fasting, reducing protein breakdown.
