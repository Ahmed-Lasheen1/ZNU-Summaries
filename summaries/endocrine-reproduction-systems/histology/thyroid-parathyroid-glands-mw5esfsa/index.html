Thyroid gland: stroma and parenchyma

In shortA thin-septated, highly vascular capsule supports follicles lined by cuboidal cells and filled with colloid.

Stroma

  • Capsule: highly vascularized, made of two layers. The outer layer is dense connective tissue continuous with the pretracheal fascia; the inner layer is adherent loose connective tissue.
  • Trabeculae: very thin, descending from the capsule without any lobulation.
  • Reticular fibers: a fine network around the thyroid follicles.

Parenchyma

Thyroid follicles are the structural unit of the gland. They are rounded, mostly filled with colloid (strongly PAS-positive), and lined by simple cuboidal epithelium resting on a prominent basement membrane.

Each follicle contains two cell types: principal follicular cells and parafollicular cells.

Principal follicular cells

The majority of the follicular epithelium; they secrete thyroglobulin, stored in the follicle center as colloid.

Light microscopy

  • Simple cuboidal cells on the basement membrane, with central pale round nuclei and basophilic cytoplasm.
  • Cell shape follows gland activity: columnar in a hyperactive gland, squamous in an inactive gland.

Electron microscopy (protein-synthesizing cell)

  • Well-developed supranuclear Golgi complex and basal RER.
  • Elongated mitochondria, free ribosomes and many lysosomes.
  • Apical secretory vesicles and many microvilli.
  • Tight junctions between adjacent cells.

Function: synthesis of thyroid hormones.

Don't confuse

Interfollicular cells seen between follicles are tangentially cut follicular cells (with occasional C cells), not a separate cell type.

Parafollicular (C) cells

  • Also called clear cells; larger than follicular cells, with a round or oval nucleus and pale cytoplasm.
  • Found singly or in small clusters between follicular cells, more numerous in the central region of the thyroid lobes.
  • They rest on the same basement membrane and do not reach the lumen.
  • Belong to the diffuse neuroendocrine system (DNES); on EM they are APUD cells with few RER cisternae, elongated mitochondria, a large Golgi apparatus and numerous hormone granules.
High-yield

C cells secrete calcitonin, an antiparathyroid hormone that lowers blood calcium by inhibiting bone resorption.

Making and releasing thyroid hormones

A. Synthesis and storage of iodinated thyroglobulin

  1. Thyroglobulin synthesis

    Starts on the RER; glycosylation occurs in the Golgi apparatus. It leaves in small vesicles and is discharged by exocytosis into the follicle lumen.

  2. Iodide handling

    The cell takes up iodide from blood, oxidizes it to active iodine at the villous surface, and transports it to the lumen.

  3. Colloid formation

    Iodine and thyroglobulin join in the lumen to form iodinated thyroglobulin, stored as colloid.

B. Secretion of T3 and T4

  1. Endocytosis

    On stimulation of the cell receptor, colloid is taken back into the cell by endocytosis.

  2. Lysosomal hydrolysis

    Lysosomes break down iodinated thyroglobulin into thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3).

  3. Release

    T3 and T4 leave from the basal surface of the follicular cell and enter the bloodstream.

Parathyroid glands

Four small glands embedded in the posterior surface of the thyroid gland.

  • Stroma: thin connective tissue capsule; thin trabeculae (septa) that divide the gland into incomplete lobules; reticular fibers forming a network.
  • Parenchyma: cells arranged in cords and clumps separated by blood vessels, of two types: chief cells and oxyphil cells.

Chief cells

  • Most numerous; polygonal with vesicular nuclei.
  • Pale cytoplasm from abundant glycogen.
  • Replaced by adipocytes with increasing age.
  • EM: well-developed Golgi and RER, many spherical mitochondria, abundant glycogen granules, irregular electron-dense granules containing PTH.
  • Secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH) in response to low blood calcium.

Oxyphil cells

  • Appear at about the 7th year and increase with age.
  • Larger than chief cells; polygonal with a small dark nucleus.
  • Deeply acidophilic cytoplasm due to numerous mitochondria.
  • EM: numerous rounded mitochondria with many cristae, small Golgi, few RER.
  • Function not yet known (may be stem cells for degenerated chief cells).

Key terms

Check yourself

What shape are follicular cells in a hyperactive versus an inactive thyroid?

Columnar in a hyperactive gland and squamous in an inactive gland.

Where are T3 and T4 released from the follicular cell, and what comes just before?

They are released from the basal surface into the blood, after lysosomes hydrolyze endocytosed iodinated thyroglobulin.

Which parathyroid cell secretes PTH, and what stimulates its release?

The chief cell, in response to low blood calcium.

Key Points / Quick Review

Thyroid structure

  • Two-layered, highly vascular capsule; outer layer continuous with pretracheal fascia.
  • Thin trabeculae with no lobulation; reticular fibers around follicles.
  • Follicle = structural unit: simple cuboidal epithelium, PAS-positive colloid.

Thyroid cells

  • Principal follicular cells: central pale nuclei, basophilic cytoplasm; make thyroglobulin and thyroid hormones.
  • C cells: pale, do not reach the lumen, DNES/APUD, secrete calcitonin.

Hormone production

  • Thyroglobulin: RER, then Golgi glycosylation, then exocytosis into the lumen.
  • Iodine plus thyroglobulin form colloid; endocytosis and lysosomal hydrolysis release T3 and T4 at the basal surface.

Parathyroid

  • Four glands on the posterior thyroid; cords and clumps of cells, incomplete lobules.
  • Chief cells: pale (glycogen), secrete PTH on low calcium.
  • Oxyphil cells: appear about age 7, acidophilic (mitochondria), function unknown.
