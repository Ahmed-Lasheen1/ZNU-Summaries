1Thoracic Wall & Intercostal Structures›

Intercostal Nerves – Classification

  • Typical (3rd–6th): stay entirely within the thorax; have all 5 branches
  • Atypical: 1st, 2nd, 7th–11th – extend beyond thoracic wall
  • 12th thoracic = subcostal nerve (runs below 12th rib in abdominal wall)

Neurovascular Bundle (Costal Groove)

  • Order from ABOVE downward: VAN = Vein, Artery, Nerve
  • Safe needle insertion: just ABOVE the upper border of the rib below
  • Branches of typical nerve (5): rami communicantes, muscular, collateral, lateral cutaneous, anterior cutaneous

Atypical Intercostal Nerves

  • 1st: most joins C8 → lower trunk of brachial plexus; no anterior/lateral cutaneous branches
  • 2nd: lateral cutaneous = Intercostobrachial nerve → skin of axilla floor + medial arm; mediates cardiac referred pain
  • 7th–11th (thoracoabdominal): supply abdominal wall muscles and skin + parietal peritoneum

Posterior Intercostal Arteries

  • 1st & 2nd: from superior intercostal artery (branch of costocervical trunk)
  • 3rd–11th: from descending thoracic aorta directly
  • Each terminates at costochondral junction, anastomosing with anterior intercostal artery

Internal Thoracic Artery

  • Origin: 1st part of subclavian artery
  • Terminates at 6th intercostal space → divides into: superior epigastric + musculophrenic arteries
  • Anterior intercostal arteries 1–6 from ITA; 7–9 from musculophrenic artery
  • Perforating branches (2nd–4th) supply the breast

Azygos System

  • Azygos vein: formed at T12 (right ascending lumbar + right subcostal) → opens into SVC at 2nd costal cartilage
  • Left 5th–8th posterior intercostal veins → accessory hemiazygos
  • Left 9th–11th → hemiazygos; both cross to the right at T8/T9
  • Clinical: In SVC obstruction, azygos becomes the main collateral channel
NB: VAN order in costal groove. Cardiac pain referred via intercostobrachial nerve (T2 lateral cutaneous branch) to medial arm. Needle insertion safely ABOVE rib below.

2Anatomy of the Diaphragm›

Structure & Origin

  • Dome-shaped: peripheral muscular part + central fibrous tendon
  • Central tendon inseparably fused with fibrous pericardium
  • Sternal part: posterior surface of xiphoid process
  • Costal part: inner surface of lower 6 ribs and their costal cartilages
  • Right crus: L1–L3 | Left crus: L1–L2 (right is larger)

EsophagealT10Slightly left of midline (muscular)Esophagus + Right & left vagal trunks

Nerve Supply

  • Phrenic nerve (C3, C4, C5): ALL motor supply + sensory to central diaphragm/pleura
  • Lower 5 intercostal nerves: sensory to PERIPHERAL part only
  • Mnemonic: "C3,4,5 keeps the diaphragm alive"

Actions

  • Main muscle of respiration: contraction → descends → ↑ vertical diameter of thorax
  • Muscle of abdominal straining (↑ intra-abdominal pressure)
  • Thoraco-abdominal pump: aids venous return to the heart
  • Sphincteric action on esophagus (prevents gastric reflux)
Mnemonic for openings: "I 8 (ate) 10 eggs at 12" → IVC at T8, Esophagus at T10, Aorta at T12.
Hiccups: Involuntary spasmodic diaphragm contraction + glottis closure. Causes: phrenic nerve irritation, subphrenic abscess, uremia, alcohol, hysteria.

3Thoracic Duct›

Formation & Course

  • Formed in the abdomen (near L1–L2) from union of right and left lumbar trunks + intestinal trunk (cisterna chyli is present in ~50% of people)
  • Enters thorax through aortic opening (T12) — between aorta (left) and azygos vein (right)
  • Ascends in posterior mediastinum from T12 to T5 on the RIGHT side of the spine, between the aorta and the azygos vein
  • Lies on vertebral bodies, behind the esophagus and between the descending aorta (left) and the azygos vein (right)
  • At T5: crosses to the LEFT — passes posterior to esophagus from right to left
  • Continues upward on the LEFT border of the esophagus into the root of the neck
  • At C7: curves downward and laterally, passing posterior to carotid sheath and phrenic nerve and anterior to subclavian artery
  • Terminates: at the junction of the left internal jugular vein and left subclavian vein (left venous angle)

6 Lymph Trunk Tributaries

  • (1) Right lumbar trunk (drains right lower limb + right pelvis + right kidney + right half of abdominal wall)
  • (2) Left lumbar trunk (drains left lower limb + left pelvis + left kidney + left half of abdominal wall)
  • (3) Intestinal trunk (drains intestines + abdominal viscera — carries fat-rich chyle after meals)
  • (4) Left jugular trunk (drains left head and neck)
  • (5) Left subclavian trunk (drains left upper limb)
  • (6) Left bronchomediastinal trunk (drains left thorax + left lung)

Area of Drainage

  • ALL body regions BELOW the diaphragm (both sides)
  • Entire LEFT side of body ABOVE the diaphragm
  • Right lymphatic duct (short, 1.25 cm) drains: right head/neck, right upper limb, right thorax — opens into right venous angle

Clinical Correlations

  • Chylothorax: Thoracic duct injury (surgery, trauma) → chyle (milky lymph) accumulates in LEFT pleural cavity. Recognized by milky appearance + triglycerides >110 mg/dl
  • Chylous ascites: Injury below diaphragm → chyle in peritoneal cavity
  • Lymphedema: Obstruction → protein-rich edema (non-pitting) in drained region
  • Virchow's node: Left supraclavicular node (at thoracic duct termination) — enlarges in GI/thoracic malignancies (Troisier's sign)
NB: After a fatty meal, thoracic duct lymph appears milky (chyle) due to chylomicrons. The duct contains valves that prevent backflow. Its walls have smooth muscle, allowing active propulsion of lymph.

4Mediastinum›

Definition, Boundaries & Mobility

  • The mediastinum is the central partition of the thoracic cavity between the two pleural sacs
  • Boundaries: Anterior = sternum | Posterior = vertebral column (T1–T12) | Lateral = mediastinal pleurae | Superior = thoracic inlet (1st rib + manubrium + T1 body) | Inferior = diaphragm
  • Mobile: Consists of hollow viscera in loose connective tissue and fat → can shift in disease (pneumothorax, effusion, atelectasis) → called "mediastinal shift" visible on CXR

Division by Transverse Thoracic Plane

  • Transverse thoracic plane: Passes through STERNAL ANGLE anteriorly (angle of Louis, junction of manubrium + body of sternum) and T4/T5 INTERVERTEBRAL DISC posteriorly
  • This plane is clinically important: level of arch of aorta, tracheal bifurcation, start of SVC, end of ascending aorta
  • Superior mediastinum: ABOVE the plane (thoracic inlet to sternal angle)
  • Inferior mediastinum: BELOW the plane → subdivided by pericardium into ANTERIOR (in front of pericardium), MIDDLE (pericardium + contents), POSTERIOR (behind pericardium)

Clinical — Mediastinal Conditions

  • Mediastinitis: Infection of mediastinum (e.g., from esophageal perforation, descending necrotizing fasciitis) — serious, life-threatening
  • Mediastinal shift: On CXR, trachea deviates TOWARD collapse (atelectasis, fibrosis) or AWAY from tension (tension pneumothorax, large effusion)
  • Superior vena cava syndrome (SVCS): Obstruction of SVC (usually by lung cancer/lymphoma) → facial/neck/arm swelling, distended neck veins, plethora
  • Mediastinal masses by location:
  • — Anterior: Thymic lesions (thymoma, thymic cyst), Teratoma/germ cell tumors, Thyroid/parathyroid, Terrible lymphoma [4 T's]
  • — Middle: Lymphadenopathy (lymphoma, sarcoidosis, TB), bronchogenic/pericardial cysts
  • — Posterior: Neurogenic tumors (neurofibroma, schwannoma), esophageal lesions, aortic aneurysm, extramedullary hematopoiesis
  • Widened mediastinum on CXR: Suspect aortic dissection (trauma), lymphadenopathy, or mediastinal mass
NB: The sternal angle is the most important surface landmark in thoracic medicine — it marks: (1) T4/T5 disc, (2) arch of aorta begins/ends, (3) tracheal bifurcation, (4) superior border of SVC, (5) junction of ascending and arch of aorta, (6) 2nd costal cartilage attachment (start of rib counting).

5Great Vessels of the Mediastinum›

Ascending Aorta

  • Begins: At aortic orifice of left ventricle, behind left half of sternum, lower border of 3rd left costal cartilage (OBS: ~aortic valve level)
  • Terminates: Upper border of right 2nd costal cartilage (= sternal angle) where it becomes the arch
  • Enclosed within the pericardium together with the pulmonary trunk (wrapped in visceral pericardium)
  • Branches — ONLY 2: Right coronary artery (from anterior aortic sinus) and Left coronary artery (from left posterior aortic sinus). Right posterior sinus = non-coronary sinus
  • Aortic sinuses of Valsalva: 3 dilatations just above aortic valve cusps; coronary arteries arise from these sinuses; they ensure coronary filling in diastole

Arch of Aorta

  • Begins: Upper border right 2nd costal cartilage (sternal angle)
  • Terminates: Left side of lower border of T4 → continues as descending thoracic aorta
  • Location: Superior mediastinum; lies entirely behind the manubrium sternum
  • Concavity (inferior surface) gives branches to: Right and left pulmonary arteries; ligamentum arteriosum (fibrous remnant of ductus arteriosus connects it to left pulmonary artery); deep cardiac plexus
  • 3 Branches from convexity (right to left) — Mnemonic BLS:
  • (1) Brachiocephalic (innominate) trunk — divides behind right sternoclavicular joint into right CCA + right subclavian
  • (2) Left common carotid artery — longest intrathoracic artery before neck
  • (3) Left subclavian artery
  • Important relations: Left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks around inferior surface of arch → ascending in tracheoesophageal groove; left vagus nerve crosses anterior surface of arch; left phrenic nerve crosses anterior surface lateral to vagus

Descending Thoracic Aorta

  • Begins: Left side of T4 (end of arch) | Terminates: T12, passes through aortic opening of diaphragm → becomes abdominal aorta
  • Initially on LEFT of vertebral column; gradually moves to front of vertebral column; at T12 lies anterior to vertebral column in the midline
  • Parietal branches (9 pairs): Posterior intercostal arteries 3rd–11th + subcostal artery (T12) + superior phrenic arteries
  • Visceral branches: Bronchial (left: 2 from aorta, right: from 3rd posterior intercostal), esophageal (4–5 small branches), pericardial, mediastinal

Superior Vena Cava (SVC)

  • Formed by: Union of right + left brachiocephalic veins at lower border of right 1st costal cartilage
  • Length: ~7 cm | No valves (relies on gravity + cardiac suction)
  • Terminates: Lower border of right 3rd costal cartilage → opens into right atrium
  • Lower half is within pericardium; upper half is extrapericardial
  • Azygos vein opens into posterior aspect of SVC just where it enters pericardium
  • Relations: Right = right phrenic nerve; Left = ascending aorta; Posterior = right pulmonary artery
  • SVC syndrome: ↑ venous pressure in head/neck/arms, facial plethora, dilated collateral veins on chest wall

Pulmonary Trunk & Brachiocephalic Veins

  • Pulmonary trunk: Arises from right ventricle (infundibulum/conus); passes upward and to the left; lies entirely within pericardium (with ascending aorta); divides below aortic arch level (left of midline, in front of left principal bronchus) into right + left pulmonary arteries
  • Right pulmonary artery: Longer; passes posterior to ascending aorta and SVC; enters hilum
  • Left pulmonary artery: Shorter; connected to aortic arch by ligamentum arteriosum
  • Right brachiocephalic vein: Short (2.5 cm); vertical course; formed behind right sternoclavicular joint (IJV + subclavian)
  • Left brachiocephalic vein: Longer (~6 cm); horizontal/oblique course across superior mediastinum from left to right; passes anterior to three arch branches; receives left superior intercostal vein (2nd–4th left posterior intercostal veins)

6Pleura & Trachea›

Pleura — Types

  • Visceral (pulmonary) pleura: Firmly adherent to lung surface; lines fissures; supplied by bronchial vessels; insensitive to pain
  • Parietal pleura: Lines the thoracic wall; 4 named parts below:
  • — Costal: lines ribs/intercostal spaces (supplied by intercostal nn.)
  • — Diaphragmatic: covers diaphragm
  • — Mediastinal: covers mediastinum laterally
  • — Cervical (dome/Sibson's fascia): covers apex, extends into neck
  • Pleural cavity: potential space containing thin serous fluid film — reduces friction during breathing

Pleural Recesses

  • Costodiaphragmatic recess: Most DEPENDENT part → fluid first collects here; opposite 8th–10th ribs along midaxillary line; extends from 8th rib anteriorly to 12th rib posteriorly
  • Costomediastinal recess: Anterior, between costal and mediastinal pleurae
  • LEFT costomediastinal recess is LARGER than right — because the cardiac notch in the left lung leaves more unfilled pleural space anteriorly

Nerve Supply of Pleura (HIGH-YIELD)

  • Costal pleura + peripheral diaphragmatic pleura: Intercostal nerves (T1–T12) → pain is WELL-LOCALIZED to chest/abdominal wall
  • Mediastinal + central diaphragmatic pleura: PHRENIC nerve (C3,4,5) → pain is REFERRED to shoulder tip / neck
  • VISCERAL pleura: no pain fibers — "painless"

Surface Markings of Pleural Reflection

  • Anteriorly: right and left pleural lines meet at the 2nd costal cartilage (sternal angle level)
  • Left pleura deviates laterally at 4th costal cartilage (cardiac notch) — creating the larger left costomediastinal recess
  • Inferiorly: 8th rib midclavicular line, 10th rib midaxillary line, 12th rib posteriorly
  • Lung borders are ~2 ribs ABOVE the pleural reflection (e.g., lung inferior border at 6th rib midclavicular)

Trachea — Key Facts

  • Length: 10–12 cm | Internal diameter: ~2.5 cm
  • Extends from: Lower border of cricoid cartilage (C6) → lower border of T4 (sternal angle = carina)
  • 16–20 C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings — maintain patent lumen
  • Posterior gap bridged by trachealis muscle (smooth muscle) + fibroelastic membrane — allows esophageal expansion
  • Relations in neck: Anterior = isthmus of thyroid (2nd–4th ring), infrahyoid muscles; Posterior = esophagus
  • Blood supply: Inferior thyroid artery (from thyrocervical trunk)
  • Nerve supply: Recurrent laryngeal nerve (motor to trachealis) + sympathetic chain
  • Carina: Median ridge at bifurcation (T4); highly sensitive — triggers intense cough reflex; widening on CXR suggests left atrial enlargement or subcarinal lymphadenopathy

Right Bronchus

  • Shorter: 1 inch (2.5 cm)
  • Wider: ~1.5 cm lumen
  • More vertical: ~20–25° from vertical
  • Foreign bodies go HERE
  • Upper lobe bronchus leaves before hilum
  • Right main bronchus = eparterial (above pulmonary artery)

Left Bronchus

  • Longer: 2 inches (5 cm)
  • Narrower: ~1.2 cm lumen
  • More oblique: ~45° from vertical
  • Passes UNDER arch of aorta and in front of descending aorta
  • All lobar bronchi arise AFTER entering hilum
  • Left main bronchus = hyparterial (below pulmonary artery)

Clinical Correlations

  • Pleurisy (Pleuritis): Parietal pleura inflammation → sharp pain aggravated by breathing + pleural friction rub on auscultation; disappears when effusion forms (fluid separates layers)
  • Pleural effusion: Fluid in pleural cavity; first collects in costodiaphragmatic recess; detected clinically when >300 ml; causes stony dullness to percussion
  • Pneumothorax: Air in pleural cavity → lung collapses. Tension pneumothorax is life-threatening — trachea deviates AWAY from side of tension
  • Thoracocentesis: Insert needle in 8th or 9th intercostal space at midaxillary line JUST ABOVE the rib below (avoids VAN bundle); enters costodiaphragmatic recess
  • Tracheotomy (Tracheostomy): Performed between 2nd–4th tracheal rings to bypass upper airway obstruction
Key Mnemonic: Lung inferior border: 6-8-10 (6th rib midclavicular, 8th rib midaxillary, 10th rib paravertebral). Pleural reflection: 8-10-12. The 2-rib difference = the costodiaphragmatic recess.

1Histology of Blood Vessels›

Basic Structure — 3 Tunics (General)

  • Tunica intima (innermost): Endothelium (simple squamous on basement membrane) + subendothelial layer of loose CT + internal elastic lamina (IEL)
  • Tunica media (middle): Smooth muscle cells ± elastic fibers; bounded externally by external elastic lamina (EEL)
  • Tunica adventitia (outermost): Dense irregular CT (collagen + elastic fibers); anchors vessel; contains vasa vasorum (small vessels nourishing large vessel walls) and autonomic nerves (vasomotor fibers)
  • General rule: as vessels get smaller, wall layers become thinner; capillaries have only endothelium + basal lamina

Elastic Arteries (Large/Conducting)

  • Also called "Windkessel vessels"
  • Media: 40–70 elastic lamellae (made of elastin); between = smooth muscle
  • IEL: NOT distinct — blends with elastic media
  • Adventitia: relatively thin with vasa vasorum
  • Examples: Aorta, pulmonary trunk, brachiocephalic, carotid, subclavian
  • Function: receive blood under high pressure; recoil maintains diastolic pressure (Windkessel effect)

Muscular Arteries (Medium/Distributing)

  • Also called "resistance arteries" (at arteriole level)
  • Media: 10–40 layers of smooth muscle (circular); few elastic fibers
  • IEL: DISTINCT — most prominent feature; wavy/fenestrated
  • EEL: present but less prominent
  • Examples: coronary, femoral, brachial, popliteal, splenic, palmar
  • Function: control blood distribution to organs by vasoconstriction/dilation

Arterioles (Terminal Arteries, < 100 μm)

  • Main regulators of peripheral vascular resistance (PVR) and blood pressure
  • Tunica media: 1–2 layers smooth muscle (circular)
  • IEL: may be present (thin) in larger arterioles; absent in terminal arterioles
  • Adventitia: thin; sparse CT
  • Autonomic innervation: rich sympathetic supply to smooth muscle → vasoconstriction (α1 receptors)
  • Metarterioles: Terminal branches connecting arterioles to capillaries; have discontinuous smooth muscle (precapillary sphincters)

Veins — Structural Features

  • General: Thinner walls than arteries of same diameter; larger + irregular lumen; adventitia = THICKEST layer (opposite to arteries where media is thickest)
  • Post-capillary venules (10–50 μm): Only endothelium + pericytes; site of inflammatory cell emigration (diapedesis); endothelial cells become cuboidal in high endothelial venules (HEV) of lymph nodes → lymphocyte homing
  • Muscular venules and small veins: Thin media (1–2 smooth muscle layers); occasional valves
  • Medium veins (2–10 mm): Adventitia = thickest; media = 3–5 smooth muscle layers; valves present — semilunar folds of intima (fibro-elastic core) in veins >1 mm; most abundant in lower limbs; absent in: portal vein, pulmonary veins, IVC, SVC, cerebral veins
  • Large veins (>10 mm): IVC, portal vein, pulmonary veins, subclavian, renal veins; adventitia contains longitudinal smooth muscle bundles (unique to large veins); media thin/absent in some

AV Anastomoses & Special Vessels

  • AV anastomoses (AV shunts): Direct connections between arterioles and venules, bypassing capillary bed; controlled by smooth muscle (glomus cells); sympathetically innervated
  • Sites: skin (fingers, toes, nose, lips, ears), thyroid, erectile tissue, GI submucosa
  • Functions: (1) Thermoregulation — cold: close (blood through capillaries → skin warm); heat: open (bypass capillaries → rapid heat loss); (2) Regulate blood pressure; (3) Control local blood flow
  • Sinusoids: Specialized wide capillaries with gaps; allow proteins, cells (RBCs) to pass freely; example: hepatic sinusoids lined by Kupffer cells (macrophages)
  • Vasa vasorum: "Vessels of vessels" — supply outer 2/3 of media and adventitia of large vessels; absent in smaller arteries (nourished by diffusion from lumen); damage → ischemic arteriopathy
Varicose Veins: Dilated, tortuous superficial veins (great saphenous system) due to incompetent valves → blood pools. Risk factors: prolonged standing, obesity, pregnancy, genetics. Complications: thrombophlebitis, venous ulcer, DVT. Treatment: compression stockings, sclerotherapy, stripping, laser ablation.
Key differences arteries vs veins: Arteries: round lumen, thick wall, media=thickest layer. Veins: irregular/collapsed lumen, thin wall, adventitia=thickest layer, may have valves. Arteries maintain shape after fixation; veins collapse. Arteries have prominent IEL (especially muscular arteries).

Nasal Cavity Histology — 3 Zones

  • Vestibule: Keratinized stratified squamous epithelium; sebaceous glands + vibrissae (hairs) filter large particles
  • Respiratory area (most of cavity): Pseudostratified columnar ciliated + goblet cells on thick vascular lamina propria (erectile tissue = nasal mucosa); warms and humidifies air

Clara (Club) Cells — Terminal Bronchioles

  • Structure: Dome-shaped apical cytoplasm; abundant RER + SER + mitochondria; electron-dense secretory granules
  • Functions (4):
  • (1) Produce surfactant-like substance (phospholipids)
  • (2) Secrete antimicrobial peptides + lysozyme + secretory IgA
  • (3) Cytochrome P450 enzymes → detoxify inhaled toxins
  • (4) Stem cells — replace damaged bronchiolar epithelium; can give rise to both ciliated cells and goblet cells

Type I Pneumocyte (Squamous Alveolar Cell)

  • Covers 95% of alveolar surface
  • Very flat/thin → minimal diffusion distance
  • Joined by occluding (tight) junctions → waterproof barrier
  • Very few organelles (sparse cytoplasm)
  • CANNOT divide (no mitosis)
  • Replaced by Type II cells after injury

Type II Pneumocyte (Great Alveolar Cell / Septal Cell)

  • More numerous (60% of alveolar cells) but only 3–5% of surface area
  • Cuboidal; microvilli on apical surface
  • Lamellar bodies = stored surfactant (dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine)
  • Surfactant ↓ surface tension → prevents alveolar collapse on expiration
  • Divide and replace Type I cells after lung injury
  • Also produce fibronectin, antiprotease

Blood-Air Barrier (4 layers, alveolus → capillary)

  • Layer 1: Surfactant layer (reduces surface tension)
  • Layer 2: Type I pneumocyte cytoplasm (very thin)
  • Layer 3: Fused basal laminae of Type I + capillary endothelial cells
  • Layer 4: Continuous capillary endothelium
  • Total thickness: 0.2–2.5 μm (thinnest at 0.2 μm for maximum gas exchange)

Alveolar Macrophages (Dust Cells)

  • Derived from monocytes; resident in alveoli and interalveolar septa
  • Dust cells: Engulf inhaled particles (carbon, silica, asbestos); accumulate → black lung
  • Heart failure cells: Engulf RBCs that have leaked from overloaded pulmonary capillaries → digest hemoglobin → contain hemosiderin (golden-brown granules); stain positive with Prussian blue; PATHOGNOMIC of chronic heart failure
  • Fate of macrophages: (1) Expectorated in mucus, (2) Carried to lymph nodes, (3) Removed by lymphatics

Alveolar Pores of Kohn & Alveolar Channels of Lambert

  • Pores of Kohn: Small pores (3–13 μm) in interalveolar septa → equalize air pressure between adjacent alveoli; allow collateral ventilation when a bronchiole is obstructed
  • Channels of Lambert: Connect terminal/respiratory bronchioles to alveoli — additional collateral airway pathway
Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (NRDS): Premature birth → Type II pneumocytes immature → insufficient surfactant → high surface tension → alveolar collapse (atelectasis) → respiratory failure. Treatment: artificial surfactant + corticosteroids antenatally to mature Type II cells.

1Cardiac Excitability & Rhythmicity›

Ventricular Action Potential – 5 Phases

  • RMP = –90 mV (stable)
  • Phase 0 (Rapid depolarization): fast voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ influx → overshoot to +20 mV
  • Phase 1 (Initial rapid repolarization): Na⁺ channels close + transient K⁺ efflux + Cl⁻ influx → 0 mV
  • Phase 2 (Plateau ~300 ms): L-type Ca²⁺ channels open → Ca²⁺ influx balances K⁺ efflux → ~0 mV
  • Phase 3 (Late repolarization): Ca²⁺ channels close + slow K⁺ channels open → K⁺ efflux → return to RMP
  • Phase 4: Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores ion gradients. Duration atrial AP: ~150 ms; ventricular + Purkinje: ~300 ms

Refractory Periods

  • ARP (Absolute): Phases 0+1+2+first half of phase 3 — zero excitability — coincides with WHOLE SYSTOLE + beginning of diastole
  • Application: Cardiac muscle CANNOT be tetanized. Long ARP provides recovery time → no fatigue
  • RRP (Relative): rest of phase 3 — stronger-than-normal stimulus needed — coincides with first half of diastole
  • Supernormal (Vulnerable) period: end of phase 3 — weaker stimulus can trigger AP → risk of VENTRICULAR FIBRILLATION (R-on-T)
  • Positive after-potential (after-hyperpolarization): just after RMP — excitability slightly below normal (more K⁺ channels remain open)

Relation: Mechanical Response & Action Potential

  • Mechanical response lasts 1.5× longer than AP
  • Mechanical response comes 0.02 sec AFTER the electrical response
  • Systole (caused by Ca²⁺ influx): starts just after depolarization, reaches maximum at end of plateau
  • Diastole (caused by Ca²⁺ uptake): starts with rapid repolarization phase, lasts ~2× the period of repolarization

Rhythmicity (Automaticity)

  • SA node: 90–110/min (dominant pacemaker — fastest drives all) | AV node: 60/min | Purkinje: 30/min | Ventricles: 20–40/min
  • Idioventricular rhythm: 20–40/min — occurs if SA + AV nodes both damaged or over-inhibited by vagus
  • Diastolic prepotential of SA node: spontaneous slow depolarization due to ↓ K⁺ efflux + Na⁺ leakage (If) + T-type Ca²⁺ channels → reaches firing level (–40 mV) → L-type Ca²⁺ channels open
  • Slope of prepotential = key determinant: steeper slope = faster HR

Factors Affecting Excitability

  • Autonomic NS: Vagal stimulation → ↓ excitability | Sympathetic → ↑ excitability
  • Thyroxine: ↑ excitability (↑ metabolism)
  • Drugs: Caffeine ↑ excitability | Digitalis ↓ excitability (vagomimetic)
  • Ischemia, hypoxia, bacterial toxins: ↓ excitability

2Heart Rate›

Normal Values

  • Newborn: 90–120/min (no vagal tone) | Adult: 65–90/min (avg 75/min)
  • Females > males | Athletes at rest: bradycardia (↑ vagal tone) | Sleep: ↓ HR | Exercise/emotion/pregnancy: ↑ HR

Supraspinal Centers (Nervous Regulation)

  • Cardiovascular Centers in medulla: VCC (vasomotor/cardiac acceleratory center) + CIC (cardiac inhibitory center)
  • Cerebral cortex: conditioned reflexes + voluntary control in some individuals
  • Hypothalamus + limbic system: emotions → tachycardia (most emotions); severe emotions → bradycardia
  • Respiratory center: inspiration → inhibits CIC → ↓ vagal tone → ↑ HR

Key Cardiovascular Reflexes

  • Marey's Reflex: ↑ ABP → baroreceptors (carotid sinus + aortic arch) → CIC → ↑ vagal tone → ↓ HR. Reverse = ↓ ABP → ↑ HR
  • Bainbridge Reflex: ↑ right atrial pressure → tachycardia-producing atrial receptors → VCC → tachycardia. NB: some authors = direct SA node stretch (Bainbridge effect)
  • Respiratory Sinus Arrhythmia: Inspiration → lung stretch receptors inhibit CIC → ↓ vagal tone → ↑ HR. Expiration → ↓ HR. Normal in young/children
  • Left Ventricular Reflexes: Distention of left ventricle → baroreceptors → bradycardia + apnea + VD + hypotension
  • Bezold-Jarisch Reflex: Injection of serotonin/veratridine/capsaicin into coronary arteries → bradycardia + VD + hypotension (coronary chemo-reflex)
  • Pulmonary reflexes: Stimulation of pulmonary vessel baroreceptors → apnea + bradycardia + VD
  • Alam-Smirk reflex: Voluntary skeletal muscle contraction → ↑ HR
  • Skin/Viscera: Cold + moderate pain → ↑ HR | Severe visceral pain → ↓ HR (bradycardia)
  • Oculocardiac reflex: Pressure on eyeball → trigeminal → vagus → bradycardia (important during eye surgery)

Chemical Regulation of HR

  • Adrenaline (small dose): directly ↑ HR (β1 on SA node)
  • Adrenaline (large dose): ↑↑ ABP → Marey → ↓ HR
  • Noradrenaline: potent VC → ↑ ABP → Marey → reflex bradycardia
  • Thyroxine: (1) Direct SA node stimulation, (2) ↑ metabolism → metabolic VD → ↑ VR → Bainbridge → tachycardia
  • Atropine: Blocks M receptors → removes vagal brake → tachycardia (up to 120–130/min)
  • Histamine: potent VD → ↓ ABP → Marey → tachycardia
  • Bile salts: Inhibit SA node + stimulate CIC → bradycardia (explains jaundice bradycardia)
  • Sympathomimetic drugs: tachycardia | Parasympathomimetic drugs: bradycardia
  • Moderate hypoxia: ↑ HR (peripheral chemoreceptors → VCC) | Severe hypoxia: ↓ HR (SA node depression)
  • Moderate hypercapnia/acidosis: ↑ HR (chemoreceptors → VCC) | Severe: ↓ HR (SA node paralysis)

Direct SA Node Factors

  • Temperature: Each 1°C rise → ↑ ~10 beats/min (direct metabolic effect on SA node). Cold → sympathetic → tachycardia
  • Mechanical (Bainbridge effect): Right atrial distention directly excites SA node → tachycardia
  • ANP: Atrial stretch → ANP release → ↓ vasopressin, ↓ renin → ↓ Ang II formation
ECG: HR = 60 / R-R interval (sec) = 300 / number of large squares between R waves.
Respiratory sinus arrhythmia is NORMAL in children and young adults — absence indicates autonomic neuropathy.

3Venous, Capillary & Coronary Circulation›

Venous Circulation

  • Veins = "capacitance vessels" — contain ~65% of total blood volume; can constrict/dilate
  • Functions: (1) Drain tissues to heart, (2) Blood reservoir (65% of volume at low pressure), (3) Venous pump — propel blood to heart, (4) Regulate COP
  • CVP: Normal 0–4 mmHg | ↑ in heart failure (→ 20–30 mmHg), venoconstriction, ↑ blood volume | ↓ in hemorrhage (→ –3 to –5 mmHg)
  • Peripheral venous pressure: 4–7 mmHg GREATER than CVP (due to: intra-abdominal pressure on abdominal veins, angulation at 1st rib, collapsed neck veins)
  • HIP (Hydrostatic Indifferent Point): 5–7 cm below diaphragm; VP = constant 10–11 mmHg regardless of posture. During swimming: HIP shifts to right atrium → no orthostatic hypotension
  • Orthostatic hypotension: Lying → standing: blood pools in lower limbs → ↓ VR → ↓ CVP → ↓ COP → ↓ ABP → dizziness/fainting

Alternation Phenomenon (Active vs Inactive Capillaries)

  • Only 10% of capillaries open at rest → rotate every few seconds (alternation phenomenon)
  • Mechanism: ↓ O₂ + ↑ CO₂ + ↑ H⁺ around closed capillaries → dilate precapillary sphincters → blood flows in → removes metabolites → sphincters close again
  • In active tissues: metabolites dilate ALL sphincters → all capillaries open → active hyperemia

Starling Forces — Capillary Hemodynamics

  • Filtering forces (OUT): Capillary hydrostatic pressure (arteriolar 30–40, venular 10–15 mmHg) + Interstitial negative pressure (–3 mmHg = suction) + ISF oncotic pressure (8 mmHg)
  • Anti-filtering force (IN): Plasma colloid oncotic pressure = 28 mmHg (mainly albumin)
  • Net filtration arteriolar end: 13 mmHg outward | Net reabsorption venular end: 7 mmHg inward
  • Small amount (~20%) not reabsorbed → removed by lymphatics
  • Safety factors against edema (total = 17 mmHg): (1) Negative ISF pressure: 5 mmHg, (2) ↑ Lymph flow: 7 mmHg, (3) Protein washout from ISF: 5 mmHg

Coronary Blood Flow — Mechanical Changes During Cardiac Cycle

  • Normal CBF at rest: 250 ml/min (5% COP). Right ventricle: 2/3 of left ventricle blood flow
  • O₂ extraction: Arterial = 19 ml%; Coronary venous = 5 ml%; Extracted = 14 ml O₂/100 ml blood (vs 5 ml in other tissues)
  • Left coronary artery — changes during cardiac cycle:
  • (1) Isometric contraction: CBF MARKEDLY DECREASED (mechanical compression + low aortic P)
  • (2) Maximum ejection: CBF gradually ↑ (aortic P rises)
  • (3) Reduced ejection: CBF ↓ (aortic P falls)
  • (4) Isometric relaxation: MAXIMUM CBF (aortic P still relatively high + myocardium relaxes)
  • (5) Then gradually decreases as aortic P falls during diastole
  • Right coronary artery: similar but milder changes (thinner RV wall, weaker contraction); max inflow also in early diastole
  • Clinical: CBF mainly during diastole. Tachycardia → shortens diastole → ↓ CBF → angina (paroxysmal tachycardia). ↓ Aortic pressure (shock, aortic stenosis) → ↓ CBF

Coronary Blood Flow — Regulation

  • Neural (indirect dominant): Sympathetic → ↑ HR + ↑ contractility → metabolites → VD. Parasympathetic → ↓ HR + ↓ contractility → ↓ CBF indirectly
  • Chemical (autoregulation): ↑ cardiac work → hypoxia + metabolites → VD → ↑ CBF; independent of neural control (works in transplanted heart)
  • Coronary DILATORS: thyroxine, adrenaline, ADP, caffeine, alcohol, nitrites, EDRF (NO)
  • Coronary CONSTRICTORS: ADH (vasopressin), angiotensin II
Key Facts: Normal blood: contains 19 ml O₂% (arterial); coronary venous blood: 5 ml O₂%. Heart extracts 14 ml O₂/100 ml (HIGHEST extraction in body). Therefore: the ONLY way to increase myocardial O₂ delivery = increase CBF (cannot increase extraction).

Why is IPP Negative? (Two opposing forces)

  • Lung recoil tendency (inward): At FRC (2.5 L), lung is distended above its relaxation volume (1 L) → wants to collapse: elastic fibers = 1/3; surface tension of alveolar fluid = 2/3
  • Chest wall expansion tendency (outward): At FRC (2.5 L), chest is compressed below its relaxation volume (5 L) → wants to expand
  • Importance of IPP: (1) Keeps lungs expanded, (2) Helps venous + lymph return to heart, (3) Is a measure of lung elasticity

Causes of POSITIVE IPP

  • Forced expiration (talking, crying) → bulging neck veins (↓ venous return)
  • Valsalva maneuver (forced expiration against closed glottis): labor, severe straining
  • Tension pneumothorax
  • Abnormal fluid in pleural sac: Hydrothorax (serous fluid), Hemothorax (blood), Pyothorax (pus), Chylothorax (lymph)

Dead Space

  • Anatomical DS: conducting airways (nose → terminal bronchioles) = 150 ml
  • Alveolar DS: non-functioning alveoli = normally 0 | Physiological DS: anatomical + alveolar = 150 ml
  • Functions of DS:
  • (1) Constitutes the conducting part of respiratory tract
  • (2) Moistens and warms inspired air before reaching alveoli
  • (3) Protective: nasal hairs trap particles >10 μm; mucus traps 2–6 μm; phagocytes remove 0.3–2 μm; <0.3 μm stay in alveoli
  • (4) Prevents sharp changes in gas pressures of inspired + alveolar air
  • (5) Creates difference between total pulmonary ventilation and effective alveolar ventilation
  • Clinical example: Heart failure RR=30, TV=200ml: total ventilation=6L (normal) but effective VA=(200–150)×30=1.5L (↓↓) → hypoxia!
  • Another example: Breathing through a long tube → ↑ DS → hypoxia

Lung Compliance

  • Definition: Change in lung volume per unit change in transpulmonary pressure (∆V/∆P) = ease of lung expansion
  • Total lung compliance: ~220 ml/cm H₂O (or 0.22 L/cm H₂O)
  • Combined lung-thorax compliance: ~130 ml/cm H₂O (about half of lungs alone, due to chest wall elastic elements)
  • Factors ↑ compliance: Surfactant (↓ surface tension → ↑ compliance)
  • Factors ↓ compliance: Pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary edema, congestion, atelectasis
  • Compliance is INVERSELY correlated with elasticity
Muller maneuver = forced inspiration against closed glottis → IAP –80 mmHg (decreases cardiac preload → used to test cardiac function).
Valsalva maneuver = forced expiration against closed glottis → IPP +40 mmHg → ↓ VR → bulging neck veins + transient ↑ then ↓ ABP.

5Pulmonary Circulation›

Characteristic Features of Pulmonary Circulation

  • HIGH flow, LOW resistance, LOW pressure system
  • PVR = 10–15× LOWER than SVR (but same blood flow through both)
  • Right ventricular wall = 30% of LV thickness (less work needed)
  • Pulmonary arteries = 30% of aortic wall thickness → more compliant
  • Pressure gradient: only 7 mmHg (vs 90 mmHg systemic) for same blood flow
  • Blood volume in pulmonary vessels: ~1 liter total; ~100 ml in capillaries
  • Blood velocity: 0.4 m/sec in root of pulmonary artery (same as aorta)
  • Transit time in capillary: 0.75 sec at rest; 0.3 sec during exercise (still enough for full gas exchange)

Physiologic Shunt

  • Anastomoses between bronchial capillaries and pulmonary capillaries/veins → some bronchial blood enters pulmonary veins (bypasses RV)
  • Also: some coronary blood flows into left heart chambers
  • Result: PO₂ in alveoli = 100 mmHg BUT PO₂ in systemic arterial blood = 95 mmHg (A–aO₂ gradient = 5 mmHg)
  • The lungs receive 101% of COP (100% from RV via PA + 1% from LV via bronchial arteries)

Zones of Pulmonary Blood Flow (West Zones)

  • Zone I (apex): alveolar P > capillary P → NO blood flow. Absent in normal healthy upright lungs. Appears in: positive pressure breathing (alveolar P reaches +10 mmHg) + hypovolemic shock
  • Zone II (middle): blood flows only during systole (when systolic capillary P > alveolar P); diastolic P < alveolar P
  • Zone III (base): continuous blood flow (capillary P always > alveolar P)
  • Normal upright lung: Zone II at apex, Zone III at base. In recumbent: all Zone III
  • During exercise: PBF ↑ in all zones → new capillaries open at apex (closed at rest)

Factors Controlling PBF

  • Sympathetic: ↓ PBF by 30% (α-receptor VC). Parasympathetic: ↑ PBF (muscarinic VD)
  • Local hypoxia (bronchial obstruction): VC of local pulmonary vessels → diverts blood to well-ventilated areas (UNIQUE — opposite to systemic circulation!)
  • Generalized hypoxia: generalized VC → ↑ pulmonary pressure → opens closed capillaries → ↑ surface area for gas exchange
  • Constrictors: catecholamines, dopamine, phenylephrine, Ang II, thromboxane A₂, PGF₂α
  • Dilators: ACh, histamine, bradykinin, Ca²⁺ channel blockers, PGI₂ (prostacyclin), ↑ O₂, NO
  • Inspiration: ↑ PBF (↓ IPP → ↑ VR + passive widening of pulmonary vessels)
  • Valsalva: ↑ IPP → compresses pulmonary vessels → ↓ PBF

Functions of Pulmonary Circulation

  • (1) Gas exchange: Main function — efficient gas exchange depends on good V/Q matching (both ventilation and perfusion higher at base than apex, but blood flow more affected by gravity)
  • (2) Defensive function: Mucociliary clearance + immune cells
  • (3) Blood reservoir: ~500 ml (10% of total blood volume) — stabilizes LV stroke volume when going from supine to standing
  • (4) Metabolic function: ACE in pulmonary endothelial cells converts Ang I → Ang II (explains dry cough with ACE inhibitors — bradykinin accumulates)
  • (5) Filters blood: Acts as sieve for thrombi, air emboli, fat globules — prevents systemic embolism

Regional Distribution: V/Q Ratio

  • Normal mean V/Q = 0.8 (VA 4.2 L / PBF 5.25 L)
  • Apex (Zone I/II): V/Q = 3 — more ventilated than perfused → HIGH PO₂, LOW PCO₂
  • Clinical (TB at apex): High PO₂ + low blood flow at apex = ideal environment for aerobic M. tuberculosis → explains apical location of TB lesions
  • Base (Zone III): V/Q lower than average — better perfused than ventilated
  • Both air flow and blood flow higher at base; blood flow MORE affected by gravity than air flow

1Ketone Bodies Metabolism›

Types, Properties & Fuel Value

  • 3 types: Acetoacetic acid (acetoacetate), β-Hydroxybutyric acid (β-hydroxybutyrate), Acetone
  • Water-soluble; freely diffuse between intracellular/extracellular; cross blood-brain barrier
  • Normal blood level: ~1 mg/dl (mainly β-hydroxybutyrate + acetoacetate)
  • Caloric value: Acetoacetate + β-hydroxybutyrate yield 4 kcal/g (same as glucose); preferred by heart
  • β-hydroxybutyrate: most abundant in plasma; NOT a ketone chemically (no C=O) but grouped with ketones
  • Neonatal brain uses them as substrates for cerebral lipid synthesis

Ketogenesis — Steps (in Liver Mitochondria)

  • Site: Liver MITOCHONDRIA only
  • Step 1: 2 Acetyl-CoA → Acetoacetyl-CoA (thiolase)
  • Step 2: Acetoacetyl-CoA + Acetyl-CoA → HMG-CoA (mitochondrial HMG-CoA synthase — RATE LIMITING)
  • Step 3: HMG-CoA → Acetoacetate + Acetyl-CoA (HMG-CoA lyase)
  • Step 4: Acetoacetate → β-hydroxybutyrate (β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase; requires NADH)
  • Step 5: Acetoacetate → Acetone + CO₂ (spontaneous decarboxylation; causes fruity breath in DKA)

Regulation of Ketogenesis

  • Stimulated by: Fasting, starvation, high fat/low carb diet, anti-insulin hormones (glucagon, cortisol, GH, catecholamines), uncontrolled DM type 1
  • Inhibited by: Insulin (via malonyl-CoA → inhibits CPT-I → blocks FA entry into mitochondria), glucose feeding, refeeding
  • Key regulatory molecule: Malonyl-CoA — rises after carbohydrate feeding → potently inhibits CPT-I (carnitine palmitoyl transferase I) → ↓ FA oxidation → ↓ acetyl-CoA → ↓ ketogenesis

Ketolysis — Steps (in Extrahepatic Tissues)

  • Site: Mitochondria of extrahepatic tissues: HEART (preferred fuel), brain, renal cortex, skeletal muscle
  • Liver CANNOT use ketone bodies (lacks succinyl-CoA transferase = CoA transferase)
  • Step 1: β-hydroxybutyrate → Acetoacetate (β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase)
  • Step 2: Acetoacetate + Succinyl-CoA → Acetoacetyl-CoA + Succinate (succinyl-CoA transferase = CoA transferase = KEY ENZYME)
  • Step 3: Acetoacetyl-CoA → 2 Acetyl-CoA (thiolase)
  • Step 4: 2 Acetyl-CoA → TCA cycle → CO₂ + H₂O + ATP
  • Requires succinyl-CoA from TCA cycle → links ketolysis to Krebs cycle

Ketosis & Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

  • Definition: Blood ketones >1 mg/dl + ketonemia + ketonuria + ketonemia
  • Causes: Prolonged fasting (>18h), high fat/low carb diet, uncontrolled DM type 1, severe exercise, alcoholism, pregnancy
  • Complications: Metabolic acidosis → blood pH ↓ → ↓ RR (Kussmaul breathing) → electrolyte disturbances (↑K⁺ externally but total body K⁺ ↓) → coma → death
  • Fruity/acetone breath in DKA from exhaled acetone
  • DKA treatment: Insulin + IV fluids + K⁺ supplementation + monitor glucose
  • Starvation ketosis: milder; ketones provide ~2/3 of brain energy by 3 weeks of fasting → spares muscle protein

Why Liver Doesn't Use Ketones

  • Lacks succinyl-CoA transferase (CoA transferase)
  • Hepatocytes lack 3-oxoacid CoA-transferase
  • Instead: liver ketone bodies are exported to extrahepatic tissues
  • This is metabolic logic: liver MAKES and EXPORTS, others USE

Why Heart PREFERS Ketones

  • Oxidized more rapidly than glucose (no regulatory steps)
  • Heart has high succinyl-CoA transferase activity
  • High fatty acid oxidation capacity + HMG-CoA lyase absent in heart
  • Heart extracts >60% of ketones from blood (highest extraction of any organ)
Key facts: β-hydroxybutyrate: β-HB dehydrogenase (requires NADH); acetoacetate: spontaneous decarboxylation → acetone (fruity breath). The heart and renal cortex use ketones even when glucose is available. Ketonuria detected by Rothera's test (nitroprusside) — detects acetoacetate + acetone but NOT β-hydroxybutyrate.

2Cholesterol Metabolism›

Sources & Distribution

  • Dietary (exogenous): 300–500 mg/day; found in liver, kidney, brain, red meat, egg yolk, butter, cheese
  • Endogenous: synthesized in ALL nucleated cells; liver is main source for plasma cholesterol (~1 g/day)
  • Highest concentrations in: CNS (structural component of myelin), liver, adrenal cortex, gonads, intestine
  • Body total: ~140 g of cholesterol; plasma = 150–220 mg/dl; 70% esterified, 30% free

Biosynthesis

  • Site: Cytoplasm — microsomal (ER) + cytosolic fractions of ALL nucleated cells
  • Starting material: Acetyl-CoA (from glucose, fatty acids, ketogenic amino acids)
  • Steps: 2 Acetyl-CoA → Acetoacetyl-CoA → (+acetyl-CoA) → HMG-CoA → (HMG-CoA reductase + NADPH) → Mevalonate → Isopentenyl pyrophosphate → Squalene (C₃₀) → Lanosterol → Cholesterol (C₂₇)
  • Rate-limiting step: HMG-CoA → Mevalonate (by HMG-CoA REDUCTASE)

HMG-CoA Reductase — Regulation (KEY)

  • Active form = dephosphorylated | Inactive form = phosphorylated
  • ↑ Activity (activated by): Insulin (dephosphorylation), carbohydrate feeding, tri-iodothyronine (T3), cortisol
  • ↓ Activity (inhibited by): Glucagon (phosphorylation), fasting, mevalonate, cholesterol itself (feedback inhibition)
  • Statins (lovastatin, atorvastatin): Competitive inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase → ↓ cholesterol synthesis → ↑ LDL receptors on liver → ↓ blood LDL (drug of choice for hypercholesterolemia)
  • NB: Thyroid hormones: T3 stimulates HMG-CoA reductase BUT MORE importantly stimulates 7α-hydroxylase (bile acid synthesis) → net effect = ↓ blood cholesterol. Hypothyroidism: ↓ 7α-hydroxylase → ↑ blood cholesterol

Blood Cholesterol & Transport

  • Normal: 150–220 mg/dl | Desirable: <200 mg/dl | Borderline: 200–239 mg/dl | High: ≥240 mg/dl
  • Atherogenic index (LDL/HDL ratio): >4/1 = high risk for atherosclerosis
  • LDL (75% of cholesterol): Atherogenic — delivers cholesterol to peripheral tissues; taken up by LDL receptors (Apo B100)
  • HDL (25% of cholesterol): Anti-atherogenic — reverse cholesterol transport back to liver
  • Estrogen → ↑ LDL receptors → ↓ blood LDL (explains lower CVD risk in premenopausal females)
  • PUFA (polyunsaturated fatty acids): ↑ LDL receptors → ↓ blood cholesterol (mechanism of olive oil/fish oil benefits)
  • LCAT (lecithin-cholesterol acyl transferase): In plasma (associated with HDL) — esterifies free cholesterol. ACAT = in tissues

Functions of Cholesterol

  • (1) Essential component of cell membranes — regulates fluidity
  • (2) Precursor of ALL steroid hormones: androgens, estrogens, progesterone, glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids
  • (3) Precursor of Vitamin D₃: cholesterol → 7-dehydrocholesterol (UV light) → cholecalciferol (Vit D₃) → in liver (25-OH-D₃) → in kidney (1,25-(OH)₂-D₃ = active calcitriol)
  • (4) Precursor of bile acids (see below)
  • (5) Required for transport of lipids as lipoproteins

Bile Acids & Enterohepatic Circulation

  • Primary bile acids: Cholic acid + Chenodeoxycholic acid (formed in liver from cholesterol)
  • Key enzyme: 7α-hydroxylase (rate-limiting for bile acid synthesis); feedback-inhibited by bile acids; stimulated by thyroxine, cholestyramine
  • Conjugated bile acids: With glycine → glycocholic acid (3:1 ratio) | With taurine → taurocholic acid (1:1 ratio)
  • Secondary bile acids (formed in intestine by bacteria): Deoxycholic acid (from cholic) + Lithocholic acid (from chenodeoxycholic)
  • Enterohepatic circulation: 95% of bile acids reabsorbed from terminal ileum → portal blood → liver → re-secreted into bile. Total pool = 3–5 g; circulates 6–10×/day
  • Daily loss (5%): Excreted mainly as lithocholic acid in feces (least soluble → least absorbed)
  • Daily synthesis: 500 mg/day to replace lost 5%
  • In stool: 500 mg/day cholesterol + coprostanol (formed from cholesterol by bacterial reduction in large intestine)
  • Total daily excretion of cholesterol (all forms): ~1 g/day (= amount synthesized → balance maintained)

Causes of Hypercholesterolemia (>240 mg/dl)

  • High saturated fat + cholesterol diet | Diabetes mellitus | Hypothyroidism (most important endocrine cause)
  • Obesity | Nephrotic syndrome (↑ VLDL synthesis) | Obstructive jaundice (↓ enterohepatic circulation)
  • Cigarette smoking + coffee | Familial hypercholesterolemia (Type IIa): LDL receptor defect → markedly ↑ LDL
  • Causes of HYPOCHOLESTEROLEMIA: Severe liver disease, malnutrition, malabsorption, hyperthyroidism, anemia
Key Numbers: Normal cholesterol 150–220 mg/dl (desirable <200). Atherogenic index LDL/HDL >4/1 = risk. Enterohepatic circulation: bile acids circulate 6–10×/day; 5% lost in feces = 500 mg/day = replaced by de novo synthesis. Coprostanol = bacterial reduction of cholesterol in colon.

  • (2) Reservoir for Apo CII and Apo E: transfers these apos to chylomicrons and VLDL (essential for their metabolism)
  • (3) Anti-atherogenic protection: Contains paraoxonase (PON1) + PAF-acetyl hydrolase → inhibit LDL oxidation → prevent foam cell formation
  • LCAT: Found in plasma (associated with HDL); esterifies free cholesterol to CE. Apo AI is its activator
  • CETP (= Apo D): Transfers CE from HDL to IDL/LDL → facilitates reverse CE transport pathway
  • Factors ↑ HDL: Aerobic exercise, moderate alcohol, estrogen, PUFA, niacin | ↓ HDL: Smoking, obesity, DM type 2, hypertriglyceridemia

Oxidized LDL & Atherosclerosis

  • LDL becomes oxidized (Ox-LDL) when exposed to free radicals (smoking, hyperglycemia, inflammation)
  • Ox-LDL: NOT recognized by LDL receptor → taken up by MACROPHAGE SCAVENGER RECEPTORS (unregulated uptake)

  • HDL protects against atherosclerosis by: reverse transport + PON1 enzyme inhibits LDL oxidation
  • Factors promoting LDL oxidation: Smoking, uncontrolled DM, metabolic syndrome, trans-fatty acids, hypertension
