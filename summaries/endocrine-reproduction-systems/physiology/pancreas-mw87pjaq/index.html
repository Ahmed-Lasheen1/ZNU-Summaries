Glucagon

In shortGlucagon is the hyperglycemic hormone: it raises blood glucose through the liver and mobilizes fat, acting through cAMP.

Definition

Glucagon is a hyperglycemic polypeptide hormone made of a chain of 29 amino acids. It binds cell membrane receptors and increases the level of cyclic AMP.

Functions

  • Hyperglycemia by two mechanisms: glycogenolysis (breakdown of liver glycogen to blood glucose) and gluconeogenesis (formation of glucose from fatty acids and amino acids in the liver).
  • Lipolysis and ketogenesis: activates adipose cell lipase, increasing the fatty acids available for energy; it also inhibits triglyceride storage in the liver.
  • Calorigenic action: due to increased hepatic deamination of amino acids, which requires thyroxine and cortisol.
  • Heart: in high concentration it enhances cardiac contractility (positive inotropic action) through increased myocardial cyclic AMP.
  • Stimulates secretion of insulin, growth hormone and somatostatin (hypoglycemic hormones that maintain blood glucose homeostasis).
  • No action on muscle glycogen or peripheral utilization of glucose; it has no receptors on skeletal muscle.
Don't confuse

Glycogenolysis is an immediate action of short duration. Gluconeogenesis is a delayed response that is long acting.

Control of glucagon secretion

Increase glucagon

  • Fall in blood glucose
  • High amino acids (alanine, arginine) after a protein meal
  • Exhaustive exercise
  • CCK-PZ and gastrin
  • Sympathetic β-adrenoceptors
  • Vagal stimulation and acetylcholine

Decrease glucagon

  • Rising blood glucose
  • Somatostatin and insulin
  • Secretin
  • Sympathetic α-adrenoceptors (β effect predominates)

A protein meal gives a greater glucagon response than an intravenous infusion of amino acids, because of the GIT hormones.

Insulin

In shortInsulin is the hypoglycemic hormone; it acts through a tyrosine-kinase type receptor to promote storage and utilization of glucose, fat and protein.

Definition

Insulin is a hypoglycemic polypeptide hormone composed of two chains (51 amino acids) connected by disulfide linkages. Its lack causes hyperglycemia and glucosuria (diabetes mellitus).

Insulin receptor

The receptor is a glycoprotein in the cell membrane of most body cells, made of 4 subunits: 2 alpha (outside the membrane) and 2 beta (penetrating into the cytoplasm).

  1. Insulin binds

    Insulin binds the 2 alpha subunits.

  2. Autophosphorylation

    The 2 beta subunits phosphorylate themselves in the cytoplasm.

  3. Local protein kinase

    The beta subunits act as a local protein kinase that activates other cytosol enzymes.

Actions

  • Increases glucose utilization in liver and muscle by helping its phosphorylation to glucose-6-phosphate (by glucokinase in the liver).
  • Stimulates glycogen formation in the liver (glycogenesis) and inhibits gluconeogenesis.
  • Inhibits lipolysis and stimulates lipogenesis.
  • Increases uptake of amino acids (arginine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, tyrosine) and muscle protein formation.
  • Increases cellular uptake of Mg++, K+ and phosphate by muscles.
  • Increases cell membrane permeability to glucose, allowing rapid entry of glucose into cells.

Control of insulin secretion

A rise in blood glucose to 2 to 3 times the normal fasting level (about 90 mg%, range 70 to 110 mg%) markedly increases insulin secretion in two steps:

First peak

  • Within 3 to 5 minutes; output rises to 10 times the resting value
  • Due to immediate release of preformed insulin from β cells
  • Followed by a sudden drop in 5 to 10 minutes

Delayed rise

  • Starts after 15 to 20 minutes; plateau for 2 to 3 hours
  • Due to new insulin formation by activation of the enzyme system

Stimulate insulin

  • Rising blood glucose
  • Enteroglucagon (released by the upper GIT wall during glucose absorption)
  • Glucagon (potent, acts directly on β cells)
  • Vagal stimulation
  • Gastrin, secretin, CCK-PZ and gastric inhibitory peptide
  • Arginine and lysine

Inhibit insulin

  • Falling blood glucose
  • Sympathetic stimulation (via alpha receptors)
  • Somatostatin

Insulin deficiency (diabetes mellitus)

Clinical relevance

Insulin deficiency causes hyperglycemia, glucosuria, osmotic diuresis (polyuria) leading to dehydration, and wasting of fats and muscles leading to ketosis.

Somatostatin and gastrin

Both are secreted from the δ cells of the islets (10% of islet cells).

Somatostatin

  • Growth hormone release inhibiting factor (hypothalamus, pancreatic islets)
  • Suppresses release of insulin, growth hormone, prolactin and glucagon
  • Physiological role is uncertain

Gastrin

  • Polypeptide from G cells of the stomach antrum and from the pancreatic islets
  • Stimulates secretion and motility of the stomach

Check yourself

Why does glucagon not affect skeletal muscle glycogen?

Glucagon has no receptors on skeletal muscle, so it has no action on muscle glycogen or peripheral glucose utilization.

What causes the first peak of insulin secretion after a glucose rise?

Immediate release of preformed insulin from the β cells, within 3 to 5 minutes.

Which insulin receptor subunits autophosphorylate after insulin binds?

The 2 beta subunits, after insulin binds the 2 alpha subunits; they then act as a local protein kinase.

Key Points / Quick Review

Islet cells

  • α 25% glucagon; β 60% insulin; δ 10% somatostatin and gastrin; PP 5% pancreatic polypeptide.

Glucagon

  • 29 amino acids, acts via cAMP; hyperglycemic.
  • Glycogenolysis is immediate and short; gluconeogenesis is delayed and long.
  • Lipolysis and ketogenesis; no receptors on skeletal muscle.
  • Stimulated by low glucose, amino acids, exercise, CCK-PZ, gastrin; inhibited by insulin, somatostatin, secretin.

Insulin

  • Receptor: 2 alpha plus 2 beta subunits; beta autophosphorylation.
  • Promotes glycogenesis, lipogenesis, protein synthesis; inhibits gluconeogenesis and lipolysis.
  • Biphasic secretion: preformed insulin first, new insulin later.
  • GLUT4 is the insulin-responsive transporter.

Deficiency and δ-cell hormones

  • Hyperglycemia, glucosuria, polyuria, dehydration, ketosis.
  • Somatostatin suppresses insulin, GH, prolactin and glucagon; gastrin stimulates stomach secretion and motility.
