Anatomy of male ducts, spermatic cord & accessory glands

Source: pages 12 to 17

Describe the lobes of the prostate gland.

The prostate has five lobes: anterior, posterior, median and two lateral.

  • Anterior lobe: in front of the urethra.
  • Posterior lobe.
  • Median lobe: between the urethra and the two ejaculatory ducts.
  • Two lateral lobes (right and left): one on each side of the urethra.
  • About the age of fifty, the median lobe may enlarge and press on the sphincter vesicae at the bladder neck, obstructing the flow of urine.

Key words five lobes, median lobe, ejaculatory ducts, bladder neck

Anatomy of the uterus & vagina

Source: pages 20 to 28

Describe the peritoneal covering of the uterus.

The peritoneum covers the fundus and body of the uterus and forms two pouches: the utero-vesical pouch in front and the recto-vaginal (Douglas) pouch behind.

  • In front: from the anterior surface of the body, at the isthmus, it is reflected onto the superior surface of the urinary bladder, forming the utero-vesical pouch.
  • Behind: it covers the posterior surface of the body, the cervix and the upper third of the vagina, then passes to the middle and upper thirds of the rectum, forming the recto-vaginal (Douglas) pouch.
  • Both pouches contain loops of small intestine.
  • The anterior and posterior layers from the side wall of the uterus come together as the broad ligament, which connects the uterus to the lateral pelvic wall.

Key words utero-vesical pouch, recto-vaginal pouch, isthmus, broad ligament

Source: pages 21 to 22

Enumerate the ligaments of the uterus.

The uterus has five ligaments: anterior, posterior, transverse cervical, round and broad.

  1. Anterior ligament: the vesico-uterine fold of peritoneum, from the body of the uterus to the superior surface of the bladder.
  2. Posterior ligament: the recto-vaginal fold of peritoneum; it forms the floor of the recto-vaginal (Douglas) pouch.
  3. Transverse cervical ligament (Mackenrodt, cardinal or lateral): one on each side, from the side of the cervix and lateral vaginal fornix to the lateral pelvic wall; it is the main support of the uterus.
  4. Round ligament (ligamentum teres): one on each side, about 12 cm; from the fundus, below and in front of the uterine tubes, through the inguinal canal to the labium majus.
  5. Broad ligament: a peritoneal fold whose free upper border encloses the uterine tube.

Key words transverse cervical ligament, main support, round ligament, inguinal canal, broad ligament

Source: pages 23 to 25

Describe the blood supply of the uterus.

The uterus is supplied by the uterine artery and the ovarian artery and drained by two venous plexuses into the internal iliac vein.

  • Uterine artery: a branch of the internal iliac artery. It runs a tortuous course on the lateral wall of the uterus, then laterally along the uterine tube within the broad ligament to anastomose with the ovarian artery.
  • Ovarian artery: a branch of the abdominal aorta.
  • Veins: they form two plexuses, one on each side of the lateral wall; two veins arise from each plexus and end in the internal iliac vein.

Key words uterine artery, internal iliac, ovarian artery, abdominal aorta, venous plexus

Anatomy of fallopian tubes & ovaries

Source: pages 29 to 31

Describe the parts and the arterial supply of the uterine (fallopian) tube.

The tube has four parts, from the uterus outwards: intramural part, isthmus, ampulla and infundibulum.

Intramural
Isthmus
Ampulla
Infundibulum
  • Intramural part: the shortest; it pierces the uterine wall to open into the uterine cavity.
  • Isthmus: just lateral to the uterus; the narrowest part.
  • Ampulla: the widest part and the most common site of fertilization.
  • Infundibulum: the lateral, funnel-shaped part that overlies the ovary; its finger-like fimbriae pick up the ovum released by the ovary.
  • Arterial supply: the medial three-quarters from the uterine artery, the lateral quarter from the ovarian artery.

Key words intramural, isthmus, ampulla, fertilization, infundibulum, fimbriae

Anatomy of placenta & umbilical cord

Source: pages 92 to 106

Mention 3 functions of the placenta.

The placenta has seven functions.

  1. Respiratory function.
  2. Nutritive function.
  3. Excretory function.
  4. Production of enzymes.
  5. Production of pregnancy-associated plasma proteins (PAPP).
  6. Barrier function.
  7. Endocrine function.

Key words respiratory, nutritive, excretory, enzymes, PAPP, barrier, endocrine

Source: page 97

Describe the definitive umbilical cord.

The definitive cord is covered by amnion and contains two umbilical arteries, one umbilical vein and Wharton’s jelly.

  • Contents: two umbilical arteries (right and left), one umbilical vein (left), surrounded by Wharton’s jelly.
  • Size: 50 to 60 cm long and about 2 cm in diameter; tortuous, with false nodes (unequal growth of the two arteries).
  • Attachment: between the primitive umbilical ring of the fetus and the centre of the placenta.
  • Arteries: carry venous blood from the fetus to the placenta. Left vein: carries arterial blood from the placenta to the fetus.
  • It allows the fetus to swim freely in the amniotic cavity.

Key words two arteries, left vein, Wharton’s jelly, 50 to 60 cm

Histology: male reproductive system

Source: pages 163 to 189

Describe the blood-testis barrier.

The blood-testis barrier is formed by tight (occluding) junctions between the lateral processes of adjacent Sertoli cells, near their bases.

  • Compartments: it divides the seminiferous tubule into a basal compartment (proliferating spermatogonia and blood capillaries) and an adluminal compartment (primary and secondary spermatocytes, spermatids and sperms).
  • It creates the suitable environment for gamete production.
  • It prevents an autoimmune reaction against the genetically different haploid cells.
  • It protects spermatogenic cells from toxic substances in the blood.
  • It selects the hormones and nutrients that pass for the growth of sperms.
  • Clinical: a defect lets sperm-specific antibodies form; they agglutinate sperm and prevent their movement and interaction with the ovum.

Key words Sertoli cells, tight junctions, basal compartment, adluminal compartment, autoimmune

Physiology: ovulation

Source: pages 230 to 232

Enumerate the functions of the LH surge at ovulation.

The LH surge completes meiosis, luteinizes the granulosa cells and weakens the follicular wall so the ovum is released.

  1. Completes meiosis by removing the inhibitory effect of the oocyte maturation inhibitor.
  2. Luteinizes the granulosa cells by removing the inhibitory effect of the luteinization inhibitor.
  3. Increases progesterone synthesis: hyperemia and plasma transudation swell the follicle, and the follicular wall becomes more distensible without a change in intrafollicular pressure.
  4. Increases collagenase activity: digests collagen in the follicular wall.
  5. Increases plasminogen activator: plasminogen becomes plasmin, which lyses the connective tissue of the wall.
  6. Increases prostaglandins (PGE and PGF): they free lysosomal enzymes that digest the wall and contract the ovarian smooth muscle to expel the ovum into the peritoneal cavity.

Key words OMI removed, LI removed, progesterone, collagenase, plasmin, prostaglandins

Source: page 231

Mention 3 signs of ovulation.

Ovulation occurs on the 14th day of the cycle, and the handout gives eight signs.

  1. LH surge: plasma LH rises 9 to 11 hours before ovulation.
  2. Urine: pregnanediol, the end product of progesterone.
  3. Temperature: morning body temperature rises by about 0.5 °C at ovulation and stays raised until menstruation.
  4. Ovary: lower abdominal pain at the time of ovulation.
  5. Uterus: early secretory phase in the endometrium.
  6. Vagina: increased vaginal secretion, and proliferation of the epithelium in a vaginal smear.
  7. Cervix: thick secretion that does not form a fern pattern.
  8. Ultrasound: shows the mature Graafian follicle.

Key words LH surge, pregnanediol, temperature rise, fern pattern, ultrasound
