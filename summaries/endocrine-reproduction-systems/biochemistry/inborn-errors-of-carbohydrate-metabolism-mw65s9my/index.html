Inborn errors of carbohydrate metabolism
At a glance

Inborn errors of carbohydrate metabolism are enzyme defects in fructose, pentose, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, glycogen and galactose metabolism.

Fructose metabolism

Fructose comes from the hydrolysis of sucrose (table sugar), which gives glucose and fructose, and from the monosaccharide fructose found in honey and fruits. Fructose supplies about 15% of daily energy.

Fructose is converted into glucose in the liver and intestine. The reverse conversion, glucose into fructose, takes place in the seminal vesicles, lens and peripheral nerves.

High-yield

Fructose is the main nutrient and major energy source for spermatozoa. Deficiency of fructose in semen correlates with male infertility.

Fructose disorders

Essential fructosuria

This is caused by deficiency of fructokinase. It is not a serious condition.

Hereditary fructose intolerance

This congenital disease is caused by deficiency of aldolase B. The resulting accumulation of fructose 1-phosphate leads to hypoglycemia after sucrose or fructose intake:

Liver phosphorylase inhibited
Glycogenolysis inhibited
Hypoglycemia

Affected patients also show fructosemia and fructosuria, and nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain after fructose intake.

Essential pentosuria and the uronic acid pathway

The uronic acid pathway is a minor pathway that converts glucose into glucuronic acid in the cytoplasm of the liver. Its products are used for:

  • Synthesis of glycosaminoglycans, such as chondroitin sulfate.
  • Conjugation with compounds such as steroid hormones and bilirubin, making them more soluble before excretion.
  • Detoxification of toxic compounds such as phenols, making them less toxic.

Vitamin C is also made from this pathway, but not in humans.

Glucuronic acid is converted to L-xylulose, then to D-xylulose, which joins the HMP pathway to be completely oxidized:

In essential pentosuria, L-xylulose cannot be converted into D-xylulose because of a genetic deficiency of L-xylulose reductase. L-xylulose therefore accumulates and is excreted in urine.

Favism and G6PD deficiency

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) is the key enzyme of the HMP pathway, which produces NADPH plus H+ and ribose 5-phosphate. G6PD deficiency is an inherited disease with hemolytic anemia, caused by inability to detoxify oxidizing agents. It affects more than 200 million people worldwide.

Most people with G6PD mutations show no symptoms. Hemolysis occurs when they are exposed to precipitating factors:

  • Oxidant drugs such as sulfamethoxazole and chloramphenicol, some antimalarials, and the antipyretic acetanilide (but not acetaminophen).
  • Favism in Mediterranean forms, caused by the oxidizing agent divicine in fava beans.
  • Infection, the most common precipitating factor, because it generates more reactive oxygen species.

Accumulated hydrogen peroxide also increases the oxidation of hemoglobin to methemoglobin.

Diagnosis

A patient with anemia, jaundice and hemolysis should have the following tests:

  1. Complete blood count and reticulocyte count.
  2. Liver enzymes, to exclude other causes of jaundice.
  3. Haptoglobin, which is decreased in hemolysis.
  4. Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test, which should be negative because hemolysis in G6PD deficiency is not immune mediated.
  5. Estimation of G6PD activity.

Glycogen storage disease: von Gierke's disease

Glycogen storage diseases are inborn errors of glycogen metabolism caused by deficiency of one enzyme of glycogenesis or glycogenolysis. Von Gierke's disease is the best known type and is due to deficiency of glucose-6-phosphatase in the liver.

Its manifestations are fasting hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly, hyperlipidemia, lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia and short stature. They arise as follows:

  • Glucose-6-P cannot be converted to glucose, so it cannot reach the blood. This causes fasting hypoglycemia, and the deficiency also affects the final steps of gluconeogenesis.
  • Inability to use glucose leads to lipolysis and increased fatty acid oxidation, causing hyperlipidemia.
  • Accumulated glucose-6-P enters glycolysis and raises lactic acid, causing lactic acidosis. Lactic acid in urine reduces uric acid excretion, causing hyperuricemia.
  • Glucose-6-P is converted to ribose 5-P through the HMP pathway, which stimulates PRPP synthetase and increases de novo purine synthesis, adding to hyperuricemia.

Galactose metabolism and galactosemia

Lactose, the milk sugar, is hydrolyzed by intestinal lactase into glucose and galactose. Galactose is absorbed into the portal blood and carried to the liver, where it is converted into glucose.

Galactosemia results from deficiency of either galactokinase or galactose-1-P uridyl transferase. Galactose is then not converted to glucose, causing galactosemia and galactosuria. Its incidence is about 1 in 60,000 births.

Symptoms often begin around day 3 of life in the newborn:

  • Vomiting and diarrhea after milk ingestion.
  • Jaundice and liver cirrhosis, because accumulated galactose-1-P depletes phosphates in the liver and leads to liver failure.
  • Mental retardation and severe bacterial infection.
  • Cataract, because aldose reductase converts galactose to galactitol, which accumulates in the lens.
  • Failure to thrive.

Treatment is a lactose-free diet.

In contrast, in lactating mammary glands glucose is converted into UDP-galactose, which reacts with glucose to form lactose through the enzyme lactose synthase. This is the basis of lactose biosynthesis.

Key Points / Quick Review

Fructose

  • Fructose supplies about 15% of daily energy and is the main energy source for sperm.
  • Essential fructosuria: fructokinase deficiency, not serious.
  • Hereditary fructose intolerance: aldolase B deficiency, causing hypoglycemia after fructose or sucrose.

Pentose

  • Essential pentosuria: L-xylulose reductase deficiency, with L-xylulose excreted in urine.

G6PD deficiency

  • Key enzyme of the HMP pathway, producing NADPH and ribose 5-phosphate.
  • Infection is the most common precipitating factor.
  • Hemolysis results from reactive oxygen species damaging red cell membranes.

Glycogen storage

  • Von Gierke's disease: glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency in the liver, with fasting hypoglycemia and hyperuricemia.

Galactose

  • Galactosemia: galactokinase or galactose-1-P uridyl transferase deficiency, treated with a lactose-free diet.
  • Cataract results from galactitol accumulating in the lens.
