1Complete Airway & Breathing Support

🫁 Airway Anatomy

  • Two openings: Nose → nasopharynx, Mouth → oropharynx
  • Passages separated anteriorly by palate, join posteriorly in pharynx
  • Epiglottis functionally separates oropharynx from laryngopharynx (hypopharynx)
  • Upper airway: nose/mouth to larynx
  • Lower airway: structures distal to larynx (trachea, bronchi, lungs)

⚠️ Causes of Airway Obstruction

  • In unconscious patient: posterior displacement of tongue (decreased muscle tone) — MOST COMMON
  • Foreign material: vomit, blood, foreign body
  • Laryngeal obstruction: oedema from burns, inflammation, anaphylaxis
  • Laryngeal spasm: upper airway stimulation or inhalation of foreign body
  • Lower airway obstruction: excessive bronchial secretions, mucosal oedema, bronchospasm, pulmonary oedema, aspiration of gastric contents

👁️ Recognition (Look, Listen, Feel)

  • Partial obstruction: noisy breathing, airway diminished
  • Inspiratory stridor: obstruction at laryngeal level
  • Expiratory wheeze: lower airway obstruction
  • Gurgling: liquid or semisolid foreign material in upper airway
  • Snoring: pharynx partially occluded by the tongue
  • Complete obstruction: paradoxical see-saw chest/abdominal movement, no air entry, cyanosis

🔧 Opening the Airway

  • Head tilt-chin lift: push down on forehead, place fingers under chin, pull UP on mandible bony parts (NOT soft tissues — pressure to neck soft tissues may obstruct airway)
  • Jaw thrust: stand at head, palms on temples, fingers under mandibular rami. Lift mandible until lower incisors higher than upper. Use when cervical spine injury suspected
  • After each manoeuvre: check with look, listen, feel
  • Finger sweep: remove any solid foreign body visible in the mouth

📏 Oropharyngeal Airway (OPA)

  • Prevents backward displacement of tongue
  • Size: incisors to angle of jaw OR ear lobule to angle of the mouth
  • Insertion: upside down to junction of soft/hard palate, rotate 180°, advance until lies in posterior pharynx

👃 Nasopharyngeal Airway (NPA)

  • Better tolerated than OPA
  • Indicated: clenched jaw, trismus, maxillofacial injuries
  • Contraindicated: fracture base of skull (risk of intracranial placement)
  • Size: 6-7mm for adults (measured by patient's little finger or anterior nares)
  • Insertion: check patency of right nostril first, lubricate, insert vertically along floor of nose with slight twisting
  • Side effects: bleeding in 30% of cases; if tube too long → stimulates laryngeal reflexes → laryngeal spasm or vomiting

🌬️ Ventilation Methods

  • Mouth-to-mask (pocket mask): transparent, uni-directed valve, has port for oxygen supplementation. Use two-hand technique for seal
  • Self-inflating bag (BVM): ventilates with air or oxygen. Single person use → leak can occur. High pressure forces gas into stomach → vomiting and aspiration

🔵 Laryngeal Mask Airway (LMA)

  • Wide bore tube with elliptical inflated cuff sealing around laryngeal inlet
  • Advantages: decreases gastric inflation/aspiration risk; no head extension needed (device of choice in C-spine injury); can be used by nursing/paramedical staff; useful if intubation failed and bag-mask inadequate
  • Size: 5 for adult male, 4 for adult female
  • Insertion: check cuff patency, deflate, lubricate. Hold like pen, insert behind upper incisors with upper surface to palate. Advance to posterior pharyngeal wall, press downwards and forwards
  • Cuff inflation: (size - 1) × 10 ml (e.g., size 5 → 40ml)
  • Fix tube, auscultate chest — small leak is acceptable
  • Limitations: significant leak in pulmonary oedema/bronchospasm; aspiration risk; may cause laryngeal spasm if patient not deeply unconscious; epiglottis may fold down covering laryngeal inlet

🔴 Tracheal Intubation

  • Advantages: protects airway from aspiration; adequate tidal volume; ability to suction secretions
  • Disadvantages: unrecognized misplacement; needs trained person; difficult intubation risk
  • Equipment: cuffed tracheal tubes size 8 for male, 7 for female; laryngoscope; syringe; lubricating jelly; tape
  • Confirmation of correct placement:
    1. Auscultation
    2. Increase in peak inspiratory pressure
    3. Palpating cuff in sternal notch while compressing pilot balloon
    4. Capnography (CO₂ detection) — GOLD STANDARD
    5. Chest X-ray (rarely required, except ICU)
  • Rule: "If doubt, get it out and ventilate the patient with a bag-mask"
  • Causes of difficult intubation: short neck, stiff neck, older scar in neck, big goiter/mass, receding lower jaw, prominent incisors, narrow mouth

✂️ Cricothyroidotomy (Emergency Surgical Airway)

  • Indications: "cannot intubate, cannot ventilate"; extensive facial trauma; laryngeal edema
  • Needle Cricothyroidotomy:
    • Complications: cannula outside trachea → massive surgical emphysema, hemorrhage, oesophageal perforation
  • Surgical Cricothyroidotomy:
    • Advantages: protected airway by cuffed tube; higher airway pressures; tracheal suction possible; enables lung ventilation despite complete airway obstruction above
    • Equipment: #11 blade scalpel, tracheostomy or ET tube, iodine and swab, tracheal dilator & hook

2Foreign Body Airway Obstruction

😮 Choking Overview

  • Physiological response to sudden obstruction of the airway
  • FBAO causes asphyxia — terrifying condition, occurs acutely, patient often unable to explain
  • Severe cases: rapid loss of consciousness and death if first aid not undertaken quickly

📊 Mild vs Severe Obstruction

  • Mild obstruction:
    • Patient able to breathe, cough effectively, and speak
    • Children: fully responsive, crying or verbally respond, loud cough, able to take breath before cough
  • Severe obstruction:
    • Unable to breathe or speak/vocalize
    • Wheezy breath sounds
    • Quiet or silent cough attempts
    • Cyanosis and diminishing conscious level (especially in children)
    • Universal choking sign: hands clutching the throat

🆘 Management

  • Mild: encourage patient to continue coughing
  • Severe (conscious adult):
    1. Give 5 back blows (heel of hand between shoulder blades)
    2. If unsuccessful → 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich) — above umbilicus, below xiphoid
    3. Continue alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until successful or patient becomes unconscious
  • Infant (conscious): 5 back blows + 5 chest thrusts (avoid abdominal thrusts)
  • Unconscious victim: start CPR 30:2. Before compressions, look in mouth for visible object and remove if seen
  • After successful relief: victim should be seen by a doctor (abdominal thrusts may cause internal injury)

3Basic Life Support & Defibrillation

📖 Definitions

  • First aid: immediate care given to an injured or suddenly ill person
  • Cardiac arrest: sudden cessation of heartbeat and cardiac function — no pulse, no breathing, unconscious

🔄 BLS Algorithm (DRSABC-D)

  1. Danger — check for hazards/risks/safety
  2. Response — shake shoulders, ask "Are you all right?"
    • If responds: leave as found, find out what's wrong, reassess regularly
    • If unresponsive: shout for help
  3. Send for help — call emergency services
  4. Airway — open airway (head tilt-chin lift or jaw thrust)
  5. Breathing — look, listen, feel for NORMAL breathing (max 10 seconds)
    • Check carotid pulse at the same time
    • Do not confuse agonal breathing with normal breathing
  6. Circulation — 3C: Confirm cardiac arrest, Call resuscitation team, Compress CPR
  7. Defibrillation — attach AED as soon as available

❤️ Chest Compressions

  • Ratio: 30 compressions : 2 rescue breaths
  • Rate: 100-120 per minute
  • Depth: 5-6 cm (2-2.4 inches)
  • Hand placement: heel of one hand in centre of chest (lower half of sternum), other hand on top, interlock fingers
  • Technique: equal compression:relaxation, allow full chest recoil
  • Change operator every 2 minutes to avoid fatigue
  • Do NOT re-check for carotid pulse or signs of life during CPR
  • Re-check pulse after 1 minute

💨 Rescue Breaths

  • Pinch nose, take normal breath, place lips over mouth
  • Blow until chest rises (~1 second)
  • Allow chest to fall
  • If unwilling/unable to perform rescue breaths: continue chest compressions only
  • With advanced airway in place: give rescue breaths only at rate of 10 per minute

⚡ Defibrillation (AED)

  • Turn on AED and follow voice prompts
  • Apply pads, shock if advised
  • Resume CPR immediately for 2 minutes after shock
  • Minimize pauses between compressions

🛑 When to Stop CPR

  • Qualified help arrives and takes over
  • Victim starts breathing normally
  • Rescuer becomes exhausted

4Recovery Position, Positions & Safe Transfer

😵 Fainting

  • Loss of consciousness caused when blood supply to the brain is momentarily reduced

🔄 Management Scheme

  1. Safety — assess danger for you AND patient
  2. Check response — shake shoulder, shout in BOTH ears "Can you hear me?"
    • If response: put patient in recovery position
    • If no response: call for help
  3. Shout for help
  4. Open airway — head tilt/chin lift (unless C-spine injury)
  5. Check breathing — look (chest movement), listen (mouth), feel (breath on cheek) — NOT MORE THAN 10 SECONDS
  6. Check carotid pulse
  7. If unresponsive + not breathing normally: call emergency, start CPR

📞 Calling Emergency Services (8 Steps)

  1. Identify yourself
  2. The telephone number
  3. Your place/location
  4. Number of victims
  5. The condition of the victims
  6. What do you want?
  7. What will you do?
  8. Ask if he wants any information

🔄 Recovery Position (5 Steps)

  1. Kneel beside victim, ensure both legs straight
  2. Place arm nearest to you at right angle, elbow bent, palm up
  3. Bring far arm across chest, hold back of hand against victim's cheek nearest to you
  4. Grasp far leg just above knee, pull it up keeping foot on ground
  5. Holding hand against cheek, pull on far leg to roll victim towards you. Adjust upper leg (hip and knee bent at right angles). Tilt head back to ensure airway open. Reassess breathing.
  • Maintains free airway, prevents tongue blocking, allows fluids to drain

🚑 Safe Transfer

  • Aim: achieve specialized care in different location
  • Before transfer (6 points):
    1. Notify receiving unit
    2. Continue all monitors during transfer
    3. Secure all cannulas, catheters, tubes and drains
    4. Reassess the patient before transfer
    5. Ensure portable suction, oxygen, emergency drugs and defibrillator monitor accompany patient
    6. Prepare full records of events, drugs, and procedures
  • Modes of transport: chair, trolley on foot, ambulance, helicopter

5Clinical History Taking & Exam

🤝 Communication Skills

  • Knock before entering a room
  • Address patient formally first (Miss, Ms., Mrs., Mr.)
  • Ensure physical comfort for all, including yourself
  • Look at patient; maintain good eye contact if culturally appropriate
  • Watch your language — avoid professional jargon
  • Do not accept a previous diagnosis as a chief complaint
  • Seek clues from patient's verbal behaviors and body language
  • Look for hidden concerns underlying chief complaint

📚 Useful Sources of Information

  • Relatives/cohabitants
  • Close friends/room-mates
  • The GP or primary care team
  • The pharmacist
  • Nursing/residential home staff
  • Anyone who witnessed the event

📋 Standard History Framework

  1. Personal history — name, age, sex, occupation, marital status, residence, habits, handedness, menstrual history
  2. Complaint — patient's own words, short, with duration
  3. History of present illness — onset, course, associated symptoms
  4. Past history — diseases, operations, transfusions
  5. Drug history — type, duration, allergies
  6. Family history — similar conditions, consanguinity, common diseases

👤 Personal History Details

  • Name: to be familiar, gain confidence
  • Age: young → congenital heart disease; old → atherosclerosis
  • Sex: female → thyroid, autoimmune; male → coronary heart disease
  • Occupation: farmer → bilharziasis; healthcare → viral hepatitis (B, C)
  • Marital status: single/married/divorced/widow; duration, number of children, age of youngest
  • Residence: born in, live in; endemic areas → parasitic disease
  • Habits: smoking, alcohol
  • Handedness: determines dominant hemisphere
  • Example: "Ayman Mostafa Ibrahim, 45 years old male, works as farmer, married with 2 offspring, from Zagazig, right-handed, cigarette smoker with no other special habits"

📝 Complaint & Present Illness

  • Complaint: as short as possible, duration, no medical terms, patient's own words
    • Examples: "abdominal distention" (not ascites), "awareness of heartbeat" (not palpitation)
  • History of present illness: use medical terms
    • Onset: acute, gradual, insidious
    • Course: progressive, regressive, stationary
    • Duration, associated symptoms, what increases/decreases
    • Related investigations, related treatment (medical/surgical)
    • Review of other systems

📖 Past, Drug & Family History

  • Past history: diseases (CHD, hepatitis, TB), operations, blood transfusion, similar attacks
  • Drug history: type, duration, drug allergy
  • Family history: similar condition, positive consanguinity, common diseases (HTN, DM)
    • IF NEGATIVE: "Family history is of no medical importance"

🔍 General Examination Setup

  • Introduce yourself, explain what you'll do, obtain verbal consent
  • Ensure privacy to undress
  • Make sure you won't be disturbed
  • Check clean sheet on examination couch
  • Ask if companion should stay
  • Check equipment (torch, cotton wool, tendon hammer, stethoscope)
  • Patient supine with head and shoulders raised to ~45°

🧠 Conscious Level (5 Levels)

LevelDescription
1. AlertAwake or readily aroused; oriented; fully aware; responds appropriately
2. Lethargic/SomnolentNot fully alert; drifts off to sleep when not stimulated; can be aroused to name when called in normal voice but looks drowsy

🎨 Colour Assessment

  • Pallor (anemia/blood loss)
  • Central cyanosis (tongue/mucous membranes)
  • Peripheral cyanosis (fingertips, earlobes)
  • Jaundice
  • Abnormal skin pigmentation

  • Cardiovascular examination
  • Respiratory examination
  • Abdominal examination
  • Neurological examination
  • Head, Neck, Extremities examination

6Pulse, BP, Respiration, Temperature

⏰ When to Assess Vital Signs

  1. On admission — to obtain baseline data
  2. When patient has change in health status or reports symptoms (chest pain, fainting)
  3. According to nursing or medical order
  4. Before and after administration of certain medications affecting RR or BP
  5. Before and after surgery or invasive diagnostic procedures
  6. Before and after any nursing intervention affecting vitals (e.g., ambulation)

💓 Pulse

  • Do NOT use thumb to feel pulses — you may mistake your own pulse for the patient's weak pulse
  • Peripheral pulse: foot, neck (e.g., radial, brachial, carotid)
  • Apical pulse (central): at apex of heart
  • Pulse deficit = apical rate - peripheral rate (normally zero; seen in atrial fibrillation)
  • Rate: 60-90 bpm resting. Bradycardia <60, Tachycardia >100
  • Rhythm: normal = regular; abnormal = irregular
  • Volume (Pulse Pressure) = Systolic - Diastolic. Average 30-60 mmHg. Abnormal: small or big volume

👐 Pulse Sites & Technique

  • Radial: pads of index/middle/ring fingers over right radial artery. Count 30 sec × 2. Palpate both radial pulses simultaneously for volume difference
  • Brachial: medial side of antecubital fossa, just medial to tendinous insertion of biceps
  • Carotid: find larynx, move 2cm laterally, press backwards medial to sternomastoid. Do NOT compress both carotids at once — especially in frail and elderly

🩺 Blood Pressure

  • Measure in both arms — higher reading closer to central aortic pressure
  • Rest patient 5 minutes before measurement
  • Arm at heart level, no tight clothing
  • Standard cuff: 12.5cm width, 30-35cm length. Bladder should be ~80% of arm length, 40% of width
  • Palpatory method first: inflate until pulse impalpable = rough systolic estimate
  • Inflate 30mmHg more, then auscultate with diaphragm over brachial artery
  • Deflate slowly: 2-3 mmHg/s

5 Korotkoff Sounds:

  1. Phase I: Sounds start to appear → Record Systolic BP
  2. Phase II: Sounds muffle
  3. Phase III: Sounds sharp
  4. Phase IV: Sounds muffle (muffling)
  5. Phase V: Sounds disappear → Record Diastolic BP

Common Problems in BP Measurement:

  1. Different in each arm: >10mmHg difference suggests subclavian artery stenosis — record highest
  2. Wrong cuff size: standard adult cuff suits arm circumference 22-26cm. In obese patients, use large adult or thigh cuff
  3. Auscultatory gap: sounds disappear between systolic and diastolic (up to 20% of elderly hypertensives). Avoid by palpating systolic first
  4. Arm at wrong level: ~5mmHg change per 7cm elevation difference
  5. Terminal digit preference: record true reading, don't round to 0 or 5
  6. Postural change: check after standing 2 min. Drop ≥20mmHg systolic = postural hypotension

🫁 Respiration

  • Costal (thoracic): chest movement up/down — commonly used for males
  • Diaphragmatic (abdominal): abdomen movement — commonly used for females
  • Rate: 14-20/min. Measure full minute if irregular, 30 sec if regular
  • Rhythm: regularity of expiration and inspiration. Normal = automatic & effortless
  • Depth: normal, deep (large volume), or shallow (small volume)

🌡️ Temperature

  • Core temperature: internal organs, remains constant (36.8-37.2°C oral)
  • Surface temperature: skin, variable, doesn't indicate internal temperature
  • Fever: morning >37.2°C or evening >37.7°C
  • Oral: most common, leave 3-5 min. Wait 10-15 min after hot/cold food or smoking
  • Rectal: most reliable, core temp, >0.5°C higher than oral. Hold 3-5 min. Contraindicated in diarrhea and anal fissure
  • Axillary: safe for infants, 0.5°C less than oral

7ID, SC, IM, IV, IO & Z-track

💉 Safe Injection Practices

  • Set of steps to perform injections in optimally safe manner for patients, healthcare workers, and others
  • Prevent transmission: patient→worker, worker→patient, patient→patient
  • Safe Injection = No Infection
  • 4 Steps:
    1. Safe production: sterile medications from manufacturer
    2. Safe preparation: prepare in clean area
    3. Safe administration: follow standard precautions
    4. Safe disposal: minimize risks

⚠️ Potential Complications

  • Infection, incorrect location, pain
  • Pain factors: the needle, chemical composition, technique, speed of injection, volume of drug
  • Anaphylaxis, nerve damage, hemorrhage, hitting blood vessel
  • Sterile abscess, lipodystrophy

🧰 Equipment & Documentation

  • Equipment: medication order, alcohol swabs, sharps disposal container, medication, gloves, syringe and needle of appropriate size
  • Documentation: time, drug name, drug dosage in mg, route, patient response

💊 Intradermal (ID)

  • Injected into dermis, just below epidermis
  • Longest absorption time of all parenteral routes
  • Angle: 5-15°, Volume: <0.5 mL
  • Sites: inner forearm, upper back, upper chest
  • Uses: TB screening, allergy testing, local anesthesia, Botox

💉 Subcutaneous (SC)

  • Injected into fatty layer beneath dermis
  • Volume: ≤1 mL, Angle: 45° (or up to 90° if fat layer thick and needle short)
  • Blood supply less than muscle → slower absorption than IM
  • Absorption rates by site: abdomen (fastest) → upper arm (intermediate) → thigh/buttocks (slowest)
  • Uses: insulin, heparin, testosterone
  • Sites: abdomen (avoid 1-2" around umbilicus), upper arm, thigh, buttocks

💪 Intramuscular (IM)

  • Indications: noncompliant, uncooperative, unable other routes, don't tolerate oral (dysphagia, profound vomiting, malabsorption)
  • Contraindications: active infection/cellulitis/dermatitis at site, allergy to drug, thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy, hypovolemic shock, myopathies/muscular atrophy
  • Angle: 72-90°
  • Z-track technique: ensures medication stays in muscle, prevents tracking back into subcutaneous tissue
  • Safe sites: ventrogluteal, vastus lateralis, deltoid. Avoid dorsogluteal (sciatic nerve risk)
  • Complications (9):
    1. Persistent pain at site
    2. Muscle fibrosis and contracture
    3. Abscess
    4. Gangrene
    5. Nerve injury — sciatic in gluteal, femoral in vastus lateralis
    6. Periostitis, osteomyelitis
    7. HIV/hepatitis transmission
    8. Glass particles from vials
    9. Vascular injury

💧 Intravenous (IV)

  • Fastest route. Uses syringe/catheter or infusion pump/gravity drip
  • Uses: antimicrobials, anticonvulsants, diuretics, steroids, analgesics
  • Complications: burning/stinging/redness if leak, pulmonary edema (fluid overload), air embolism
  • Air embolism symptoms: shortness of breath, increased heart rate, shoulder pain, light-headedness, confusion
  • Note: some meds can be given multiple ways (epinephrine IM or SC, epoetin IV or SC, Botox IM/ID/intravesical)

🦴 Intraosseous (IO)

  • Indications: emergency when IV access impossible — cardiac arrest, shock, status epilepticus, burns, edema, obesity
  • Site: tibia (most common)
  • Contraindications: fracture of tibia/femur, infection at site, previous orthopedic procedure, knee replacement, tumor near site, peripheral vascular disease, excessive tissue at insertion site

🔬 Other Injection Types

  • Intra-articular — into a joint
  • Intraosseous — into bone marrow
  • Intradetrusor — into bladder wall muscle
  • Intraocular (intravitreal) — into eye
  • Intraperitoneal — within abdominal cavity
  • Intracardiac — into heart muscle/ventricles
