1Pericardium›

Transverse Sinus

  • Formation: reflection of visceral pericardium from front of 2 atria → back of ascending aorta + pulmonary trunk.
  • Boundaries: Anterior = ascending aorta + PT | Posterior = 2 atria + SVC | Superior = right pulmonary artery.
  • Importance: allows distension of great vessels (systole) and atria (diastole). Used surgically to place ligature around aorta + PT (cardioplegia).

Oblique Sinus

  • Site: pericardial recess BEHIND the left atrium, separating it from esophagus and descending aorta.
  • Boundaries: Anterior = posterior wall of LA + visceral pericardium | Posterior = parietal pericardium.
  • Opens inferiorly between IVC opening and lower left pulmonary vein.
  • Importance: permits distension of LA during return of oxygenated blood from lungs.

Pericardiocentesis

  • Definition: insertion of needle into pericardial cavity to aspirate excess fluid.
  • Site: left 5th–6th ICS close to sternum (bare area of pericardium) → avoids pleura + lung.
  • Method: needle inserted vertically through the bare area.
  • Indications: cardiac tamponade | hemopericardium | pyopericardium | hydropericardium (>50 ml) | pneumopericardium.

Surfaces of the Heart

  • 3 surfaces: Sternocostal (anterior), Diaphragmatic (inferior), Left (pulmonary).
  • Sternocostal: Atrial part = RA + right auricle + left auricle. Ventricular part = RV (2/3) + LV (1/3). NB: LA hidden by ascending aorta + PT. Part uncovered by lung = superficial cardiac dullness.
  • Diaphragmatic: Flat; rests on central tendon. LV (2/3) + RV (1/3).
  • Left surface: Mainly LV + partly LA + left auricle. Directed upward, backward, left.

Borders of the Heart

  • Right border (vertical): Right atrium. SVC opening to IVC opening. Separates base from sternocostal surface.
  • Left border (curved/oblique): Mainly LV + partly left auricle. Left auricle to apex.
  • Lower border (horizontal): Right ventricle. IVC opening to apex. Separates sternocostal from diaphragmatic surface.
  • Upper border: Both atria (mainly LA). Hidden by ascending aorta + PT.

Grooves of the Heart

  • Atrioventricular (coronary) groove: Encircles the heart; separates atria from ventricles. Contains: RCA (right part) + circumflex branch of LCA (left part) + coronary sinus (posterior part).
  • Anterior interventricular groove: On sternocostal surface. Contains: LAD (anterior IV artery) + great cardiac vein.
  • Posterior interventricular groove: On diaphragmatic surface. Contains: posterior IV artery (from RCA 80%) + middle cardiac vein.

3Internal Features of the Heart›

Right Atrium

  • Crista terminalis: vertical ridge separating smooth part (sinus venarum) from rough part (musculi pectinati).
  • Fossa ovalis: oval depression in interatrial septum = remnant of fetal foramen ovale.
  • Openings: SVC (no valve) | IVC (Eustachian valve) | Coronary sinus (Thebesian valve) | Right AV orifice (tricuspid) = LARGEST | Venae cordis minimae.

Right Ventricle

  • Wall: 1/3 of LV. Cross-section: crescent-shaped.
  • Parts: large lower rough inflowing part + upper smooth outflowing = infundibulum.
  • Papillary muscles: 3 — anterior (largest), posterior, septal.
  • Moderator band: IV septum → anterior papillary muscle; carries right bundle branch; prevents overdistension.
  • Openings: right AV orifice (tricuspid) + pulmonary orifice (pulmonary valve).

Left Atrium

  • Interior: smooth (except left auricle = muscular reticulum). Forms base of heart.
  • Openings: 4 pulmonary veins (no valves) + left AV orifice (mitral valve) + venae cordis minimae.

Left Ventricle

  • Wall: 3× thicker (3:1 ratio). Cross-section: circular.
  • Parts: large lower rough inflowing part + upper smooth outflowing = aortic vestibule.
  • Papillary muscles: 2 — anterior + posterior. NO moderator band.
  • Openings: left AV orifice (mitral) + aortic orifice (aortic valve).

4Blood Supply of the Heart›

Right Coronary Artery (RCA)

  • Origin: anterior aortic sinus. Main branches:
  • SA nodal artery (60% from RCA; 40% from LCA)
  • Marginal artery (largest branch; along lower border → apex)
  • Posterior IV artery → posterior IV septum + AV node (80%)

Left Coronary Artery (LCA)

  • Origin: left posterior aortic sinus. Divides into:
  • LAD (anterior IV artery): anterior IV septum + most of LV + part of RV + part of left bundle branch.
  • Circumflex artery: left posterior AV groove → anastomoses with RCA; gives left marginal artery.

Coronary Sinus

  • Site: left posterior coronary sulcus (posterior AV groove).
  • Opens into: RA between IVC opening and right AV orifice.

5Pharynx›

Overview

  • Funnel-shaped fibromuscular tube; 12–14 cm; base of skull → lower border of cricoid (C6).
  • Common passage for food and air.

Waldeyer's Ring

  • Components: pharyngeal tonsil (posterosuperior) + palatine tonsils (lateral) + lingual tonsil (anteroinferior).
  • Forms a ring of MALT at the entrance of respiratory and digestive tracts.

Palatine Tonsils

  • Tonsillar fossa: between palatoglossal (ant.) and palatopharyngeal (post.) arches.
  • Lymph drainage: jugulodigastric (tonsillar) node = upper deep cervical.
  • Paratonsillar vein in lateral fibrous capsule → injury = severe tonsillectomy bleeding.

Piriform Fossa

  • Site: deep recess in anterior lateral wall of laryngopharynx, on each side of laryngeal inlet.
  • Contents under mucosa: internal laryngeal nerve + superior laryngeal vessels.
  • Clinical: foreign bodies lodge here; endoscope perforation = internal laryngeal nerve injury.

Pharyngeal Wall Layers (inside→out)

  • Mucous membrane → Pharyngobasilar fascia → Muscular coat → Buccopharyngeal fascia.
  • Constrictors (3): superior, middle, inferior — insert into median pharyngeal raphe.
  • Longitudinal (3): stylopharyngeus, palatopharyngeus, salpingopharyngeus — elevate larynx.

Key Cartilages

  • Thyroid: 2 laminae; acute angle (Adam's apple) in males, obtuse in females.
  • Cricoid: signet-ring; C6 level; ONLY cartilage encircling entire lumen.
  • Arytenoids: pyramidal; vocal process (attaches vocal cord) + muscular process.

Cricothyroid Ligament (CTL)

  • Inf. attachment: upper border of cricoid.
  • Lateral CTL free upper border = TRUE VOCAL CORD (vocal ligament).
  • Rima glottidis = space between both vocal cords.
  • Median CTL (thickened middle part): emergency cricothyrotomy site.

Quadrangular Membrane

  • Upper free border = aryepiglottic fold (laryngeal inlet boundary).
  • Lower free border = vestibular ligament = FALSE VOCAL CORD.
  • Rima vestibuli = space between vestibular folds.

Nerve Supply

  • Motor (all intrinsic EXCEPT cricothyroid): recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN).

Thyrohyoid Membrane

  • Below: upper border of thyroid cartilage. Above: upper border of body + greater horn of hyoid bone.
  • Median + 2 lateral parts thickened = median + lateral thyrohyoid ligaments.
  • Pierced by: superior laryngeal artery + internal laryngeal nerve (sensory, supraglottic).

Intrinsic Muscles of the Larynx

  • Open/close laryngeal inlet:
    • Close: oblique arytenoids + aryepiglottic.
    • Open: thyroepiglottic.
  • Abduct/adduct vocal cords:
    • Abduct (open rima glottidis): posterior cricoarytenoid ONLY.
    • Adduct (close rima glottidis): lateral cricoarytenoid + transverse arytenoid.
  • Tension of vocal cords:
    • Tense: cricothyroid + vocalis.
    • Relax: thyroarytenoid.
  • NB: All paired EXCEPT transverse arytenoid (unpaired).

Cavity of the Larynx

  • Extends: laryngeal inlet (communicates with laryngopharynx) to lower border of cricoid (continuous with trachea).
  • Laryngeal inlet boundaries: Anterior = epiglottis; Posterior = mucosa between arytenoids; Lateral = aryepiglottic folds.
  • 3 Subdivisions (divided by vestibular + vocal folds):
    • Vestibule (supraglottic): laryngeal inlet to vestibular folds.
    • Ventricle/sinus (glottic): between vestibular and vocal folds.
    • Infraglottic: vocal folds to lower border of cricoid.

Blood Supply, Venous & Lymphatic Drainage

  • Arterial: Above vocal folds = superior laryngeal artery (from superior thyroid artery). Below = inferior laryngeal artery (from inferior thyroid artery). NB: rima glottidis has dual blood supply.
  • Venous: Superior laryngeal vein -> superior thyroid vein. Inferior laryngeal vein -> inferior thyroid vein.
  • Lymphatics: Above vocal cords -> upper deep cervical nodes. Below vocal cords -> prelaryngeal + pretracheal nodes -> lower deep cervical nodes.

Nerve Damage Effects

  • External laryngeal nerve damage: Weakness of phonation (cricothyroid paralyzed -> vocal cords cannot tense).
  • Recurrent laryngeal nerve damage (unilateral): Cord lies in paramedian position; does not vibrate; other cord usually compensates.
  • Recurrent laryngeal nerve damage (bilateral): Both cords in paramedian position -> loss of phonation + difficulty breathing.
  • Both recurrent + external laryngeal nerve damage (bilateral): Cords further abducted + fixed = cadaveric position (rima glottidis).

1Cardiac Output›

Definitions

  • COP = SV × HR = 70 ml × 70/min = 5 L/min (normal).
  • SV = EDV − ESV = 140 − 70 = 70 ml. EDV = 140 ml; ESV = 70 ml.

Intrinsic Regulation (Autoregulation)

  • Heterometric (Starling's law): ↑preload (EDV) → ↑fibre length → ↑force → ↑SV. Starts ~30 sec. ONLY mechanism in heart failure. Limited by over-stretch.
  • Homeometric (Anrep): ↑force WITHOUT ↑EDV. Starts 2–5 min. Prolonged exercise. LOST in heart failure.
  • Both work in transplanted (denervated) hearts.

Extrinsic Regulation

  • Sympathetic: ↑HR (chronotropic) + ↑contractility (inotropic) + venoconstriction → ↑VR.
  • Parasympathetic: ↓HR (negative chronotropic). Catecholamines + thyroxine: +ino+chrono.

HR Effects on COP

  • Mild–moderate change (60–160/min): minimal effect on CO.
  • Marked ↑ (>160/min): ↓diastole → ↓EDV → ↓SV → ↓CO.
  • Marked ↓ (<40/min): ↑SV cannot compensate → ↓CO.

Venous Return (VR)

  • VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RVR. MSFP = 7 mmHg; CVP = 2 mmHg.
  • Exercise: mild = ↑200%; severe (trained athletes) = ↑700%.

2Regulation of Arterial Blood Pressure›

Three Levels

  • Rapid (seconds): nervous — medullary CVS centers.
  • Intermediate (min–hours): capillary fluid shift + stress relaxation + thirst.
  • Slow (hours–days): renal — pressure diuresis + RAAS.

Medullary CVS Centers

  • Pressor area: VCC → vasoconstrictor tone + ↑HR + ↑contractility + venoconstriction.
  • Depressor area: VDC (inhibits VCC) + CIC (via vagus → ↓HR). Dominant centers = VCC + CIC.
  • Baroreceptors: aortic arch (CN X) + carotid sinus (CN IX = buffer nerve). Range: 60–180 mmHg.
  • Bainbridge reflex: ↑RAP → stretch atrial receptors → inhibit CIC → tachycardia.
  • Bezold-Jarisch: MI chemicals → ventricular chemoreceptors → ↓ABP + bradycardia + apnea.

Slow Renal Mechanisms

  • Pressure diuresis: ↑ABP → ↑GFR → ↑urine → ↓BV → ↓ABP (most powerful long-term).
  • RAAS: ↓ABP → renin → Ang I → ACE (lung) → Ang II → VC (50× NA) + ↑aldosterone + ↑ADH + ↑thirst.
  • ANP: opposite to RAAS → VD + natriuresis + ↓aldosterone + ↓ADH.

4Hemorrhage and Shock›

1. Definition & Classification of Hemorrhage

  • Definition: Loss of blood from the cardiovascular system.
  • By site: External | Internal | Interstitial.
  • By onset: Acute | Chronic.
  • By prognosis: Compensated (<20% loss) vs Uncompensated (>20% loss).

2. Immediate Compensatory Mechanisms (minutes)

  • I. Nervous: ↓ABP → ↓baroreceptors → ↑sympathetic → VC + ↑HR + ↑contractility + coronary VD + ↑liver glycogenolysis.

  • III. Other: Intense thirst sensation → ↑water intake | Hyperventilation (chemoreceptors → VC → ↑VR + ↑COP + ↑ABP).

  • Reversible (Compensated): Immediate + delayed mechanisms restore ABP → negative feedback.

6. Causes of Death in Refractory Shock

  • a. Cardiac depression: ↓ABP → ↓coronary flow → myocardial ischemia → ↓contractility.
  • b. Cerebral depression: ↓ABP → ↓cerebral flow → depression of VMC → no correction of ↓ABP.
  • c. Precapillary dilation + venular constriction: Blood trapped in capillaries → ↓VR + ↑filtration → plasma loss → worse shock.
  • d. Release of toxins: Myocardial toxic factor (↓heart) | Endotoxin (VD + cardiac depression) | Free radicals (vessel damage).
  • e. Thrombosis of small vessels: Sluggish circulation → clotting factor activation + platelet aggregation.
  • f. Metabolic acidosis: ↓O₂ → lactic acid → ↑H⁺ → tissue damage.
  • g. Acute respiratory failure: Alveolar damage + toxin release.
  • h. Acute renal failure: Severe renal VC → tubular necrosis | Myoglobin from damaged muscle → tubular damage.

10Ventilation/Perfusion Relationship›

V/Q Ratio - Definition & Values

  • V = alveolar ventilation = 4.2 L/min; Q = pulmonary blood flow = 5 L/min. Optimal V/Q = 0.84.
  • V/Q -> infinity (dead space): Ventilated, not perfused (e.g., pulmonary embolism) -> wasted ventilation -> increased alveolar PO2 + decreased PCO2.
  • V/Q -> 0 (shunt): Perfused, not ventilated (e.g., mucus plug, atelectasis) -> venous admixture -> hypoxia + hypercapnia.

Regional Distribution (Upright Lung)

  • Apex: Increased V/Q (gravity affects Q more than V; poorly perfused). Higher PO2, lower PCO2 - favourable for TB.
  • Base: Decreased V/Q (better perfused than ventilated). Lower PO2, higher PCO2. Most gas exchange occurs here.
  • Overall: non-uniform V/Q reduces gas exchange efficiency.

Dead Space

  • Anatomical dead space: Conducting airways (no gas exchange) ~150 ml.
  • Alveolar dead space: Ventilated alveoli with no perfusion.
  • Physiological (total) dead space = anatomical + alveolar.

11Gas Exchange›

Fick's Law

  • Diffusion rate ∝ (ΔP × S × A × T) / (d × √MW)
  • O₂ gradient: alveolar 100 − venous 40 = 60 mmHg.
  • CO₂ gradient: venous 46 − alveolar 40 = 6 mmHg (CO₂ is 20× more soluble → diffuses 20× faster).

Respiratory Membrane

  • Thickness: 0.5–0.6 μm (↑ in edema + fibrosis). Area: ~70 m² (↑ in exercise; ↓ in emphysema).
  • 7 layers: surfactant → alveolar fluid → type I epithelium + BM → interstitium → capillary BM → endothelium.

Diffusion Capacity

  • O₂: 25 ml/min/mmHg at rest; ↑65 in exercise. CO₂: 500 ml/min/mmHg (20× O₂).
  • Equilibration time at rest: 0.75 sec (O₂ equilibrates in 0.25 sec → 0.5 sec reserve).

Factors Affecting Gas Exchange

  • 1. Pressure gradient: O2: alveolar 100 - venous 40 = 60 mmHg. CO2: venous 46 - alveolar 40 = 6 mmHg. Both increase in exercise. CO2 diffuses 20x faster due to higher solubility.
  • 2. Surface area (70 m2): Increases in exercise (open + dilate capillaries + increase alveolar size). Decreases in emphysema, pneumothorax, lung resection, old age.
  • 3. Membrane thickness (0.6 um): Increases (decreases diffusion) in pulmonary oedema + lung fibrosis.
  • 4. V/Q ratio: Abnormal V/Q impairs gas exchange.
  • 5. Solubility / MW (Fick + Graham): D proportional to S/square root MW. CO2 solubility 20x O2 -> diffuses 20x faster.

12Oxygen Transport›

Forms of O2 Transport

  • A. Physical solution (dissolved): Arterial = 0.3 ml/100ml (PO2 100 x 0.003); Venous = 0.12 ml/100ml. Determines PO2, direction of diffusion, Hb saturation.
  • B. Chemical (Hb-bound): Arterial = 19 ml/100ml; Venous = 14 ml/100ml. O2 storage.

O2 Capacity, Content & Saturation

  • O2 Capacity: Max O2 if Hb fully saturated. 1g Hb = 1.33 ml O2; 15g x 1.33 = 20 ml/100ml. Depends on Hb amount ONLY.
  • O2 Content: Actual O2 in chemical form. Arterial = 19ml; Venous = 14ml/100ml. Depends on Hb amount + PO2.
  • O2 Saturation: Content/Capacity x 100. Arterial = 95%; Venous = 70%. Depends on PO2 ONLY.
  • O2 Utilization coefficient: At rest = (19-14)/19 x 100 = 25%. Exercise = 75%.

O2 Dissociation Curve

  • Shape: S-shaped (sigmoid). Each Fe++ binding facilitates next (cooperative binding). R-state (high PO2, high affinity); T-state (low PO2, low affinity).
  • At lung (high PO2): PO2 100 -> 95% sat; PO2 70 -> 90% sat. Flat curve -> Hb loads O2 easily.
  • At tissue (low PO2): PO2 40 -> 70% sat; PO2 25 -> 40% sat. Steep curve -> Hb unloads O2 easily.
  • Bohr Effect: Decreased pH -> decreased Hb affinity for O2 -> facilitates unloading at tissues + loading at lungs.
  • Shift RIGHT (decreased affinity, increased unloading): Decreased PO2, increased PCO2, increased H+ (acidosis), increased temperature, increased 2,3-DPG; anaemia, pregnancy, exercise. Increases O2 supply to active muscle.
  • Shift LEFT (increased affinity, increased loading): Increased PO2, decreased PCO2, decreased H+ (alkalosis), decreased temperature, decreased 2,3-DPG; polycythemia, fetal Hb, CO poisoning.
  • Myoglobin: Rectangular hyperbolic curve (left of Hb); 1 Fe++; O2 storage only; releases O2 at very low PO2.

13Carbon Dioxide Transport›

Forms of CO2 Transport

  • Body produces 200 ml CO2/min. Arterial: 48 ml/100ml, PCO2 40 mmHg. Venous: 52 ml/100ml, PCO2 46 mmHg.
  • 1. Physical solution (3 ml): Free CO2 mainly in plasma; depends on PCO2.
  • 2. Bicarbonate (42 ml, 90%): CO2 + H2O -> H2CO3 -> H+ + HCO3- (needs carbonic anhydrase in RBCs).
  • 3. Carbamino compound (3 ml): CO2 + NH2 groups of Hb or plasma protein. Haldane effect: reduced Hb carries more CO2.

Tidal CO2 & Chloride Shift (Hamburger Phenomenon)

  • Tidal CO2 = venous - arterial = 52 - 48 = 4 ml/100ml. Buffered as: 10% physical + 25% carbamino + 65% bicarbonate (via Cl- shift).
  • At tissues: CO2 enters RBCs -> HCO3- + H+; H+ buffered by reduced Hb; HCO3- diffuses to plasma; Cl- enters RBCs (Donnan equilibrium) -> RBCs swell (increased hematocrit in venous blood); pH constant.
  • At lungs: Reverse: HCO3- re-enters RBCs -> CO2 reformed and expired; Cl- returns to plasma.

CO2 Dissociation Curve & Alkali Reserve

  • CO2 dissociation curve: More linear than O2 curve. Physiological curve (A-B line): arterial (PCO2 40 -> 48 ml) to venous (PCO2 46 -> 52 ml). Haldane effect: deoxyHb carries more CO2.
  • Alkali reserve: Volume of CO2 as HCO3- in arterial blood. Normal = 55-70 ml CO2/100ml plasma; HCO3- = 27 mEq/L. Functions: carry CO2, buffer acids, index of acid-base balance.

14Regulation of Respiration›

Respiratory Centers

  • DRG (Dorsal Respiratory Group): Inspiratory center; sends ramp signals to inspiratory muscles.
  • VRG (Ventral Respiratory Group): Active in forced breathing (inspiration + expiration).
  • Pneumotaxic center (pons): Switches OFF inspiratory ramp -> shorter inspiration -> increased RR.
  • Apneustic center (pons): PREVENTS switch-off -> longer inspiration -> decreased RR.
  • Pre-Botzinger complex: Pacemaker cells in medulla; generates rhythmic respiratory discharges; activates phrenic nerve (diaphragm) + external intercostals.
  • Hering-Breuer reflex: Lung stretch -> vagal afferents -> inhibit inspiratory center -> expiration. Prevents over-inflation.

Peripheral & Central Chemoreceptors

  • Peripheral (carotid bodies, aortic bodies): Main stimulus = decreased PO2 (dissolved O2 only; 2000 ml/100g/min blood flow). Also: increased PCO2 (30% of CO2 effect), increased H+, increased K+ (exercise), nicotine. Afferents: glossopharyngeal (carotid) + vagus (aortic).
  • Central (ventrolateral medulla, near CSF): ONLY stimulated by increased PCO2 indirectly (CO2 crosses BBB -> increased H+ in CSF). H+ does NOT cross BBB. Responsible for 70% of ventilatory response to CO2.

Ventilatory Responses

  • Response to decreased PO2: Weaker stimulus (only peripheral receptors). Mild decrease (above 60 mmHg): buffering by reduced Hb + CO2 washout inhibits response. Significant when PO2 below 60 mmHg or central receptors depressed (anesthesia: 100% O2 may be fatal).
  • Response to increased PCO2: Stronger stimulus (central 70% + peripheral 30%). 5% CO2 -> doubles ventilation (carbogen). 10% -> 10x increase. Severe -> CO2 narcosis.
  • Response to increased H+: Metabolic acidosis -> peripheral receptors -> increased ventilation -> CO2 washout (respiratory compensation).

Pulmonary Receptors

  • A. Irritant receptors (rapidly adapting): Larger airway epithelium; respond to irritants (ammonia, smoke, pollen, cold air) -> bronchoconstriction + mucus + cough. Implicated in asthma.
  • B. J-receptors (juxtapulmonary capillary): Unmyelinated C-fibres in alveolar walls; respond to lung inflation, injury, vascular congestion, chemicals -> bronchoconstriction + shallow breathing.
  • C. Stretch receptors: Myelinated fibres in smooth muscle of conducting airways; respond to lung inflation -> terminate inspiration (Hering-Breuer reflex) -> prolong expiration.
  • D. Joint and muscle receptors: Chest wall + limb joints; sense wall movement + effort -> increased ventilation during exercise.

15Respiratory Disorders (Hypoxia, Cyanosis & Caisson's Disease)›

Hypoxia - Definition & 4 Types

  • Definition: O2 deficiency at tissue level (hypoxaemia = O2 deficiency in blood).
  • 1. Hypoxic hypoxia (cyanosis PRESENT): Decreased available O2. Causes: decreased PO2 in inspired air (high altitude); hypoventilation (morphine, poliomyelitis, asthma, emphysema, pneumothorax); diffusion block (fibrosis, pneumonia, congestion); abnormal V/Q (CHD shunt, emphysema).
  • 2. Anemic hypoxia (cyanosis ABSENT): Decreased functioning Hb; normal arterial PO2 but decreased O2 content. Causes: quantitative (anaemia); qualitative (carboxyHb, metHb).
  • 3. Stagnant hypoxia (cyanosis PRESENT): Normal O2 + Hb but decreased blood flow -> decreased venous PO2. Generalized: heart failure, haemorrhagic shock, polycythemia. Localized: Raynaud disease, thrombus.
  • 4. Histotoxic hypoxia (cyanosis ABSENT): Normal arterial O2; increased venous O2 (tissue cannot use it). Causes: cyanide (inhibits cytochrome oxidase; Rx: methylene blue/nitrites -> metHb -> cyano-metHb non-toxic); alcohol + narcotics (block dehydrogenase).

Q7: Describe role of baroreceptors in regulation of ABP

Q13: Explain delayed compensatory mechanisms of hemorrhage

  • I. Restoration of plasma volume (12–72 h): ADH → water retention & ↑ thirst sensation → water intake → ↑ plasma volume | capillary shift mechanism.

Q14: Define shock and describe its different types

  • Definition: Inadequate tissue perfusion with blood due to progressive decrease in COP and ABP.

    → Anaphylactic: antigen-antibody reaction → histamine release → VD & ↓ ABP.
    → Septic: bacterial endotoxins depress vasomotor center → VD & ↓ ABP progressively.
  • 2. Hypovolemic (cold) shock: Blood volume decreased — trauma, hemorrhage, burn, dehydration.
  • 3. Cardiogenic shock: Infarction, heart failure or arrhythmia.
  • 4. Obstructive shock: Obstruction of blood flow at center of circulation (e.g. pulmonary embolism).

Q15: Describe prognosis of shock

  • Reversible (compensated) shock: Immediate & delayed compensatory mechanisms gradually restore normal ABP → negative feedback control.
  • Irreversible (refractory) shock: Severe cases not treated for 3–5 hours → progressive ↓ COP & ABP → positive feedback mechanism.

  • 2. Precapillary sphincter dilatation (venules remain constricted → ↓ VR + plasma loss)
  • 3. Metabolic acidosis
  • 4. Acute respiratory failure
  • 5. Acute renal failure
  • 6. Thrombosis of small vessels
  • 7. Release of toxins by ischemic tissues

1Smooth Muscle (LM & EM)›

Overview

  • Also called: visceral / unstriated / plain muscle. Involuntary.
  • Location: walls of blood vessels, GIT, respiratory, urinary, reproductive tracts, iris.

LM Features (6 points)

  • 1. Shape: elongated, tapering (spindle-shaped), unstriated.
  • 2. External lamina: each cell enclosed by external lamina + type I and III collagen (endomysium).
  • 3. Size: 20 μm (small vessels) → 500 μm (pregnant uterus).
  • 4. Nucleus: single, elongated, central.
  • 5. Gap junctions: numerous → functional syncytium → coordinated contraction.
  • 6. On contraction: borders become scalloped (wavy); nucleus becomes distorted/corkscrew-shaped.
📌 NO cross-striations (no sarcomere pattern) because thick + thin filaments are arranged OBLIQUELY — not in parallel arrays as in striated muscle.

EM Features (7 points)

  • 1. Organelles near nucleus: mitochondria + polyribosomes + RER + Golgi.
  • 2. Caveolae: numerous plasmalemma invaginations at cell surface; analogous to T-tubules (Ca²⁺ entry).
  • 3. Sarcoplasmic reticulum: rudimentary (poorly developed). NO T-tubules.
  • 4. Myofilaments: thin (actin) + thick (myosin) arranged obliquely → lattice-like network.
  • 5. Myosin: fewer crossbridges + less regular arrangement than striated muscle.
  • 6. Dense bodies: cytoplasmic + plasmalemmal; contain α-actinin = equivalent of Z-discs; anchor actin filaments.
  • 7. Intermediate filaments: desmin (visceral SM) or vimentin (vascular SM) → connect to dense bodies.
📌 Contraction: Ca²⁺ → calmodulin → CaM-Ca → MLCK → MLC phosphorylation → crossbridge cycling. No troponin in smooth muscle.

2Trachea to End of Chapter (p.120–134)›

Trachea — 4 Layers

  • 1. Mucosa: Respiratory epithelium; lamina propria (loose CT + BVs + lymphoid elements + serous & mucous glands); elastic lamina.
  • 2. Submucosa: Subepithelial CT with elastic fibers; serous glands (keep epithelium moist) + mucous glands (trap dust); mucus swept toward larynx by cilia; lymphoid aggregations present.
  • 3. Cartilage: 10–12 C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings; open posteriorly, completed by ligaments (prevent over-distension) + trachealis muscle (regulates diameter).
  • 4. Adventitia: Fibroelastic CT with BVs & nerves.

Bronchial Tree

  • Trachea → 2 primary extrapulmonary bronchi → secondary bronchi (right ×3, left ×2) → tertiary/segmental → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles.
  • Extrapulmonary bronchus: Similar to trachea.
  • Intrapulmonary bronchus: Respiratory epithelium (few goblet cells) + glands; spirally arranged SM; irregular hyaline cartilage plates; thin adventitia. Cartilage ↓ with branching.

Bronchioles (<1 mm, no cartilage, no glands)

  • Epithelium: columnar ciliated → cuboidal ciliated → cuboidal non-ciliated. Terminal bronchioles (<0.5 mm): cuboidal + Clara cells.
  • Clara cells: Dome-shaped apices, secretory granules, abundant organelles. Functions: (1) surfactant-like substance, (2) antimicrobial peptides/cytokines, (3) stem cells.
  • Musculosa: spirally arranged SM + fibroelastic CT (maintains patency).

Respiratory Portion

  • Respiratory bronchioles: Incomplete wall (opens to alveoli); cuboidal + Clara cells; 1–2 SM layers. Branch → alveolar ducts.
  • Alveolar ducts: Linear arrangement of alveoli; type I pneumocytes; SM at rims → disappears. End as alveolar sacs.
  • Alveoli (~300 million, ~200 μm): Communicate via pores of Kohn (equalise pressure; collateral air flow if bronchiole blocked).
  • Type I pneumocyte: Flat, cover 95% surface; very thin; occluding junctions (prevent fluid entry).
  • Type II pneumocyte: Cuboidal, cover 3–5%; lamellar bodies (surfactant). Functions: secrete surfactant, recycle surfactant, replace type I cells.
  • Alveolar macrophages: Dust cells (dust) + heart failure cells (escaped RBCs).

Blood-Air Barrier & Septa

  • Blood-Air Barrier: (1) Surfactant → (2) Type I pneumocyte → (3) Fused basal laminae → (4) Continuous capillary endothelium.
  • Inter-alveolar septum: Lined both sides by alveolar epithelium; thick (capillaries + CT cells) or thin (capillaries only); NO smooth muscle in alveolar walls or septa.
  • Pleura: Flat mesothelial cells on loose CT (elastic fibers + BVs + nerves + lymphatics).

3Thymus›

1. General Characters of Thymus

  • Primary lymphatic organ — site of T-lymphocyte maturation.
  • Lies posterior to upper part of mediastinum, extends over great vessels of heart.
  • Small encapsulated organ composed of two lobes.
  • Continues to grow until puberty → then involutes (atrophy) and becomes infiltrated by adipose tissue.

2. Histological Structure — Stroma

  • Capsule: dense irregular collagen fibers.
  • Trabeculae (septa): divide lobes into incomplete lobules → each lobule = outer dark cortex + inner pale medulla. Lobules are continuous with each other.
  • Reticular network: NO reticular fibers (unlike other lymphoid organs) → framework formed by processes of epithelial reticular cells.

3. Histological Structure — Parenchyma (Thymic Cells)

  • A. Outer dark cortex: large number of immunoincompetent T-lymphocytes + epithelial reticular cells. T-lymphocytes migrate from bone marrow → periphery of cortex → proliferate → become immunocompetent.
  • B. Inner pale medulla: few mature T-lymphocytes | Macrophages | Epithelial reticular cells | Hassall's corpuscles. T-lymphocytes leave medulla via fenestrated blood capillaries → thymus-dependent zones.
  • Also present: Macrophages | Epithelial reticular cells (in both cortex and medulla).
  • Thymocytes NOT organized in lymphatic nodules — distributed between processes of reticular cells.

4. Hassall's Corpuscles

  • Pale whorl-shaped bodies present only in the medulla of thymus.
  • Formed of concentric layers of large pale-stained reticular cells that coalesce around each other.
  • Some cells degenerate and calcify.
  • Number increases with age.
  • Function: unknown.

5. Epithelial Reticular Cells — LM & Sites

  • Differ from CT reticular cells → cannot synthesize reticular fibers.
  • LM:
    Shape → highly branched cell with long wide sheath-like processes joined by desmosomes + occluding junctions.
    Nucleus → large pale + little heterochromatin.
    Cytoplasm → pale, contains many tonofilaments.
  • 3 Sites in thymus:
    1. Around cortex → separates it from capsule, trabeculae and blood vessels.
    2. Mid-cortex → subdivides cortex into small compartments filled with lymphocytes.
    3. Cortico-medullary junction (CMJ) → separates cortex from medulla.
  • Functions:
    1. Prevent developing T cells from contact with foreign antigens (blood-thymus barrier).
    2. Act as antigen-presenting cells to present antigens to developing T-lymphocytes.

6. Blood-Thymus Barrier

  • Definition: Barrier that separates cortex of thymus (containing immature T-lymphocytes) from contact with bloodborne antigens.
  • Components (EMPBPE):
    1. Continuous Endothelium of cortical blood capillaries
    2. Pericytes
    3. Thick Basal lamina of cortical capillaries
    4. Macrophages
    5. Epithelial reticular cells and their processes

7. Functions of Thymus

  • Acts as lymphoid organ AND endocrine gland.
  • 1. Lymphoid: Proliferation of mature T-lymphocytes → supply blood and thymus-dependent zones of peripheral tissues.
  • 2. Endocrine: Secretion of hormones and cytokines (thymulin, thymopoietin, thymosins) → regulate T-lymphocyte maturation, proliferation and function within thymus and other lymphoid tissues.

8. Thymus-Dependent Zones

  • 1. Paracortical zone of lymph nodes.
  • 2. Periarterial lymphatic sheath (PALS) in white pulp of spleen.
  • 3. Isolated lymphoid tissue in Peyer's patches in small intestine.

9. Thymus vs. Other Lymphoid Organs

  • Has NO: lymphatic nodules | afferent lymphatic vessels | plasma cells | reticular fibers.
  • Has: Hassall's corpuscles.
  • Concerned with: maturation of T-lymphocytes.

  • Secondary lymphatic organ; kidney/oval-shaped; along lymph vessels (neck, axilla, groin, body cavities).

  • Spleen = LARGEST secondary lymphoid organ. Responds to BLOOD-BORNE antigens.

1Cardiac & Fatty Acid Metabolism›

Cardiac Metabolism

  • Highest metabolic demands of any organ; requires continuous ATP for contractile function.
  • Large numbers of mitochondria for aerobic ATP production; limited energy stores.
  • 3 carbon substrates: fatty acids, carbohydrates, ketone bodies.
  • Fuel sources: FFA predominant (60–70% postabsorptive); ketone bodies (fasting/low glucose → acetyl-CoA → Krebs); glucose (after CHO meal); lactate (exercise).
  • During ischemia/hypoxia: glycogen → anaerobic ATP + lactic acid; limited glycogen rapidly depleted.

Sources of Fatty Acids

  • Diet + endogenous biosynthesis (excess protein & carbohydrate → FA → stored as TG).
  • Oxidation of fats: TG → FA + glycerol (by hormone-sensitive lipase). FFA + albumin in blood → transported to tissues → passive diffusion → β-oxidation.
  • 3 mechanisms of FA oxidation: β-oxidation (main), α-oxidation, ω-oxidation.

β-Oxidation of Fatty Acids

  • Definition: Oxidation at β-carbon → acetyl-CoA → Krebs cycle.
  • Site: Mitochondrial matrix — liver, heart, skeletal muscle (NOT brain).
  • Step I — Activation (cytoplasm): FA + CoA + ATP → Acyl-CoA (by acyl-CoA synthetase). Uses 2 high-energy phosphate bonds.
  • Step II — Carnitine Shuttle (long-chain FA only):
    1. CPT-I (outer mitochondrial membrane): Acyl-CoA + carnitine → Acylcarnitine [rate-limiting].
    2. Carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase (inner membrane antiporter): Acylcarnitine IN ↔ Carnitine OUT.
    3. CPT-II (inside inner membrane): Acylcarnitine + CoA → Acyl-CoA (regenerated).
    NB: FA <12C enter mitochondria without carnitine.
  • Step III — β-Oxidation Spiral (4 steps, remove 2C/cycle):
    1. Dehydrogenation (acyl-CoA DH; FAD→FADH₂) → Δ²-trans-enoyl-CoA [IRREVERSIBLE].
    2. Hydration (enoyl-CoA hydratase; +H₂O) → L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA.
    3. Dehydrogenation (3-hydroxyacyl-CoA DH; NAD⁺→NADH) → 3-ketoacyl-CoA.
    4. Thiolysis (thiolase; +CoA) → acetyl-CoA + shorter acyl-CoA (−2C).
    Cycle repeats until complete breakdown to acetyl-CoA.

Carnitine — Sources & Deficiencies

  • Endogenous synthesis: Lysine + methionine → liver + kidney (needs Vit C + Fe²⁺).
  • Dietary: meat products.
  • Carnitine deficiency: Prematurity, malnutrition, vegetarians (↓intake); liver disease (↓synthesis) → muscle weakness, heart + kidney affected; FA accumulation.
  • CPT-I deficiency (liver only): ↓FA oxidation + ↓ketogenesis → fasting hypoglycemia. Sulfonylurea drugs inhibit CPT-I.
  • CPT-II deficiency (skeletal muscle only): Myoglobinuria + myopathy after exercise.

Regulation of β-Oxidation

  • ↑β-oxidation: DM + starvation → ↓insulin → ↑anti-insulin hormones → ↑lipolysis → ↑FFA → ↑β-oxidation → ↑ketogenesis.
  • ↓β-oxidation: Fed state → ↑insulin → ↑malonyl-CoA (FA synthesis product) → inhibits CPT-I → ↓FA entry into mitochondria.

Energy Gain from β-Oxidation

  • Palmitate (16C) example: 7 cycles → 8 acetyl-CoA + 7 FADH₂ + 7 NADH.
  • FADH₂ → 2 ATP; NADH → 3 ATP; per cycle = 5 ATP × 7 = 35 ATP.
  • 8 acetyl-CoA × 12 ATP (Krebs) = 96 ATP.
  • Total = 96 + 35 = 131 ATP; Net = 131 − 2 (activation) = 129 ATP.
  • Formulas: cycles = (C/2)−1; acetyl-CoA = C/2.

Odd-Number FA & Ketone Bodies

  • Odd-number FA: β-oxidation → acetyl-CoA + propionyl-CoA → succinyl-CoA.
  • Methylmalonyl aciduria: Excretion of methylmalonyl-CoA in urine due to Vit B₁₂ deficiency → neurological manifestations.
  • Ketone bodies: Acetoacetic acid, β-hydroxybutyric acid, acetone. Properties: water-soluble, freely mobile, cross blood-brain barrier, efficient metabolic fuel.

2Metabolism of Cholesterol›

Biosynthesis of Cholesterol

  • Site: Cytoplasm (microsomal + cytosolic fractions).
  • Substrate: Acetyl-CoA.
  • Steps: (1) 2 Acetyl-CoA → acetoacetyl-CoA. (2) + Acetyl-CoA (HMG-CoA synthase) → HMG-CoA. (3) HMG-CoA reductase + NADPH → mevalonic acid → isoprene unit. (4) 6 isoprene units → squalene → cholesterol.
  • Rate-limiting enzyme: HMG-CoA reductase.

Regulation of Cholesterol Synthesis

  • Hormonal: Active form = dephosphorylated. Insulin + carbohydrate feeding → activate. Glucagon + fasting → inhibit.
  • Feedback: Mevalonate + cholesterol → inhibit HMG-CoA reductase.
  • Drugs (statins): Lovastatin, mevastatin, atorvastatin → competitive inhibition → ↓cholesterol → ↑LDL receptors.

Blood Cholesterol & Esterification

  • Normal level: 150–220 mg/dl. Hypercholesterolemia >240 mg/dl.
  • 70% esterified + 30% free. 25% on HDL; 75% on LDL.
  • Atherogenic index: LDL/HDL >4 → predisposes to atherosclerosis.
  • Esterification in plasma: LCAT (in HDL). In tissue: ACAT. Cholesterol esters metabolized faster; do NOT precipitate in vascular endothelium.

Functions of Cholesterol

  • 1. Cell membrane: Component of plasma + cell membrane lipoproteins (modulates fluidity).
  • 2. Steroid hormones: Precursor of androgens, estrogens, progesterone, corticoids.
  • 3. Vitamin D₃: Cholesterol → 7-dehydrocholesterol → (UV/skin) → cholecalciferol → calcitriol.
  • 4. Bile acids: Primary (liver): cholic acid + chenodeoxycholic acid. Secondary (intestinal bacteria): deoxycholic + lithocholic acid. Excreted as bile salts (Na-glycocholate, Na-taurocholate). Key enzyme: 7α-hydroxylase (feedback inhibited by bile salts; stimulated by thyroid hormones).
  • Functions of bile salts: Emulsification of lipids; activation of pancreatic lipase; absorption of lipids + fat-soluble vitamins; keep biliary cholesterol soluble.

Excretion & Enterohepatic Circulation

  • ~1g cholesterol excreted/day: 50% as bile acids + 50% as cholesterol.
  • Intestinal bacteria reduce cholesterol → coprostanol (principal fecal sterol).
  • Enterohepatic circulation: Bile acids excreted in bile → partly reabsorbed → portal blood → liver → re-excreted. Main fecal bile acid = lithocholic acid (least soluble).

Causes of Hypercholesterolemia

  • 1. Diet rich in carbohydrates, saturated FA, and cholesterol.
  • 2. Diabetes mellitus (↑synthesis + ↓clearance by LPL).
  • 3. Hypothyroidism (thyroxine stimulates cholesterol → bile acids).
  • 4. Obesity (associated with hyperlipoproteinemia).
  • 5. Obstructive jaundice (↓excretion of cholesterol + bile acids).
  • 6. Nephrosis (unknown mechanism).
  • 7. Familial hypercholesterolemia (type II hyperlipoproteinemia — LDL receptor defect).
  • 8. Coffee + cigarette smoking (↑FFA mobilization → ↑plasma lipoproteins).

3Free Radicals & Antioxidants›

Free Radicals — Definition & Types

  • Definition: Particles with an unpaired electron in outer orbital → highly reactive → steal electrons from nearest molecule → chain reactions.
  • ROS (Reactive Oxygen Species): Superoxide O₂·⁻, Hydroxyl ·OH, Peroxyl ROO·, Alkoxyl RO·, Hydroperoxyl HO₂·.
  • RNS (Reactive Nitrogen Species): Nitric oxide NO·, Nitrous oxide N₂O.

Sources of Free Radicals

  • Endogenous: (1) Mitochondrial respiratory chain (mainly superoxide → H₂O₂). (2) Cytochrome P450 (xenobiotic metabolism). (3) NADPH oxidase of leukocytes (superoxide). (4) Hb → metHb oxidation. (5) L-amino acid oxidase + xanthine oxidase.
  • Exogenous: Smoking, irradiation, pollution.

Physiological Functions of Free Radicals

  • 1. Bactericidal: NADPH oxidase of leukocytes → superoxide; myeloperoxidase: H₂O₂ + Cl⁻ + H⁺ → HClO + H₂O (kills bacteria in phagocytic vacuole).
  • 2. Cell signaling: NO + other radicals → important in apoptosis of DNA-damaged cells.

Dangerous Effects of Free Radicals

  • 1. Cell damage: Peroxidation of PUFA in cell membranes.
  • 2. Cancer: Mutations in DNA caused by free radicals.
  • 3. Atherosclerosis: Oxidation of LDL → foam cells.
  • 4. Aging.
  • 5. Cataract: Oxidative stress → oxidation of lens proteins + lipids.
  • 6. Myocardial infarction.

Antioxidant Defense System

  • Definition: Substances that scavenge free radicals (act as electron donors).
  • Classification:
  • I. Preventive antioxidants (prevent initiation):
    • Catalase: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. High affinity for H₂O₂; present in peroxisomes, hepatocytes, mitochondria, RBC cytoplasm. Acts on 2 H₂O₂ simultaneously; NO hydrogen donor needed.
    • Peroxidase (GPx): Uses glutathione (GSH) as H-donor; needs NADPH for GSH regeneration.
    • Metal-chelating proteins: Transferrin, ceruloplasmin (bind Fe/Cu → prevent Fenton reaction).
  • II. Chain-breaking antioxidants (prevent propagation):
    • SOD (Superoxide dismutase): 2O₂·⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂; present in all O₂-metabolizing cells.
    • Vitamin E (tocopherol): Fat-soluble; H-donor to peroxyl radicals of PUFA → breaks chain.
    • Vitamin C (ascorbate): Water-soluble; regenerates Vitamin E.
  • Exogenous antioxidants (nutrients): Vitamin C, Vitamin E, carotenoids.
  • Endogenous antioxidants: Bilirubin, uric acid, glutathione, N-acetyl cysteine + antioxidant enzymes (SOD, catalase, peroxidase).

📌 Topic 1: Fatty Acid Oxidation

Q1: Describe carnitine shuttle

  • Occurs in the mitochondria — inner membrane is impermeable to acyl CoA.
  • Cell uses carnitine as carrier of acyl group across the membrane.
  • Sources of carnitine: Dietary (meat products) | Endogenous from lysine & methionine in liver and kidney.
  • Transport depends on 3 enzymes:
    1. CPT I: in outer mitochondrial membrane — converts long chain acyl CoA to acylcarnitine.
    2. Carnitine acylcarnitine translocase: inner membrane exchange transporter.
    3. CPT II: on inside of inner membrane — catalyzes reaction between acylcarnitine and CoA to regenerate acyl CoA.

Q2: Describe carnitine deficiency

  • Occurs in: prematures, malnutrition, vegetarians (inadequate intake) | liver diseases (↓ biosynthesis).
  • Leads to muscle weakness during long-term exercise (when FA are important energy source).
  • Heart & kidney also affected — use FA for majority of their energy supply.
  • Symptoms: mild muscle cramps → severe weakness → even death.
  • CPT-1 deficiency: Affects only liver → ↓ FA oxidation & ketogenesis & hypoglycemia.
  • CPT-2 deficiency: Affects skeletal muscle.

Q3: Describe regulation of β-oxidation

  • ↑ when body unable to oxidize glucose — as in DM and starvation.
  • Starvation → ↓ blood glucose → ↑ anti-insulin hormones → ↑ lipolysis → ↑ β-oxidation.
  • Insulin and feeding carbohydrates → inhibit β-oxidation (both ↑ glucose oxidation & ↓ FA release).

Q5: Describe metabolism of VLDL

  • Site of synthesis: Liver | Function: Carries lipids from liver to peripheral tissues.
  • Lipid: 90% | Protein: 10% | Main lipid: Triacylglycerols (endogenous — synthesized from excess carbohydrates).
  • Nascent VLDL (Apo B100) synthesized by liver → in plasma receives Apo CII & Apo E from HDL → Mature VLDL.
  • LPL (activated by Apo CII) → hydrolyzes TAG → FA + Glycerol → Apo CII & E return to HDL → IDL (B100 + TAG + E).
  • IDL → hepatic lipase → LDL (B100 + CE only) | 30% taken by liver | 70% by extra-hepatic tissues via LDL receptor.

Q6: Describe function of HDL

  • 1. Reverse Cholesterol Transport — carries cholesterol from tissues to liver (Good Cholesterol) → Anti-atherogenic agent.
  • 2. Esterification of cholesterol by LCAT.
  • 3. Reservoir of Apo CII and Apo E for other lipoproteins.

Q7: Describe oxidized LDL

  • Oxidation of LDL occurs when LDL-cholesterol particles react with free radicals → Ox-LDL becomes more reactive with surrounding tissue → tissues damage.
  • Factors which increase LDL-oxidation: 1. Metabolic syndrome | 2. Uncontrolled diabetes | 3. Smoking | 4. Diet rich in trans fats.
  • Oxidized LDL taken by macrophages → converted into foam cells → central role in atherosclerosis.
  • HDL inhibits oxidation of LDL via: Paraoxonase enzyme | PAF-acetyl hydrolase enzyme.

Q9: Describe regulation of fatty acid synthesis

  • 1. Allosteric regulation: Citrate (indicator of plentiful supply of acetyl-CoA) → activates ACC | Long chain FA → inhibit ACC.
  • 2. Hormonal regulation: Controlled by cAMP-mediated phosphorylation–dephosphorylation mechanism — phosphorylated = less active | dephosphorylated = active.
  • 3. ACC: Activated by insulin & citrate | Inhibited by glucagon, epinephrine & long chain FA.
