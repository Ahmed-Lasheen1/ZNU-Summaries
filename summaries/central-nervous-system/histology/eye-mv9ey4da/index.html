Histology
Eye

Quick Overview

The eye is a complex, highly developed photosensitive organ that analyzes the form, light intensity and color of objects. This lesson covers the three layers of the eyeball wall (outer fibrous, middle vascular pigmented, inner nervous), the chambers, lens and refractive media, the ten-layered retina with its photoreceptors, and the accessory structures: conjunctiva and eyelids.

Complete Lesson Content

Layers of the eyeball wall

The eyeball wall has three layers:

  1. Outer fibrous layer
  2. Middle vascular pigmented layer (uveal tract)
  3. Inner nervous layer (retina)

I. Outer fibrous layer

Dense white fibrous connective tissue predominates. It has two parts: the cornea and the sclera.

A. Cornea

The colorless, transparent, avascular anterior one-sixth of the fibrous layer. It has five layers from outside inward:

LayerKey features
1. Corneal epitheliumStratified squamous non-keratinized; rich in free nerve endings; always moist with tears; regenerates rapidly after abrasion.
2. Bowman's membraneAbout 10 μm thick, structureless and acellular; network of fine type I collagen fibers; injury results in corneal opacity.
3. Substantia propriaAbout 90% of corneal thickness; parallel lamellae of type I collagen; flattened stellate fibroblasts (corneal corpuscles) lie between the collagen bundles.
4. Descemet's membrane5–7 μm thick; basal lamina of the corneal endothelial cells; homogeneous (fine collagenous fibers in ground substance); regenerates rapidly after injury.
5. EndotheliumSimple squamous epithelium; its organelles support protein synthesis for forming and maintaining Descemet's membrane; engaged in metabolic exchanges that maintain corneal transparency.

Corneal transparency and nutrition

Transparency depends on avascularity and on the regular arrangement of connective tissue cells and fibers, which share the same refractive index. Nutrition reaches the cornea by diffusion from the aqueous humor and from limbal blood vessels. Oxygen for the epithelium comes directly from the atmosphere, and the anterior surface is kept wet by tears.

B. Sclera

The "white of the eye", forming the posterior 5/6 of the fibrous layer. It is composed of flat bundles of type I collagen running parallel to the surface but in different directions.

Corneoscleral junction (limbus)

The transition zone between cornea and sclera; a highly vascularized, ring-like structure. Changes at the limbus:

  • The corneal epithelium thickens to ten or more layers and becomes continuous with the conjunctiva.
  • Bowman's membrane ends abruptly and is replaced by the stroma of the conjunctiva.
  • The regular collagen bundles of the substantia propria become the irregular bundles of the sclera.
  • Descemet's membrane splits into the trabeculae of the pectinate ligament, forming the trabecular meshwork (spaces of Fontana).
  • The corneal endothelium stops abruptly.

II. Middle vascular pigmented layer (uveal tract)

Consists of three parts: choroid, ciliary body and iris.

1. Choroid

Highly vascularized loose connective tissue containing numerous melanocytes, resting on an amorphous hyaline membrane, Bruch's membrane. It absorbs light and supplies nutrition to the outer portion of the retina.

2. Ciliary body

An anterior expansion of the choroid, anterior to the ora serrata (the boundary between retina and ciliary body). It is composed of a stroma containing smooth muscle (ciliary muscle) and is covered by pigmented epithelium. Functions:

  • The pigmented epithelium absorbs light.
  • Secretes the aqueous humor.
  • Helps fix the lens in position.
  • Contraction of ciliary muscle fibers helps in accommodation.

3. Iris

Has a central opening, the pupil, and divides the space between cornea and lens into anterior and posterior chambers. Its layers are highly vascular and rich in melanocytes, which determine the color of the iris.

Chambers, lens and refractive media

  • Anterior chamber: bounded anteriorly by the cornea and posteriorly by the iris and ciliary body.
  • Posterior chamber: bounded anteriorly by the iris, posteriorly by the lens and suspensory ligament, and laterally by the ciliary processes.
  • Vitreous space: between the posterior surface of the lens and the neural retina; occupied by the clear, jelly-like vitreous body with few cells (hyalocytes).
  • Lens: an elastic, biconvex, transparent structure behind the iris and in front of the vitreous body.

Refractive media (all transparent structures light passes through to reach the retina): cornea, aqueous humor of the anterior chamber, aqueous humor of the posterior chamber, lens, and vitreous body.

III. Inner nervous layer (retina)

The retina has two portions: the photosensitive region lining the inner eye posterior to the ora serrata, and the non-photosensitive region anterior to the ora serrata, lining the ciliary body and the posterior surface of the iris. The neural retina contains three orders of neurons with associated short neurons and neuroglial cells, arranged in 10 layers from the choroid inward:

LayerContents / features
1. Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)Simple cuboidal epithelium on Bruch's membrane (details below).
2. Layer of rods and conesOuter segments of photoreceptor cells, the first order neurons.
3. Outer limiting membraneNot a true membrane; a zone of adhesion (tight junctions) between photoreceptors and Müller cells.
4. Outer nuclear layerNuclei of rods and cones.
5. Outer plexiform layerSynapses between axons of rods and cones (first order) and dendrites of bipolar cells (second order) and horizontal cells.
6. Inner nuclear layerNuclei of bipolar cells (second order neurons), amacrine, horizontal and Müller cells.
7. Inner plexiform layerAxons of bipolar cells synapse with branched dendrites of ganglion cells; short amacrine cells synapse here.
8. Ganglion cell layerCell bodies of ganglion cells (third order neurons), whose axons form the next layer.
9. Layer of optic nerve fibersUnmyelinated axons of ganglion cells; myelin is added as the nerve pierces the sclera.
10. Inner limiting membraneBasal lamina of Müller cells, separating the retina from the vitreous.

Müller cells are functionally analogous to neuroglia: they support, nourish and insulate retinal neurons and fibers.

Pigment epithelium layer

  • Basal plasmalemma shows numerous invaginations with abundant mitochondria nearby (ion-transport activity); lateral plasmalemma shows zonula occludens, zonula adherens, desmosomes and gap junctions.
  • Apical surface has long microvilli projecting between the rods and cones; nuclei are basal and rounded.
  • Cytoplasm contains melanin granules, mitochondria, RER, abundant SER (site of vitamin A formation), Golgi, lysosomes and residual bodies.
  • Functions: absorption of light; phagocytosis of the tips of rod cells during turnover; storage and release of vitamin A (rhodopsin formation).

Rods

  • More numerous than cones (about 120 million in the human retina); sensitive to dim light.
  • Four parts: outer segment, inner segment, nuclear region, synaptic region.
  • Outer segment: photosensitive, rod-shaped; a series of uniform flattened membranous discs formed by plasmalemma invaginations, containing rhodopsin. The discs are phagocytosed by the pigment epithelium.
  • Inner segment: joined to the outer segment by a connecting stalk containing a modified cilium (nine peripheral microtubule doublets, no central singlet); rich in mitochondria, polysomes, SER, RER, Golgi and glycogen.
  • Nuclear region: contains the nucleus. Synaptic region: the inner rod fiber ends in rod spherules that synapse with the next neuron.

Cones

  • Fewer than rods (about 7 million); similar in structure, but the outer segment is conical with fewer membranous lamellae.
  • The lamellae are continuous with the plasmalemma and permanent; they are not phagocytosed by the pigment epithelium.
  • Sensitive to bright light and color vision; photopigment is iodopsin.
  • The inner cone fiber ends in an expanded pedicle that synapses with the second order neuron.
  • Each cone synapses with one bipolar neuron, whereas more than one rod synapses with one bipolar neuron.

Specialized areas of the retina

  • Macula lutea (yellow spot) / fovea centralis: central retina opposite the center of the lens, with yellow pigment. Formed only of cones; greatest visual acuity and sharpest color discrimination.
  • Optic disc and papilla (blind spot): all retinal layers except the optic nerve fibers cease abruptly at its margin. The absence of photoreceptors accounts for the term "blind spot".

Accessory structures of the eye

Conjunctiva

A thin, transparent mucous membrane.

  • Bulbar conjunctiva: covers the sclera; stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium with lamina propria.
  • Palpebral conjunctiva: covers the inner surface of the eyelid; stratified columnar epithelium with numerous goblet cells; lamina propria of loose connective tissue.

Eyelids

Movable tissue folds that protect the eye. Layers:

  • Skin: thin, with few fine hairs and sweat and sebaceous glands; delicate dermis rich in elastic fibers. At the lid margin the dermis contains 3–4 rows of long, stiff hairs (eyelashes) without erector pili muscles.
  • Orbicularis oculi: striated muscle supplied by the 7th cranial nerve; closes the lids.
  • Tarsus: dense fibrous plate containing the Meibomian (tarsal) gland, a modified sebaceous gland secreting an oily lubricant that prevents rapid evaporation of tears.
  • Palpebral conjunctiva: covers the inner surface of the eyelid.

Key Points / Quick Review

Wall of the eyeball

  • Three layers: outer fibrous, middle vascular pigmented (uveal), inner nervous (retina).

Cornea

  • Avascular anterior 1/6; five layers: epithelium, Bowman's, substantia propria (90%), Descemet's, endothelium.
  • Bowman's injury causes opacity; Descemet's is the endothelial basal lamina.
  • Oxygen for epithelium from the atmosphere.

Sclera and limbus

  • Sclera: posterior 5/6, irregular type I collagen.
  • Limbus: Descemet's splits into pectinate trabeculae (spaces of Fontana); Bowman's and endothelium end.

Uveal tract

  • Choroid: vascular, melanocytes, Bruch's membrane; nourishes outer retina.
  • Ciliary body: secretes aqueous humor, fixes lens, accommodation.
  • Iris: pupil; separates anterior and posterior chambers.

Refractive media

  • Cornea, aqueous humor (both chambers), lens, vitreous body.

Retina

  • 10 layers from RPE to inner limiting membrane; three neuron orders: photoreceptor, bipolar, ganglion.
  • RPE: absorbs light, phagocytoses rod tips, stores vitamin A.
  • Müller cells support and insulate; form the inner limiting membrane.

Rods vs. cones

  • Rods: about 120 million, dim light, rhodopsin, discs phagocytosed.
  • Cones: about 7 million, bright light and color, iodopsin, permanent lamellae, one cone per bipolar cell.

Special retinal areas

  • Fovea: cones only, greatest acuity.
  • Optic disc: no photoreceptors, blind spot.

Accessory structures

  • Bulbar conjunctiva: stratified squamous; palpebral: stratified columnar with goblet cells.
  • Eyelid: skin, orbicularis oculi (CN VII), tarsus with Meibomian glands, palpebral conjunctiva.
