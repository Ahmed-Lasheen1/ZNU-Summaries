The thermoregulatory system

The system has three parts: sensory receptors (thermoreceptors), a central integrator (center) and effector organs.

Thermoreceptors

  • Peripheral receptors: the skin has both cold and warmth receptors, with more cold receptors. Deep body receptors in the abdominal viscera and spinal cord detect core temperature.
  • Central receptors: the anterior hypothalamus and preoptic area contain many heat-sensitive and cold-sensitive neurons, sensitive to core (brain and blood) temperature.
Skin receptors
Lateral spinothalamic tract
Thalamus and somatosensory cortex
Hypothalamic heat center

Center and effectors

The hypothalamic thermostat compares the incoming impulses with its reference temperature, the set-point, almost 37.1°C core temperature. The effectors are the skin (blood vessels and sweat glands), skeletal muscle and endocrine glands.

Body temp above set-point
Anterior hypothalamus stimulated: heat loss
Body temp below set-point
Posterior hypothalamus stimulated: more heat production, less heat loss

Exposure to cold

Cold stimulates the posterior nuclei of the hypothalamus, which regulate heat balance in two ways.

Decrease heat loss

  • Skin vasoconstriction: posterior hypothalamus stimulates the medullary vasoconstrictor center, via sympathetic adrenergic fibers
  • Piloerection acts as an insulating layer (little effect in humans)
  • Counter-current heat exchange: heat passes from warm arterial blood to cold venous blood in deep veins
  • Behavior: heavy clothes, curling the body

Increase heat production

  • Shivering
  • Hormonal thermogenesis
  • Behavior: increased appetite to increase SDA

Shivering is involuntary rhythmic contraction of skeletal muscle. Its primary motor center is in the posterior hypothalamus, which stimulates extrapyramidal tracts and anterior horn cells to raise muscle tone.

High-yield

Shivering can be prevented by curare, a neuromuscular blocker.

Adrenaline
Raises metabolic rate, cutaneous vasoconstriction, glycogenolysis, lipolysis
Thyroxine
Raises BMR slowly (high incidence of toxic goiters in cold climates)
Cortisol
Raises blood glucose and metabolic rate

Exposure to heat

Heat stimulates the anterior nuclei of the hypothalamus. Heat gain falls through strong inhibition of heat-producing mechanisms and through apathy (less activity) and anorexia. Heat loss rises through vasodilatation and sweating.

Skin vasodilatation

  • Inhibition of the sympathetic centers in the posterior hypothalamus
  • Direct effect of heat on the skin
  • Release of bradykinin from sweat glands

Sweating

Sweating is an active process. Its center is the preoptic nuclei in the anterior hypothalamus, and eccrine glands receive sympathetic cholinergic fibers (blocked by atropine).

Primary sweat

  • Secreted by the acini
  • Isotonic, like plasma

Secondary sweat

  • NaCl gradually reabsorbed by the ducts
  • Hypotonic
  • Depends on sweating rate and aldosterone level
  • Each 1 mL of evaporated sweat removes 0.6 kcal; sweating starts at an environmental temperature of 32°C.
  • Dribbling of sweat without evaporation gives no heat loss.
  • Cold sweat is emotional sweating, even with cold and vasoconstriction.

Acclimatization of sweating

Acute exposure

  • 700 mL/h sweat
  • 15-30 g NaCl lost per day

After 6 weeks

  • 2000 mL/h sweat
  • 3-5 g NaCl lost per day
  • Greater sweat-gland capacity and increased aldosterone

Fever (pyrexia)

Definition

Fever is hyperthermia caused by resetting of the hypothalamic set-point to a higher level.

  1. Pyrogen release

    Degenerated tissue gives exogenous pyrogens, which act on monocytes and macrophages to release interleukin and tumor necrosis factor: the endogenous pyrogens.

  2. Set-point reset

    IL-1 reaches hypothalamic thermosensitive neurons and, within ten minutes, causes formation of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which resets the set-point by synthesis of cAMP.

  3. Chills

    Body temperature is still below the new set-point, so the person feels cold: vasoconstriction with cold skin and shivering continue until the set-point is reached.

  4. Crisis (flush)

    When the cause is removed, the set-point returns to normal while body temperature is still higher, giving a sensation of hotness, vasodilatation (flushed skin) and intense sweating.

Control and treatment

  • PGE2 gives negative feedback on interleukin-1; increased IL-1 down-regulates its receptors; glucocorticoids such as cortisol decrease IL-1.
  • Aspirin decreases PGE2 synthesis from arachidonic acid, lowering the set-point and increasing heat loss by sweating.
High-yield

Aspirin does not lower the body temperature of a normal person, who has no interleukin-1. It also stimulates the heat-losing center.

Heat stroke and hyperthermia

Heat stroke

It follows exposure to hot humid weather or high fever. The sequence: raised temperature, excessive sweating, dehydration and salt loss, depression of the heat-regulating center, less sweating and dry hot skin, and further rise. At 43°C tissue protein denatures irreversibly, depressing the center further (a vicious circle) and leading to death.

  • Clinical picture: dizziness; fluid loss may lead to circulatory shock; tissue degeneration.
  • Treatment: immediate cooling by immersion in ice-cold water, sponging with alcohol, and antipyretics such as aspirin.
Clinical relevance

Sun stroke adds damage of brain tissue by direct sun rays to sweating and dehydration, with severe fever. It is treated by an ice bath and drinking saline.

Physiological changes in hyperthermia

CNS
Tremors and convulsions first; above 41°C loss of reflexes and coma
Heart rate
Up by 10 beats/min per 1°C rise
Blood pressure
Systolic up, diastolic down; in hot humid air, dehydration and hypotension (heat exhaustion)
Respiration
Rate increased by central and peripheral chemoreceptors

Hypothermia

Hypothermia is a drop of body temperature to a low level with slow metabolic and physiologic processes.

  • Body exposure: ice water for 20 minutes lowers temperature to 35°C and the heart stops; hypothalamic regulation is impaired, with sleepiness and even coma.
  • Frost-bite: freezing of ear lobes and digits may lead to gangrene.
  • Artificial hypothermia: strong sedatives depress the heat-regulating centers, or the patient is cooled with ice, to stop the heart for minutes in cardiac surgery.

Key terms

Check yourself

Which fibers supply eccrine sweat glands, and what blocks them?

Sympathetic cholinergic fibers; they are blocked by atropine.

Why does a person feel cold at the start of a fever?

The set-point has been raised, so body temperature is still below it, causing vasoconstriction and shivering.

Why does aspirin not lower the temperature of a normal person?

A normal person has no interleukin-1 driving PGE2 synthesis.

Key Points / Quick Review

System

  • Receptors, hypothalamic center (set-point about 37.1°C), effectors (skin, muscle, endocrine glands).
  • Above set-point: anterior hypothalamus; below: posterior hypothalamus.

Cold

  • Vasoconstriction, counter-current exchange, shivering (blocked by curare).
  • Adrenaline, thyroxine (slow), cortisol.

Heat

  • Vasodilatation and sweating; 1 mL evaporated sweat removes 0.6 kcal.
  • Acclimatization: 700 to 2000 mL/h, NaCl loss falls to 3-5 g/day.

Disorders

  • Fever: IL-1, PGE2, cAMP, set-point raised; aspirin lowers PGE2.
  • Heat stroke: protein denatures at 43°C; ice-water cooling.
  • Hypothermia: 35°C after 20 minutes in ice water.
