Definition, Indications and Contraindications

Source: pages 69 to 71
Definition

Cannulation is the insertion of a cannula (a thin tube) into a vein to provide intravenous access.

It is used to deliver medications, fluids and blood products, and to obtain blood samples.

Proper cannulation gives timely and effective treatment, particularly in emergency and intensive care settings. Mastery minimizes discomfort, reduces complications and enhances patient safety.

Indications

  • Fluid resuscitation: dehydration, shock or significant fluid loss.
  • Medication administration: drugs that need immediate effects or are irritating to the tissues.
  • Blood transfusion: patients with anemia or blood loss.
  • Diagnostic procedures: such as blood sampling for various tests.

Contraindications and precautions

  • Infection: avoid areas with signs of infection.
  • Thrombophlebitis: a contraindication, because of the risk of exacerbating inflammation.
  • Recent surgery: avoid sites near recent surgical procedures to prevent complications.
  • Poor vein condition: veins that are sclerosed or have a history of difficult access may be avoided.

Anatomy and Physiology of Veins

Source: pages 69 to 70

Knowing venous anatomy is fundamental for successful cannulation. Veins are divided into peripheral and central.

Veins used for cannulation
  • Peripheral veins
    • Median cubital elbow crease; often preferred (size, depth)
    • Cephalic lateral side; easy to palpate, harder to cannulate
    • Basilic medial side; deeper, less palpable
  • Central veins
    • Internal jugular neck; long-term access, large volumes
    • Subclavian beneath collarbone; stable access
    • Femoral groin; emergencies, other sites unavailable
  • The median cubital vein lies in the antecubital fossa (elbow crease).
  • The cephalic vein runs on the lateral side of the forearm and arm; the basilic vein runs on the medial side and needs a more skilled technique.
  • Central veins are used for central venous catheters, and the femoral vein is often used in emergencies.

Physiology of veins

  • Veins carry blood back to the heart and have thinner walls than arteries.
  • Valves prevent backflow of blood and can be a challenge if they lie in the path of the needle.
  • The vein wall has three layers (intima, media and adventitia), which influence its flexibility and responsiveness.

Preparation and Equipment

Source: pages 71 to 72

Patient preparation

  1. Informed consent: explain the procedure and obtain consent.
  2. Positioning: make the patient comfortable, with the limb or site properly supported.
  3. Hand hygiene: thorough hand washing or an alcohol-based hand sanitizer.

Equipment

  • Cannula: select the gauge, for example 18G for large volumes and 22G for smaller veins.
  • Tourniquet: engorges the veins and makes them more visible.
  • Antiseptic solution: commonly iodine-based or alcohol swabs.
  • Needle and syringe: for local anesthetic if needed.
  • Dressing and tape: to secure the cannula after insertion.

Sterility and infection control

  • Use sterile gloves, drapes and a sterile field to prevent infection.
  • Clean the site with antiseptic in a circular motion from the center outward to reduce the bacterial load.

Cannulation Techniques

Source: page 73

Peripheral cannulation

  1. Apply tourniquet

    Place it above the intended site to distend the vein.

  2. Palpate vein

    Identify a suitable vein by palpation or visualization.

  3. Clean site

    Use antiseptic solution to clean the area.

  4. Insert cannula

    Hold it at a 15-30 degree angle and insert with a steady motion.

  5. Advance cannula

    Once blood returns in the flashback chamber, advance the cannula further.

  6. Secure cannula

    Release the tourniquet and secure the cannula with a sterile dressing.

  7. Flush cannula

    Use normal saline to ensure patency and correct placement.

Central venous cannulation

  1. Preparation

    Use sterile drapes and gloves, and consider ultrasound guidance.

  2. Identify site

    Use anatomical landmarks or ultrasound images.

  3. Local anesthesia

    Administer local anesthetic if required.

  4. Needle insertion

    Insert with sterile technique and confirm placement with blood return.

  5. Guidewire and catheter

    Thread a guidewire through the needle, then advance the catheter over it.

  6. Secure and confirm

    Remove the guidewire, secure the catheter, and confirm placement with X-ray if needed.

Pediatric and special considerations

  • Pediatric: use smaller gauge cannulas and consider less traumatic sites such as the scalp or feet.
  • Elderly: may need special techniques because of fragile veins and skin.

Documentation and Follow-Up

Source: page 75

Documentation

  • Date and time: the exact time and date of cannulation.
  • Cannula details: gauge, type and site of insertion.
  • Patient response: any immediate reactions or complications.

Follow-up care and monitoring

  • Regular monitoring: check the site for signs of infection or complications.
  • Routine flushing: flush with normal saline to maintain patency and prevent clotting.
  • Replacement: change the cannula every 72-96 hours or as clinically indicated.

Why is the median cubital vein often the preferred site?

Because of its size and depth; it lies in the antecubital fossa.

What signals that the cannula can be advanced after insertion?

Blood returning in the flashback chamber.

How is air embolism managed?

Position the patient left lateral decubitus and seek immediate medical attention.

How often should the cannula be changed?

Every 72-96 hours or as clinically indicated.

Key Points / Quick Review

Basics

  • Cannulation: a cannula placed in a vein for IV access.
  • Indications: fluid resuscitation, medications, transfusion, diagnostic sampling.
  • Avoid infection, thrombophlebitis, recent surgery sites and poor veins.

Veins

  • Peripheral: median cubital (preferred), cephalic, basilic.
  • Central: internal jugular, subclavian, femoral (emergencies).
  • Valves may lie in the needle's path.

Technique

  • Sterile technique; clean from center outward.
  • Peripheral: tourniquet above site, 15-30 degrees, advance on flashback, secure, flush.
  • Central: guidewire, catheter over guidewire, X-ray if needed.

Complications and follow-up

  • Infection, phlebitis, hematoma, air embolism.
  • Document date, time, cannula details and patient response.
  • Flush routinely; change every 72-96 hours or as indicated.
